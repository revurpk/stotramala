How to Pronounce IAST

A guide for the complete beginner

IAST (the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration) writes Sanskrit in Roman letters with dots and bars added. Those marks are not decoration — each one changes the sound, and often the meaning. The good news is that the system is perfectly regular: one letter always makes one sound. Learn the marks once and you can read any Sanskrit text aloud.

Five things to know first

  1. c is the ch of church — never a k and never an s. So cetāṃsi begins like chetting, not ketting.
  2. A bar over a vowel (ā ī ū) means hold it about twice as long. That is all it means. It is not stress.
  3. An h after a consonant is a puff of breath, not a new sound. th is never the th of thin; ph is never the ph of phone.
  4. A dot under a letter (ṭ ḍ ṇ ṣ) means curl your tongue back to touch the roof of the mouth.
  5. Give every syllable its full value. English swallows unstressed syllables; Sanskrit does not.

why e and o have no bar

In Sanskrit these two vowels are long by nature — a short e or o does not exist. Marking them would be redundant, so IAST leaves them bare. Texts printed for South Indian readers often write ē and ō anyway, out of habit from Telugu and Kannada, which do distinguish short from long.

The consonant grid

The three that trip people up

c
the ch of church. ch is the same with an added puff.
ṅ
the ng of sing
ñ

Curled against teeth

English t and d sit between the two Sanskrit rows, so both will sound slightly off at first. For the teeth row (t th d dh n) press your tongue flat against the back of your upper teeth — softer than English. For the curled row (ṭ ṭh ḍ ḍh ṇ) curl the tip back and strike the roof of the mouth — this is the sound English speakers hear as “Indian-accented” t and d.

Semivowels and sibilants

y
the y in yes
r
a light tap of the tongue, as in Spanish pero — not the English bunched r
l
the l in let
v
between English v and w. After another consonant it leans toward w: svasthaiḥ ≈ swasthaiḥ.
ḷ
a curled l, tongue back at the roof of the mouth — as in mahākāḷī
ś
the sh of ship, tongue forward
ṣ
an sh with the tongue curled back — deeper and duller than ś
s
the plain s of sun
h
the h of hat, always voiced and audible
ś versus ṣ

Both are “sh” sounds and beginners may treat them alike without being badly misunderstood. The difference: ś is made with the tongue near the front, ṣ with it curled back. So śiva uses the forward one and kṛṣṇa the curled one.

The three special marks

ṃ
anusvāra — a nasal that takes its colour from whatever follows. Before k or g it is ng; before p or b it is m. At the end of a word, say it as m: tvaṃ ≈ tvam.
ḥ
visarga — a light breath at the end of a word that faintly echoes the vowel before it. namaḥ ≈ nama-ha, the echo much softer than the main vowel.
’
avagraha — marks a short a that has dropped out after e or o. In namo’stu, simply do not pronounce anything there: say namostu.

Reading the verses

The vertical bars

A single bar | (daṇḍa) closes the first half of a verse — pause as you would at a comma. A double bar || closes the verse, like a full stop. The numeral between two bars is the verse number, not something to be read aloud.

Doubled consonants

When a consonant is written twice, hold it — the delay is real and it carries the metre. prayacchati pauses on the cch, much as the k is held across black cat.

Spacing between words

Sanskrit verse is composed as a continuous stream of sound, and neighbouring words fuse at their edges by rules called sandhi. Editions like this one insert spaces to show where the individual words lie, which makes the grammar visible and the page far easier to read. When reciting, though, let the words run together across those spaces: the metre depends on it.

a note on tone

Sanskrit has no word stress in the English sense. Rather than leaning on one syllable of each word, keep an even pace and let the long vowels — and only the long vowels — take extra time. That steadiness is most of what makes recitation sound right.
