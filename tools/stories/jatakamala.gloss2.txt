4.1 | When there is something to forgive, there is forgiveness; where there is nothing, there is none; the good value even those who wrong them as a gain. So it is told: |
4.2 | The Bodhisattva was once a wild buffalo bull in a certain forest, going on foot, his rough body, from the mud he rolled in, like a break in a dark rain-cloud. Even in that animal birth, where a sense of right is hard to find and bewilderment is thick, because of his keen understanding his mind did not lose its zeal for the practice of dharma. |
4.3 | Compassion, bound to him by long habit, never left him; whether this was the power of his karma or of his own nature, so he was. |
4.4 | And so the Blessed One surely said that the working of karma is beyond thought: though of a compassionate nature, he took an animal birth, and in it a sense of dharma. |
4.5 | Without karma there is no chain of rebirths, and no good deed has an unwelcome fruit; though aware of dharma, he met with this or that remnant of karma, and so he came to be as he was. |
4.6 | Then a wicked monkey, seeing that his gentle nature had shown itself over time and that, through his compassion, he was free of anger and violence, thinking “there is nothing to fear from him,” tormented that great being again and again in one cruel way after another. |
4.7 | A wicked man, bold with the pride that grows in him, goes to the worst excess against those made gentle by kindness, for he sees no danger from them; but when he is touched by even the faintest fear of danger, he behaves humbly and low, his arrogance quelled. |
4.8 | Sometimes the monkey would drop suddenly on him while he lay trustfully asleep or nodding with drowsiness. Sometimes he would climb him like a tree and shake him hard. Sometimes, when the buffalo was hungry, he would stand blocking his path. Once he knocked his ears about with a stick. Sometimes, when the buffalo was eager to bathe, he would climb on his head and cover his eyes with both hands. He would even mount him with a raised stick and ride him by force, aping the sport of Yama. But the Bodhisattva, that great being, regarding all that insolent behaviour as if it were a service, bore it without any disturbance of anger or resentment. |
4.9 | It is in the very nature of the wicked to follow the road of unruliness; and the patience of the good, by habit, is like a kindness to them. |
4.10 | Then a certain yakṣa, who could not bear that insult, or wished to test the buffalo’s nature, stood in the way as the monkey rode that great being, the buffalo bull, and said: “Ho, are you hired by this wicked monkey? Or have you lost to him at dice? Or do you fear something from him? Or do you not know your own strength, that he rides you thus with contempt? Look: |
4.11 | “The diamond of your horn-tips, driven with force, could split the mightiest mountains as a thunderbolt does, and these hooves, loosed in rage, would sink into the mountain’s back as into mud. |
4.12 | “And this body of yours, firm and compact as a mountain, perfect in beauty, glowing with natural vigour at a glance, would be hard to approach even for a lion. |
4.13 | “Crush him; or tear out his pride with a razor or with the tip of your horn. Why do you bear the pain of this monkey’s tormenting like one without strength?” (The reading of the second half of this verse is uncertain.) |
4.14 | “Where is a bad man ever cured by the kindly courtesy of a gentle nature? As a disease of the phlegm spreads where only sharp, hot and dry things cure, so he spreads.” (This verse is obscure in the text; the rendering is tentative.) |
4.15 | Then the Bodhisattva, looking at the yakṣa, his mind inclined to patience, spoke with gentle words: |
4.16 | “I know he is fickle and always fond of misbehaving; that is exactly why it is right for me to bear with him. |
4.17 | “What patience is there in one who cannot retaliate against a stronger one? And what is there to forgive in the good, who are well trained in conduct? |
4.18 | “Only the strong can forbear the fault of the weak; so better to be humbled than for virtues to be defeated. |
4.19 | “When an unworthy deed is done by one of little strength, that is the best occasion to show virtue; what lover of virtue, seeing this, would exert himself to break his own steadiness? |
4.20 | “Patience always, to be sure; but a chance to be patient is hard to get, since it depends on another. When another provides it, what room is there for the course of anger? |
4.21 | “He who, heedless of the harm to his own dharma, has set about this as if to cleanse my sins — if I did not show forbearance here, how ungrateful a creature I should be!” |
4.22 | The yakṣa said: “Then you will never be free of his tormenting — |
4.23 | “for who is able to curb the unruliness of a wicked man who sets no store by virtues, so long as he does not give up…” (the second half of this verse is corrupt in the text, and the rendering is tentative.) |
4.24 | The Bodhisattva said: |
4.25 | “A pleasure got by giving pain to another, or the warding off of a trouble in that way, is not fit to be pursued by one who seeks happiness; for its result does not make for happiness. |
4.26 | “If, though thus awakened by me through my patience, he does not understand, then those who are not patient with him will turn him back from his wrong road, in whatever way he comes upon them. |
4.27 | “And having met with ill-treatment from such people, he will not do so to one like me; for one who has been found at fault does not act that way again; and so I shall be freed.” |
4.28 | Then the yakṣa, his mind drawn by joy, wonder and respect, praised the great being with “Well said, well said,” shaking his head and waving his fingers, and spoke to him with kind words: |
4.29 | “Where do animals get such steadiness, and such wide regard for virtues? You must be someone who, with some purpose, has taken this body and practises austerities in the penance-grove.” |
4.30 | So praising him, he shook the wicked monkey off his back, gave orders for his protection, and vanished on the spot. |
4.31 | So when there is something to forgive, there is forgiveness; where there is nothing, there is none; the good value even those who wrong them as a gain: this is to be said in a discourse on patience. And thus the skilfulness in judgement of the Bodhisattvas, even in animal births, has been seen. What man, or what one sworn to the religious life, who lacked it, would look well? This too is to be said — in praise of the Tathāgata, and when listening respectfully to the teaching. |
