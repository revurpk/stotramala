1.1 | Near a certain city a merchant’s son had begun to build a temple in the middle of a grove. The workmen, the carpenters and the rest, used to go into the city at midday to eat. Once a troop of monkeys, roaming this way and that, happened to come there. There stood a post of añjana wood that one of the carpenters had half split, with a khadira wedge driven into the middle. Meanwhile the monkeys began to play as they pleased on the treetops, the roofs, the peaks and the beams. One of them, whose death was near, out of restlessness sat on that half-split post, gripped the wedge with both hands and began to pull it out; and then, as the wedge slipped from its place and his testicles were caught in the middle of the post, what befell him has been told already (that is, he was crushed to death). |
2.1 | A jackal named Gomāyu, his throat withered with hunger, was wandering here and there in a forest when he saw a battlefield where two armies had fought. On it he heard the sound of a drum that had fallen there and was being struck by the tips of creeper-branches in the wind. His heart shaken, he thought, “Ah, I am lost! Before I come within sight of whatever makes this sound, I shall go off another way. And yet it is not right to do so in haste.” |
2.2 | When fear or joy has come, he who weighs it and does not act in haste does not come to grief. |
2.3 | “So let me find out whose sound this is.” Taking heart and thinking, he went very slowly, and then he saw the drum. Recognising it, he went close and, out of curiosity, struck it himself. Then he thought gladly, “Ah, after so long a great feast has fallen to us! Surely it is full of flesh, fat and blood.” So, covered as it was with rough hide, he somehow tore it open, made a hole in one place and, delighted, went inside. But the tearing of the hide broke his fangs. Then, bereft of hope, seeing only the wood that was left, he recited the verse that begins “I knew it before” (the verse itself is not given in this text). So one should not be afraid of mere noise. |
3.1 | In a certain place there was a lake full of many creatures of the water. A crane who lived there, grown old and unable to kill fish, sat on the shore with his throat dry from hunger and wept, wetting the ground with streams of tears like handfuls of pearls. A crab, with other water-creatures, came up to him, grieved at his grief, and said kindly: “Uncle, why are you not seeking your food today? You only stay here with your eyes full of tears, sighing.” |
3.2 | He said: “My child, you have seen rightly. Out of deep disgust at eating fish I have now resolved to starve myself to death, and so I do not eat even the fish that come close to me.” |
3.3 | Hearing this, the crab said: “Uncle, what is the cause of this disgust?” He said: “Child, I was born and brought up in this lake. And I have heard that a twelve-year drought is about to set in.” |
3.4 | The crab said: “From whom did you hear it?” The crane said: “From an astrologer. For Saturn, having pierced the wain of Rohiṇī, will go on with Mars and Venus. As Varāhamihira has said:” |
3.5 | If the son of the Sun (Saturn) splits the wain of Rohiṇī in the paths of the sky, then for twelve years Indra will not rain on the earth. |
3.6 | And likewise: |
3.7 | When the wain of Prajāpati (Rohiṇī) is split, the earth, as if she had done a sin, takes on a Kāpālika’s vow, strewn with ash and fragments of bone. |
3.8 | And again: |
3.9 | If Saturn, or Mars, or the Moon should split the wain of Rohiṇī — what shall I say? — the whole world is destroyed in an ocean of calamity. |
3.10 | (The text of this verse is corrupt; this is a tentative rendering.) When it stands in the midst of Rohiṇī’s wain, people take refuge in the moon and go off somewhere, eating food cooked from their own children and drinking cracked, sun-scorched water. |
3.11 | “So this lake has little water; it will soon dry up. When it is dry, all those with whom I grew up and always played will perish for lack of water. I cannot bear to see them parted from me; that is why I have undertaken this fast. Now the creatures of all the small ponds are being carried to great lakes by their own kin, and some, crocodiles, monitor lizards, river dolphins, water-elephants and such, go of themselves. But here in this lake the water-creatures are unconcerned, and so I weep the more, because not even the remnant of a seed of them will be saved.” |
3.12 | Hearing it, the crab told what he had said to the other water-creatures too. Then all of them, fish, tortoises and the rest, their minds struck with fear, came to the crane and asked: “Uncle, is there any means by which we may be saved?” |
3.13 | The crane said: “Not far from this pond there is a lake full of water, adorned with lotus-beds, which will not dry up even in twenty-four years of drought. If one of you climbs on my back, I will take him there.” |
3.14 | Trusting him, they crowded round on every side, crying “father!”, “uncle!”, “brother!”, each saying “I first, I first!” And the wicked-minded one took them one after another on his back, came to a rock not far from the pond, dashed them on it, ate them as he pleased, and came back to the pond; and, pleasing the water-creatures with false news and messages, he made his living daily in this way. |
3.15 | One day the crab said to him: “Uncle, our affection and talk began with me. Why then do you leave me and take others? Save my life today.” Hearing that, the wicked one thought: “I am sick of eating fish. Today I shall make this crab my relish.” So he put him on his back and set out for the slaughter-rock. |
3.16 | The crab, seeing from afar the mountain of bones by the rock and recognising fish-bones, asked him: “Uncle, how far is that pond? You are very tired from my weight. Tell me.” |
3.17 | Thinking, “This is a dull creature of the water and has no power on land,” he said with a smile: “Crab, what other pond? This is how I live. So remember your chosen deity; I shall throw you too on this rock and eat you.” |
3.18 | As he spoke, the crab seized him with both pincers by his soft neck, white as a lotus-stalk, and he died. Then the crab took the crane’s neck and slowly made his way to the pond. |
3.19 | Then all the water-creatures asked him: “Crab, why have you come back? Is all well? Uncle has not come either; why is he so long? We are all waiting, eager, for the moment.” |
3.20 | Thus addressed, the crab laughed and said: “Fools! That liar cheated all the water-creatures, threw them on a rock-slab not far off and ate them. But since my life was not yet over, I understood that traitor’s intent and have brought back this neck. So there is no cause for alarm; now all the water-creatures will be safe.” |
3.21 | So I say: “Having eaten many fish…” (the verse that this tale illustrates). |
4.1 | The crow said: “Good friend, tell me how that wicked snake will be killed.” |
4.2 | The jackal said: “Go to some city that is the seat of a king. There, take a golden chain or a necklace from some careless rich man, minister of the king or the like, and drop it into the snake’s hollow, so that the snake is killed on account of its being taken.” |
4.3 | At once the crow and his mate, hearing this, flew up of their own will. |
4.4 | And then the she-crow came to a lake and saw in the middle of it the women of some king’s household playing in the water, having laid by the water’s edge their gold chains, pearl necklaces, clothes and ornaments. The she-crow took a single gold chain and set off toward home. |
4.5 | Then the chamberlains and eunuchs, seeing it being carried off, followed quickly with sticks in their hands. The she-crow dropped the gold chain into the snake’s hollow and stayed far off. When the king’s men climbed the tree and looked into the hollow, there lay the black snake with its coils spread out. They killed it with blows of their clubs, took the gold chain and went where they wished. The pair of crows lived happily ever after. So I say: “By a clever means, what one…” (the verse this tale illustrates). |
5.1 | In a certain forest there lived a lion named Bhāsuraka. In the excess of his strength he never ceased killing many beasts every day, deer, hares and the rest. Then one day all the animals born in that forest — antelopes, boars, buffaloes, hares and others — met together, went to him and said: “Lord, what is the use of killing all the beasts every day? One animal alone is enough to satisfy you; so let us make a covenant with you. From today, while you stay sitting here, one animal in turn, by kind, will come to you each day to be eaten. If this is done, your living will be secured without trouble, and we shall not be wholly destroyed. So let this royal rule be kept.” |
5.2 | Then one day the hare’s turn came, in the order of the kinds. Driven by all the animals, though unwilling, he went very slowly, thinking how to kill the lion; having let the time go by, his heart troubled, as he went along the road he saw a well. Looking over it, he saw his own reflection in the water. Seeing it, he thought: “There is a way. I shall enrage Bhāsuraka and, by my own wit, make him fall into this well.” |
5.3 | At the end of the day he reached Bhāsuraka. The lion, his throat parched with hunger from the lateness, seized with anger and licking the corners of his mouth, was thinking: “Ah! In the morning I must empty the forest of living things for my meal.” While he was thinking so, the hare came slowly, bowed and stood before him. Then Bhāsuraka, blazing with rage, rebuked him: “You vile hare! First you are tiny, and then you have come late. For this offence I shall kill you and tomorrow destroy all the herds of beasts.” |
5.4 | Then the hare said humbly: “Lord, the fault is not mine, nor the other creatures’. Please hear the reason.” The lion said: “Tell it quickly, before you are between my teeth.” |
5.5 | The hare said: “Lord, today all the animals, knowing by the turn of the kinds that I was the smallest, sent me with five other hares. And as I came, in the middle of the way, another great lion came out of a hole and said to me, ‘Remember your chosen deity.’” |
5.6 | “Then I said: ‘We are going to our lord, the lion Bhāsuraka, to be his food, by covenant.’” |
5.7 | “He said: ‘If so, this forest is mine. By the covenant all the beasts must deal with me. That Bhāsuraka is a thief. Now if he is king here, leave four hares here as a pledge, call him, and come back quickly; and whichever of the two of us proves by valour to be king shall eat all these.’” |
5.8 | “Then, sent by him, I have come to my lord. This is the reason for the lateness. In this the lord is the judge.” |
5.9 | Hearing this, Bhāsuraka said: “Friend, if so, quickly show me that thief of a lion, so that I may pour out on him my anger at the beasts and be at ease. As it is said:” |
5.10 | Land, a friend and gold are the three fruits of war; if not one of these is to be had, one should by no means make war. |
5.11 | Where there would not be great gain and there would be defeat, a wise man should not start and wage war. |
5.12 | The hare said: “Lord, that is true. Kṣatriyas fight for their own land and over an insult. But he lives in a fort; when we came out, he stopped us. And an enemy in a fort is hard to subdue. As it is said:” |
5.13 | What a king can achieve in war with a single fort he cannot achieve with a thousand elephants or a hundred thousand horses. |
5.14 | A single archer standing on a rampart can hold off a hundred; therefore those skilled in the science of policy praise the fort. |
5.15 | Long ago, at the command of his teacher, and from fear of Hiraṇyakaśipu, Indra built a fort by the power of Viśvakarman. |
5.16 | And he also gave a boon: “The king who has a fort shall be victorious.” Thus forts came to be on earth by the thousand. |
5.17 | As a snake without fangs, as an elephant without rut, so a king without a fort becomes subject to all. |
5.18 | Hearing this, Bhāsuraka said: “Friend, show me that thief of a lion even in his fort, that I may kill him. As it is said:” |
5.19 | He who does not bring an enemy or a disease to rest as soon as it is born, even though he is very strong, grows because of it and is struck down by it. |
5.20 | And likewise: |
5.21 | One who wishes his good should not neglect a rising enemy; the wise hold an enemy and a disease alike, for both, if neglected, grow strong. |
5.22 | And again: |
5.23 | An enemy, though his strength is failing, if neglected through carelessness by men blind with pride, though curable at first becomes incurable, like a disease. |
5.24 | And likewise: |
5.25 | The man who goes forth having weighed his own strength and the spirit in his heart, though alone, slays many, as Bhārgava slew the Kṣatriyas. |
5.26 | The hare said: “That is so. Yet I have seen that he is strong. It is not right for my lord to go without knowing his might. As it is said:” |
5.27 | Eager, not knowing his own strength or the other’s, he who goes straight at a fire perishes like a moth. |
5.28 | Even a strong man who sets out in force to strike a foe that is mightier comes back shorn of pride, like an elephant with broken tusks. |
5.29 | Bhāsuraka said: “Sir, what business is this of yours? Show me him, even in his fort.” |
5.30 | Then the hare said: “If so, let my lord come.” Saying this, he went before him. |
5.31 | Then, as he went, he came to the very well he had seen and said to Bhāsuraka: “Lord, who can bear your might? Seeing you even from far off, the thief of a lion has gone into his fort. So come, that I may show him.” Bhāsuraka said: “Show me the fort.” Then the hare showed him the well. The foolish lion saw in the middle of the well his own reflection in the water and roared a lion’s roar; and from the well an echo came back twice as deep. |
5.32 | Thinking it his enemy, he threw himself on him and gave up his life. The hare, glad at heart, cheered all the animals, and, praised by them, lived happily in that forest. So I say: “He who has wit has strength.” |
6.1 | In a certain part of a forest there lived a jackal named Caṇḍarava. Once, hungry and greedy of tongue, he went into a city. The city dogs saw him, ran at him barking from every side and began to bite him with their sharp fangs. Being bitten, in fear for his life he ran into a nearby washerman’s house. In it a great vat full of indigo dye had been made ready. Attacked by the dogs, he fell into the middle of the vat. When he came out, he was blue. The other dogs, not recognising that jackal, went off in whatever direction they pleased. Caṇḍarava too reached a far-off place and set off toward the forest. But one who is blue never loses his colour. As it is said: |
6.2 | Diamond-cement, a fool, women, a crab and fish each hold on by a single grip, as do indigo and the drunkard’s wine. (The verse is tentative in the text.) |
6.3 | Then, seeing that strange creature, dark as Śiva’s poisoned throat or a tamāla tree, all the forest-dwellers — lions, tigers, leopards, wolves and the rest — their minds shaken with fear, began to run away on every side. They said: “We know nothing of this creature’s conduct or prowess. Let us go far away. As it is said:” |
6.4 | One whose conduct, family and valour one does not know, the wise man who wishes his own prosperity should not trust. |
6.5 | Caṇḍarava, seeing them stricken with fear, said: “Ho, beasts! Why do you run away in terror at the mere sight of me? You need not be afraid. Brahmā himself created me today and said to me: ‘Since the beasts have no king, I anoint you today lord of all the beasts, under the name Kakudruma. Go to the earth and protect them all.’ So I have come here. Therefore all the beasts must live in the shade of my umbrella. I am the king called Kakudruma, in the three worlds too.” |
6.6 | Hearing this, the beasts, with the lion and tiger at their head, surrounded him, saying, “Lord, master, command us.” Then he gave the lion the rank of minister, the tiger the office of bed-keeper, the leopard the charge of the betel, and the wolf the post of door-keeper. With the jackals, his own kin, he would not even speak; he had them all thrown out by the neck. So, while he was reigning, the lions and the rest would kill animals and lay them before him, and he, in the manner of a lord, divided them among all and gave them out. |
6.7 | As time passed, once, far off, he heard the clamour of a pack of jackals howling. Hearing the sound, his hair stood on end, his eyes filled with tears of joy, and he rose and began to howl in a high voice. Then the lion and the others, hearing that high cry, realised, “It is a jackal!”, and for shame stood a moment with their faces downcast, and said: “Ah! We have been made to carry this low jackal! Let him be killed.” |
6.8 | And he, hearing it, wished to flee, but there on the spot he was torn to pieces by the lions and the rest, and died. So I say: “He by whom his own people were abandoned…” (the verse this tale illustrates). |
