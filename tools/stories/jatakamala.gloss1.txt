1.1 | Even among creatures born as animals, good and great-souled ones have been seen to give according to their power. What man could fail to give? So it is told: |
1.2 | In a certain forest region, thick with lovely creepers, grass and trees, rich in flowers and fruit, its borders adorned by a river whose pure water was dark as lapis, its ground a pleasure to see and to touch with its carpet of soft young grass, a place where ascetics roamed, the Bodhisattva was born as a hare. |
1.3 | Through his goodness, the beauty of his body, his great strength and vigour, neither suspected nor feared by the small beasts, he roamed that forest as sportively as the lion, king of beasts. |
1.4 | Clothed in his own skin as in a hide, with his own fur for bark-garments, he shone there like a sage, content with shoots of grass. |
1.5 | By the pure kindness of his thought, speech and action, the beasts there, wicked as they were, became for the most part like pupils attending on him. |
1.6 | Those whose hearts were bound to him in a special way by the affection and reverence born of his surpassing virtues were three companions: an otter, a jackal and a monkey. They lived there happily together like kinsmen joined by mutual ties, or friends whose friendship had grown from mutual love and respect. Though turned away from the nature of animals — kind to living creatures, their greed quieted so that they forgot stealing, seeking fame in a way not at odds with duty, steady in self-restraint through keen understanding — their conduct was such as the good approve, and they became a wonder even to the gods. |
1.7 | Even a man shines when he takes the side of virtue, whether the course that suits pleasure hinders virtue or the course that suits virtue hinders pleasure; how much more one in the form of an animal. |
1.8 | Among them the one in the form of a hare was the accomplished one, weighty in his compassion for others; his own nature, rich in virtue, followed the way of the virtues, so that their fame went even to the world of the gods. |
1.9 | Then once, in the evening, that great being, respectfully attended by his friends who had come to hear the teaching, saw the moon of the fourteenth day of the bright fortnight risen, its disk nearly full, its beauty paled by its distance from the sun, like a silver mirror without a handle, a little turned on one side; and he said to his friends: |
1.10 | With its disk of almost full splendour, as if laughing, the moon announces to the good the festival of the fast-day. |
1.11 | “So it is clear that tomorrow is the fifteenth. Therefore you, keeping the observance of the fast-day, should honour whatever guest the time brings with a special food lawfully obtained, and only then sustain your lives. Consider, my friends: |
1.12 | “That by which unions end in parting, and high estates end in a ruinous fall, and life is as fickle as the zigzag of lightning — for that very reason one must be firmly heedful. |
1.13 | “So strive to increase merit by giving and by the ornament of virtue; for to a world wandering in the stronghold of rebirth, merit is the highest support. |
1.14 | “That by which the moon outshines the splendour of the host of stars with its bright beauty, and the thousand-rayed sun overpowers the lights and blazes — that is the height of the virtue of merit. |
1.15 | “Proud ministers and kings, all their pride humbled by the power of merit of the lords of the earth, bear the yoke of command as if gladly, like well-trained horses. |
1.16 | “Misfortune follows those who lack merit even while they stir about on the road of policy; and, driven off by those richer in merit, she goes after their opponents as if in resentment. |
1.17 | “So turn from the road of demerit, which rests on suffering and is bound to ill-fame, and set your minds on the glorious ways of merit, which bring about the rise of happiness.” |
1.18 | Saying “So be it,” they accepted his instruction, bowed to him, walked round him to the right, and went each to his own dwelling. When the friends had gone, that great one fell into thought. |
1.19 | These others are able to honour a guest who comes, with something or other; in this matter it is I alone who am to be pitied. |
1.20 | The tips of grass cut by my teeth, bitter, cannot in any way be given to a guest; shame on my powerlessness! |
1.21 | What use is my life, miserable in such weakness, if joy should turn to grief for a guest of mine like this? |
1.22 | “Where, then, now, in this failure of hospitality, could this worthless body, if given up, be of use to anyone?” While the great one was pondering this, he recalled something. “Why —” |
1.23 | This wealth, my body, fit to honour a guest, is mine alone, freely and easily to hand, and blameless. |
1.24 | “Why then do I grieve?” |
1.25 | I have found something to offer a guest. Heart, give up the dejection of sorrow; I shall honour the wish of whoever has come with this body of mine. |
1.26 | So resolving, that great being, as if he had gained the greatest of gains, stayed there with a mind full of joy. |
1.27 | When the surge of his resolve rose in his heart, the favour and the power of the dwellers in heaven appeared. |
1.28 | Then the earth, as if in joy, trembled with her mountains, the restless ocean her garment; the sound of the gods’ drums spread through the sky; the quarters shone, adorned with gladness. |
1.29 | The clouds on every side, rumbling softly, laughing with lightning, scattered flowers over him, with the pollen shaken loose in their mutual embraces. |
1.30 | The wind, moving steadily, bearing the fragrant pollen of many trees, seemed to honour him with thin cloths tossed up in joy, in unbroken devotion. |
1.31 | Learning of this wonder, born of the hare’s resolve and praised on every side by gods whose minds were glad and amazed, Śakra, lord of the gods, his mind filling with wonder and curiosity, wished to test the heart of that great being. On the second day, when the sun, keen in its rays, was crossing the middle of the sky, when the quarters, clothed in the web of its spreading rays and wrapped in the glare of its heat, could not bear to be looked at, when shadows were shrinking, the woodlands loud with the swelling cry of crickets, the flying of birds stopped, and travellers drained of their vigour by the heat, Śakra, lord of the gods, took the form of a brahmin and, as if lost on the road, his voice faint with hunger, thirst, weariness and despair, wailed aloud not far from them: |
1.32 | Alone, parted from my caravan, wandering in a deep forest, my body worn out by hunger and weariness — good people should save me. |
1.33 | Bewildered, unable to tell road from no road, going somewhere alone in this wilderness through confusion of direction, wearied by thirst and heat — who will cheer me with the words “Fear not”? |
1.34 | Then those great beings, their hearts shaken by that pitiful cry, hurried to the place in agitation. Seeing his piteous look, like that of a traveller lost on the road, they went to him, comforted him with courteous attentions and said: |
1.35 | Enough of your distress at being lost in the wilderness; you are with us as if with your own company. |
1.36 | So today, accepting our service, favour us, gentle sir, and tomorrow go where you wish. |
1.37 | Then the otter, taking his silence as consent to the invitation, hurried off in joyful haste, brought seven red carp, and said to him: |
1.38 | Fish left behind by the enemies of fish, forgotten, or leaping in fright onto the land, seven carp lying as if asleep from exhaustion — I have found them; eat them and stay here. |
1.39 | Then the jackal too, gathering whatever food he had found, offered it with a bow and respectfully said: |
1.40 | I found here a lizard and a pot of curds that someone had left; having eaten them out of kindness to me, may you, home of virtue, dwell in this forest. |
1.41 | So saying, with great joy of heart he offered it to him. |
1.42 | Then the monkey, taking mangoes made soft by ripeness, tawny as if dyed with powdered realgar, with bright red stalks, gathered in a bunch, said to him, with folded hands: |
1.43 | Ripe mangoes, lovely water, and shade cool with the joy of good company — since I have these, best of the knowers of Brahman, eat and stay here. |
1.44 | Then the hare came up and, when the courtesies were done, looking at him with respect, invited him with his own body: |
1.45 | A hare grown up in the forest has no beans, sesame or rice. Eat this body of mine, cooked in the fire, and live today in the ascetics’ forest. |
1.46 | What wealth one has that serves one’s wish, one uses at the festival of an asker’s coming; I have no wealth beyond my body; accept it, for it is all I have. |
1.47 | Śakra said: |
1.48 | How could one like me do harm even to any other creature? — and so what is there to say of one like you, who have shown such friendship? |
1.49 | The hare said: “This is fitting for a brahmin who has come near and deserves compassion. So please stay here, hoping for my favour, until I find some means of doing myself a kindness.” |
1.50 | Then Śakra, king of the gods, understanding his purpose, conjured up a heap of glowing coals the colour of molten gold, with thin flames flickering and showers of sparks, smokeless. The hare, looking all round, saw that mass of fire; seeing it he was glad and said to Śakra: “I have found this means of doing myself good. So by using my body make my hope of being of service fruitful. See, great brahmin: |
1.51 | “A heart eager to give, something to give, and the meeting with such a guest as you — these are not easily got together; through you let it not be in vain for me.” |
1.52 | So persuading him, that great being, out of respect and devotion and love of guests, bowed to him, and then — |
1.53 | Seeing that blazing fire like a treasure suddenly found by one in need of wealth, he leapt onto it with the greatest joy, as a lone swan leaps onto water with smiling lotuses. |
1.54 | Seeing this, the lord of the gods, his mind overcome with great wonder, took on his own form, honoured that great being with words, delightful to mind and ear, accompanied by a rain of divine flowers, and took him up in his own hands, which were rich with the beauty of lotus petals and adorned with shining rings on the fingers, and showed him to the gods: “Look, venerable gods who dwell in the abode of the Thirty, and rejoice in this most wonderful deed of this great being.” |
1.55 | Behold how fearlessly he has today given up his body, loving to guests! The faint-hearted cannot give up even a cast-off garland so steadily. |
1.56 | What a birth this is, and how opposed to it this generosity of heart and keenness of mind! This is a plain rebuke from the gods and from men to those who care little for merit. |
1.57 | Ah! how his mind is perfumed by the habit of virtue! Ah, how the love of good conduct has been shown by the generosity of his act! |
1.58 | Then Śakra, to make the greatness of that act known, looking to the good of the world, adorned with the mark of the hare’s form the disk of the moon, and also the pinnacles of the gabled halls of the finest palace, Vaijayanta, and of the assembly of the gods, Sudharmā. |
1.59 | Even today, when the moon is full, this image of the hare shines in the sky like a shadow in a mirror. |
1.60 | From that time the world calls the moon, which makes the lily-ponds smile and is the beauty-mark of the night, “Śaśāṅka” — hare-marked. |
1.61 | The otter, the jackal and the monkey too, passing from there, were reborn in the world of the gods, having found a good friend. |
1.62 | So even among those born as animals, great beings have been seen to give according to their power. |
1.63 | What man could fail to give? Even animals, too, are honoured by the good for their love of virtue; so one should have regard for virtues — this too is to be inferred. |
2.1 | The aims of the virtuous prosper for good here already, and much more hereafter; therefore one should strive for purity of conduct. So it is told: |
2.2 | The Bodhisattva was once the lord of the fish in a certain lake, not very large, its lovely water adorned with white lotuses, red lotuses and blue lilies, graced by pairs of swans, ducks and ruddy geese, strewn with the flowers of trees growing on its banks. Because in many other lives he had practised living for others, even there he busied himself with bringing happiness and welfare to others. |
2.3 | For deeds, good or bad, become natural to people by habit; such as they are, they are done without effort in another life, as in a dream. |
2.4 | The great being, whose heart was set on those fish as on his own dear children, did them the greatest kindness by the practices of giving, kind speech, doing good and the rest. |
2.5 | Restraining their liking for harming one another and increasing their affection for one another, by his skill and his means he made them forget the way of life of fish. |
2.6 | Looked after well by him, the multitude of fish prospered greatly, like a city freed from calamities under a king who acts by law. |
2.7 | Then once, through the lack of good fortune of the creatures and the carelessness of the sons of the gods who had charge of rain, the god did not rain properly. When the god rained badly, that lake was not filled as before with fresh water, white as the opened kadamba flowers. As the hot season came on, with the sun’s rays fiercer and yet, as it were, slow with weariness, with the earth heated by them, and the wind, as if eager for coolness, following the flames, the lake, drunk up day by day as if from thirst, became a puddle. |
2.8 | In the hot season the sun blazes; the wind is sharp, as if raining flame; the earth is hot as if in fever; as if in anger they dry up the waters. |
2.9 | Then the Bodhisattva, seeing the multitude of fish, already eyed by flocks of crows and the birds that range the water’s edge, given up to dejection and misery, with nothing left but flopping about, was moved to compassion and fell to thinking: “Alas, this is a terrible calamity that has fallen on the fish. |
2.10 | “Day by day the water dwindles, as if rivalling life itself; and even now the coming of the clouds is seen only after a long time. |
2.11 | “There is no way to move away, nor is there another to lead us; and our enemies, drawn by our misery, are coming. |
2.12 | “When this remnant of water is gone, these fish will surely be eaten, flopping, by their enemies before my eyes. |
2.13 | “What then is the thing to do now?” So pondering, that great being saw one refuge, a declaration of truth. His heart pressed by compassion, sighing long and hot and looking up at the sky, he said: |
2.14 | I do not remember ever having deliberately killed a living creature, even in the worst distress. By this truth, let the king of the gods rain and fill the lakes with water. |
2.15 | Then, through the accumulated virtue of that great being’s merit, the power of his declaration of truth, and the might of the gods, nāgas and yakṣas who were pleased by it, clouds appeared on every side, though out of season, as rain-clouds do: their forms reflected in the waters, their thunder deep and sweet, their tall dark peaks adorned with lightning-creepers, as if stretching out and embracing one another with their spreading peak-arms. |
2.16 | As if measuring the reaches of the quarters by their length, spreading darkness with their peaks, the black clouds shone, reflected in the sky as in a mirror, like the shadows of mountains. |
2.17 | Praised, as it were, by the glad peacocks with their ceaseless cries and many-coloured dance, those masses of cloud shone with their continuous low thunder, as if with deep laughter. |
2.18 | Streams of rain fell, as if pearls had been let loose; the dust settled; a scent spread freely over the earth, carried by the rain-wind. |
2.19 | Though swollen by the hot season, the power of the sun’s rays vanished; waters ran down toward the low ground, their girdles of foam tossing. |
2.20 | Again and again the lightning-creeper, colouring the horizons with golden-tawny rays, glad at the sound of the cloud-drums, moved as if dancing. |
2.21 | Then the Bodhisattva, as the lake filled with streams of water spreading pale from every side, at the very moment the rain fell, with the crows and the other birds fleeing and the multitude of fish glad, having regained hope of life, his heart flooded with joy and anxious lest the rain should stop, said again and again to the rain-god: |
2.22 | Thunder, Parjanya, deep and steady; drive away the joy of the crows; rain waters like jewels, bright with unceasing flashes of lightning. |
2.23 | Hearing it, Śakra, lord of the gods, his mind full of wonder, came in person, honouring him, and said: |
2.24 | This is the effect of your own surpassing truth, noble lord of fish; for these clouds with their sweet thunder pour like pitchers tipped over. |
2.25 | But it is a great slip, through carelessness on my part, that I did not attend to the work of those like you, whose minds are bent on the good of the world. |
2.26 | Do not worry from now on; I am the one to bear the burden of the good’s tasks. Through your virtues this place will not again fall under such distress. |
2.27 | So, pleasing him with kind words, he vanished on the spot. And that lake attained a great abundance of water. |
2.28 | So the aims of the virtuous prosper for good here already, and much more hereafter; therefore one should strive for purity of conduct. |
3.1 | Even fire cannot bear to overstep speech made firm by truth; therefore one should be devoted to truthful speech. So it is told: |
3.2 | The Bodhisattva was once a quail chick in a certain forest. A few nights out of the egg, his wings about to grow, so weak that the parts of his limbs could not be made out, he lived with his many brothers in a nest built by his parents’ labour, hidden in thick grass and sheltered by bushes and creepers. Even in that state, because his sense of right was undimmed, he would not eat the creatures his parents brought; he lived only on the grass-seeds, banyan-fruit and the like that his parents brought. Because of that meagre, dry food his body did not thrive, nor did his wings grow properly. But the other quail chicks, eating whatever was brought, grew strong and fledged. For this is the way of things: |
3.3 | He who is fearless of right and wrong and eats everything prospers happily; but one who seeks a righteous livelihood and eats only what he has picked out suffers here. |
3.4 | And the Blessed One has said, in two stanzas beginning “Easy is the life of the shameless”: |
3.5 | Easy is life for the shameless crow, of unclean ways, a snatcher, a bold one — but that life is full of defilement. |
3.6 | But for the modest, ever seeking purity, retiring, not bold, living by pure livelihood, life is hard. |
3.7 | These two stanzas are recited in the collection of the Āryasthavira school. While they were in this state, not far away a great forest fire appeared, its roar terrifying, columns of smoke swelling, its lines of flame spreading with flickering sparks, a terror to the forest-dwellers and a ruin to the thickets. |
3.8 | With its arms of flame tossed by the wind, many-coloured as in a special dance, it seemed to leap with its hair of tangled smoke, and it roared, taking away their courage. |
3.9 | The fire, rushing as if in a rage, with showers of sparks, burned the forest grasses that trembled in the buffeting of the fierce wind as if fleeing in terror. |
3.10 | The forest, its flocks of birds fled and bewildered in terror, its frightened animals wheeling about, sunk in masses of smoke, loud with the fierce crackle of fire, cried out, as it were, in anguish. |
3.11 | In due course the fire, as if pressed on by the strong wind, following the thick grass, came to the place near their nest. Then the quail chicks, their cries confused and broken with fear, regardless of one another, suddenly flew up. But the Bodhisattva, being very weak and not yet fledged, made no effort to fly up. That great being, knowing his own power, quite unperturbed, said courteously to the fire, which was drawing near as if in haste: |
3.12 | My name of bird and my feet are of no use: my wings have not grown, and in their alarm at you my parents too have flown away. I have nothing fit to offer you here as hospitality; so it is right, Fire, for you to turn back from us. |
3.13 | When he had spoken thus, by that great being, whose speech was fortified by truth — |
3.14 | Though driven by the wind, that fire, even in a dry thicket thick with grass, came to rest at once on reaching his words, as on reaching a river in full flood. |
3.15 | Even today, in that famous region of the Himalaya, the forest-fire, though its flames are raised by the wind, grows still, its flame faltering and shrinking, like a many-headed snake under a charm. |
3.16 | “What does this bring to mind?” It is said: |
3.17 | As the ocean with its surging waves for hoods does not cross its shore, as a lover of truth does not cross the rule laid down by the great sages, so even fire was unable to overstep the command of the truthful; therefore one should not abandon truth. |
3.18 | So even fire cannot bear to overstep speech made firm by truth; therefore one should be devoted to truthful speech. This is to be said also in praise of the Tathāgata. |
