7.1 | On the shore of the sea there stood a great rose-apple tree, always in fruit. A monkey named Raktamukha (Red-face) lived there. One day a crocodile named Karālamukha came out of the sea-water and settled on the edge of the shore beneath that tree, on the soft sand. Then Raktamukha said to him: “Sir, you have come to me as a guest. So eat these rose-apples that I give you, sweet as nectar.” For it is said: |
7.2 | Be he dear or hated, a fool or a learned man, one who has come at the end of the household offering is a guest, a bridge to heaven. |
7.3 | One should not ask a guest his conduct, his clan, his learning or his family, when he comes at the end of the offering or at the ancestral rite; so Manu has said. |
7.4 | One who honours a guest who comes at the end of the offering, tired by the labour of a long road, goes to the highest state. |
7.5 | The man from whose house a guest goes away unhonoured, sighing — his ancestors go away from him, turning their faces aside, together with the gods. |
7.6 | So saying, he gave him the rose-apples. The crocodile ate them, enjoyed a long and pleasant talk with him, and went back to his own home. So every day the monkey and the crocodile, sitting in the shade of the rose-apple tree, passed the time in pleasant converse on many a branch of learning. The crocodile used to carry the leftover rose-apples home and give them to his wife. Then one day she asked him: “My lord, where do you get such nectar-like fruit?” |
7.7 | He said: “Dear wife, I have a very dear friend, a monkey named Raktamukha. He gives me these fruits out of affection.” |
7.8 | Then she said: “One who always eats such nectar-like fruits must have a heart full of nectar. So, if you want me for your wife, give me his heart, that by eating it I may enjoy pleasures with you, free of old age and death.” |
7.9 | He said: “Dear, do not talk so. He has become our brother by compact, and moreover he gives us fruit. So he cannot be killed. Give up this false notion. For it is said:” |
7.10 | A mother bears one birth; the word bears a second. They have said that what is born of the word is more than a brother or a kinsman. |
7.11 | Then the crocodile’s wife said: “You have never before gone against my word. So surely she is a she-monkey, since from love for her you spend the whole day there. I have understood you well now. For:” |
7.12 | You do not answer me gladly, nor give me anything I wish; you sigh deeply at night like a blaze of fire; when you embrace me you are slack and do not kiss me with love; so, deceiver, there must be a beloved in your heart, another woman like me. |
7.13 | Then he, falling at his wife’s feet and taking her on his lap, spoke very humbly to her, in the extremity of her anger: |
7.14 | When I have fallen at your feet and become your servant, why, beloved, do you fly into a rage? |
7.15 | Hearing his words, she answered with her face bathed in tears: |
7.16 | Along with your hundred longings, deceiver, your beloved — she alone remains in your mind, lovely in her false ways; there is no room here for us; so enough of these mockeries of falling at my feet. |
7.17 | “And besides, if she is not your beloved, then why, though I have told you, do you not kill him? And if he is a monkey, what friendship have you with him? What more is there to say? If I do not eat his heart, know that I have resolved to fast to death.” |
7.18 | Knowing her resolve, his heart troubled with anxiety, he said: “Or rather, this is well said:” |
7.19 | Diamond-cement, a fool, women, a crab and fish each hold on by a single grip, as do indigo and the drunkard’s wine. |
7.20 | “What shall I do? How can he be killed by me?” Thinking so, he went to the monkey. The monkey, seeing him come after so long, and troubled, said: “Friend, why have you come so late today? Why do you not speak gladly? Why do you not recite fine verses?” |
7.21 | He said: “Friend, your brother’s wife spoke to me with very harsh words: ‘Ungrateful wretch! Do not show me your face, since you live every day on a friend, and do not repay him even by so much as showing him your house. For you there is no atonement.’ For it is said:” |
7.22 | For one who kills a brahmin, a drinker of spirits, a thief, one who breaks his vow, a rogue, the good have laid down atonement; for the ungrateful there is no atonement. |
7.23 | “‘So bring your brother-in-law home today, to repay him; if you do not, we shall not see each other in the other world.’ So, told this by her, I have come to you. Today, quarrelling with her on your account, I have been delayed this long. So come to my house. Your brother’s wife stands waiting in eagerness, with the floor decorated, wearing garments, jewels and fitting ornaments, with a garland of welcome hung at the door.” The monkey said: “Friend, my brother’s wife has spoken rightly. For it is said:” |
7.24 | A very wise man should shun a friend of the weaver’s kind, who is always greedy to draw things toward himself. |
7.25 | And likewise: |
7.26 | To give, to take, to tell a secret, to ask, to eat and to feed — these six are the marks of friendship. |
7.27 | “But we live in the forest, and your house is at the water’s edge. How can I go there? So bring my brother’s wife here instead, that I may bow to her and receive her blessing.” |
7.28 | He said: “Friend, my house is in the middle of the sea, on a lovely sandy bank. So climb on my back and go in comfort, without fear.” Hearing this, he said gladly: “Good; if so, why delay? Let us hurry. Here I am, mounted on your back.” |
7.29 | When it was done, and the crocodile was going through the deep sea, the monkey, seeing it, afraid, said: “Brother, go slowly, slowly. My body is being washed over by the waves.” |
7.30 | Hearing that, the crocodile thought: “Now that he has come into deep water, he is in my power. Sitting on my back he cannot stir by a sesame-seed’s breadth. So I will tell him my intention, that he may remember his chosen god.” And he said: “Friend, I have brought you to be killed, having been persuaded by my wife’s words. So remember your chosen god.” |
7.31 | He said: “Brother, what wrong have I done to her or to you, that you plan my death?” The crocodile said: “Your heart, which has tasted the nectar-like juice of the fruit, has stirred her to a craving to eat it. That is why this was done.” |
7.32 | The monkey, quick of wit, said: “Good friend, if so, why did you not tell me this there? I always keep my heart carefully hidden in the hollow of the rose-apple tree, so that I could have given it to my brother’s wife. Why have you brought me here with my heart left behind?” |
7.33 | Hearing that, the crocodile said gladly: “Good friend, if so, give me your heart, that that wicked wife may eat it and rise from her fast. I shall take you back to that same rose-apple tree.” Saying so, he turned back and went to the foot of the rose-apple tree. And the monkey, somehow, with prayers and the worship of many gods, reached the shore. Then, with long leaps, he climbed that very rose-apple tree and thought: “Ah, I have got my life back!” Or rather, this is well said: |
7.34 | One should not trust one who is not trusted, nor trust too far even one who is trusted; danger born of trust cuts off even the roots. |
7.35 | “This is like a second birthday for me.” While he was thinking so, the crocodile said: “Friend, give me that heart, that your brother’s wife may eat it and rise from her fast.” |
7.36 | Then the monkey laughed and rebuked him: “Shame, shame, fool, betrayer of trust! Does anyone have two hearts? So go quickly from below the rose-apple tree, and do not come here again. For it is said:” |
7.37 | He who wishes to make a friend again who has once done him wrong takes death upon himself, as a mule conceives. |
7.38 | Hearing this, the crocodile thought, ashamed: “Ah, how foolish I was to reveal my own thoughts to him! If somehow he trusts me again, I will win his trust once more.” And he said: “Friend, I only said that in jest to learn your mind. She has no need of your heart. So come to my house as an honoured guest.” |
7.39 | The monkey said: “Be gone, wicked one. I shall not come now. For it is said:” |
7.40 | What sin will a hungry man not do? Starving folk are pitiless. Tell me, good lady, of Priyadarśana: Gaṅgadatta does not come back to the well. (The verse leads into the next tale in the book, the frog Gaṅgadatta, which is not given here.) |
8.1 | In a certain place lived a brahmin named Devaśarman. His wife gave birth to a son. On that same day a female mongoose gave birth to a young mongoose and died. Then the brahmin’s wife, loving her son, nursed that mongoose too as if he were a child, with the giving of milk, rubbing with oil and so on; but she did not trust him. Since love of one’s child exceeds all other love, she was always afraid that some day he might, through the fault of his kind, do harm to the boy. For it is said: |
8.2 | Even a bad son can be the joy of a man’s heart, though ill-behaved, ugly, foolish, vicious and wicked. |
8.3 | People say that sandal is cool; but the touch of a son’s body surpasses sandal. |
8.4 | People do not value the friendship of a father who does them good, or even of their protector, as they do the claim of a son. |
8.5 | Then one day, having laid her son on the bed, taking a water-jar, she said to her husband: “Brahmin, I am going to the pond for water. You must guard this boy from the mongoose.” |
8.6 | When she had gone, the brahmin too left the empty house and went somewhere to beg. Meanwhile, by the will of fate, a black snake came out of its hole. The mongoose, knowing it a natural enemy, fought the snake to protect his brother and cut it to pieces. Then, with his face covered in blood, glad, he went toward the mother to show her what he had done. The mother, seeing his face wet with blood, thought, “Surely this wretch has eaten my child,” and in anger threw the water-jar at him. |
8.7 | So she killed the mongoose, and as she came into the house wailing, there her son lay, still asleep. Seeing the black snake near by, cut in pieces, she began to beat her head and breast with grief at the loss, as she thought, of her son. |
8.8 | Just then the brahmin came back, his hunger appeased, and saw her, tormented by grief for her son, lamenting: “Ho, greedy man! Overcome with greed you did not do as I said. So now taste the fruit of the tree of sorrow, your son’s death.” Or rather, this is well said: |
8.9 | One should not be overgreedy; one should be greedy in due measure. By the fault of overgreed the jackal came to his death. (The verse looks back to a tale told elsewhere in the book.) |
9.1 | In a certain place lived four brahmin boys in the closest friendship. Three of them had mastered the sciences but had no sense; the fourth had sense but was turned away from learning. Once the friends took counsel: “What is the use of learning, if one does not go to another land, please kings and earn wealth? Let us go to the eastern country.” |
9.2 | When they had done so and gone some way, the eldest of them said: “Ah! One of us, the fourth, is a fool; he has only sense. And the gift of a king is not won by sense without learning. So we shall not give him a share of what we earn. Let him go home.” |
9.3 | Then the second said: “Sensible one, go home, for you have no learning.” |
9.4 | Then the third said: “Ah, it is not right to do so. We have played together since childhood. So let this noble one come too; he will share equally in what we earn.” For it is said: |
9.5 | What is the use of wealth that is like a bride, for one alone, and not common like a courtesan, enjoyed by travellers? |
9.6 | And likewise: |
9.7 | “This is mine, that is a stranger’s” — such counting is for small minds; for those of noble nature the whole earth is a family. |
9.8 | “So let him come too.” |
9.9 | When it was done, as they went along they saw in a forest the bones of a dead lion. Then one said: “Ah, today let us test our learning. Here lies a dead creature. By the power of learning let us make it live. I shall gather the bones.” |
9.10 | So, in his eagerness, he assembled the bones. The second joined to them skin, flesh and blood. The third was just about to put life into it when Sensible stopped him: “Wait, sir. This is a lion that is being made. If you bring it to life, it will kill us all.” |
9.11 | So told, he said: “Shame on you, fool! I will not make my learning fruitless.” Then Sensible said: “In that case wait a moment, while I climb a tree.” |
9.12 | When this was done and the lion had been given life, it rose up and killed all three. And Sensible came down from the tree and went home. So I say: “Better sense than learning.” |
