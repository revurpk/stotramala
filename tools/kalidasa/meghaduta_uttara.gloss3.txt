29 | Wasted by grief, lying on one side on the bed of separation, like the thin sliver of the moon left at the foot of the eastern sky — she who passed the night with me in a moment, in the pleasures of love, now spends it, made long by parting, in hot tears. |
30 | She covers with lashes heavy with water the eye which, from old affection, went toward the moon’s rays, cool as nectar, coming through the lattice, and then turned away again; she is neither awake nor asleep, like a land-lotus on a cloudy day. |
31 | With a sigh that torments her lip like a shoot she thrusts back her hair, rough from a bath without oil, hanging down to her cheek; she longs for sleep, thinking “may union with me come, even in a dream,” but tears pressing from her eyes leave it no room. |
32 | The single braid she bound on the first day of our parting, putting aside the garland, which I am to loosen when the curse is over and the sorrow gone — she pushes it from her cheek again and again with a hand whose nails are untrimmed and hurt at its touch, the braid hard and tangled. |
33 | That frail woman, having laid aside her ornaments, her delicate body set down on the bed with pain upon pain, will surely make even you let fall tears of fresh water; for almost everyone whose heart is moist is moved to compassion. |
34 | I know that your friend’s heart is full of love for me; so I picture her thus in this, our first parting. It is not conceit of being fortunate that makes me talk so much; what I have said, brother, you will see before your eyes before long. |
35 | I fear that the eye of the doe-eyed one, its sideways reach blocked by her hair, deprived of the oil of collyrium, its play of brows forgotten since she gave up wine, will, when you draw near, twitch upward and match the beauty of a blue lotus stirred by a leaping fish. |
36 | Her left thigh, pale as the trunk of a fresh plantain, once marked by my nails, and now by the turn of fate deprived of the pearl-girdle it wore so long, which used to know the rubbing of my hand at the end of love, will tremble. |
37 | At that time, cloud, if she has found the comfort of sleep, wait beside her and bear to hold back your thunder for one watch of the night; let her not lose at once, when she has somehow got me in a dream, the embrace in which the creepers of my arms were tight around her neck. |
38 | Having woken her with a breeze cool from your drops, and refreshed her along with the fresh jasmine buds, then, with lightning held in your womb, and her eyes steady at the window you now share, begin boldly to speak to the proud woman in words of thunder. |
39 | “Know me, O woman whose husband lives, as the friend of your husband, a cloud, come near you with his message laid in my heart — I who with deep and soft sounds hurry the troops of weary travellers, eager to loosen their wives’ braids.” |
40 | Told so, she will look up at you, as Maithilī at the wind’s son, her heart swelling with longing, and she will see you and welcome you, and listen most attentively; for the news of her beloved brought by a friend, gentle one, is for women but a little less than meeting him. |
41 | Then, long-lived one, to do her a kindness, say to her, both in my words and your own: “Your companion, who lives in the hermitage of Rāmagiri, is unharmed and, parted from you, asks after your welfare.” This is the first thing to say to creatures whose troubles come easily. |
42 | “Far away, his road barred by hostile fate, he comes to you in fancy: his thin body to your thinner limbs, burning deep to you burning, tearful to your tears, longing without pause to your longing, hot sigh to your sigh, which is heavier still.” |
43 | “What could be spoken in words, which he, eager, in front of your friends would try to say into your ear for greed of touching your face — he has gone beyond hearing and is hidden from your eyes, and so he speaks to you by my mouth, in words made by longing.” |
