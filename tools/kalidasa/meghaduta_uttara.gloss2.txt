15 | There, north of the lord of wealth’s palace, is my house, easily seen from afar by its lovely arched gateway like Indra’s bow; beside it stands a young mandāra tree that my wife reared as a son, bent down by clusters of flowers within reach of the hand. |
16 | In the house is a pond with steps of emerald slabs, covered with golden lotuses in bloom on glossy lapis stems; the swans that live in its water, free from sorrow, will not even long for nearby Mānasa when they see you. |
17 | On its bank is a pleasure-hill, its peak made of soft sapphires, lovely to see with its ring of golden plantains; it is dear to my wife, friend, and with an anxious heart I remember it when I see you with lightning flickering at your edge. |
18 | There a red aśoka with trembling shoots and a lovely kesara tree stand close to the arbour of jasmine fenced with kurubaka; the one, like your friend, longs with me for the touch of her left foot; the other, under the pretext of a pregnancy-craving, wants the wine of her mouth. |
19 | Between them stands a golden perch with a crystal slab, its base set with gems bright as young bamboo; there at the day’s end sits your friend the blue-throated peacock, whom my wife makes dance to the claps of hands whose bracelets tinkle sweetly. |
20 | By these marks, laid up in your heart, good one, you will know it; and also by seeing at the door the conch and the lotus painted there. Without me the house now surely has a faded look; at sunset the lotus does not keep its beauty. |
21 | Becoming at once the size of an elephant calf, to make a swift descent, settle on the lovely slope of the pleasure-hill I first spoke of; you can make your lightning, fallen inside the house, give a faint light, like the glimmer of a row of fireflies. |
22 | Slender, dark, with fine pointed teeth and lips red as ripe bimba fruit, thin in the waist, with the glance of a startled doe, deep in the navel, slow of gait from the weight of her hips, a little bowed by her breasts — she who is there is the Creator’s first work among women. |
23 | Know her, sparing of words, as my second life, like a lone cakravāka hen when her mate is far away; I think that, as these heavy days pass, the young wife, deep in longing, has been changed, like a lotus-plant struck by frost. |
24 | Surely my beloved has eyes swollen from violent weeping, her lower lip discoloured by the heat of her sighs, her face, half-hidden by her long hanging hair, resting in her hand; she wears the dejection of the moon, its brightness dimmed as you follow it. |
25 | When you come into her sight she will be busy either with the offering, or drawing my likeness, thin with parting, as she imagines me; or asking the sweet-voiced mynah in its cage, “Do you remember your master, clever one? You were his favourite.” |
26 | Or, gentle one, she will have laid the lute on her soiled skirt and want to sing a song with my name worked into its words; having somehow wiped the strings, wet with her tears, she forgets again and again the tune she herself composed. |
27 | Or she lays flowers on the threshold, counting on the ground the months that remain of the term set at the start of our parting; or she tastes in her heart the union she longs for. These are mostly the pastimes of women when their husbands are away. |
28 | In the daytime, being busy, she will not be so tormented by separation; I fear for your friend at night, heavier in grief and without distraction. Standing at the lattice of the palace, look at that faithful one sleepless on her bed on the ground at midnight; my message can bring her comfort. |
