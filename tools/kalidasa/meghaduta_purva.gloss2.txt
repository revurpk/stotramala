13 | First hear the road, fitted to your journey, as I tell it; then, cloud, you will hear my message, a drink for the ear. Wherever you go, weary, set your foot on the mountain-tops to rest, and, when you are spent, drink the light water of the streams. |
14 | “Is the wind carrying off the mountain’s peak?” — so, with upturned faces, the simple Siddha women will gaze at you with startled looks, glad of your vigour. Rise from this place of damp cane-thickets and fly north, avoiding on your way the rough trunk-blows of the elephants of the quarters. |
15 | Ahead of you this fragment of Indra’s bow shows itself above an anthill, like a mingling of jewel-lights; through it your dark body will gain great splendour, like Viṣṇu in a herdsman’s dress with a gleaming peacock feather. |
16 | “The fruit of the ploughing depends on you” — so think the country-women, who know nothing of the play of brows, and drink you in with eyes soft with affection. Climb the upland field, fragrant with fresh ploughing, go a little westward, and then quickly north again. |
17 | Mount Āmrakūṭa, whose forest-fires you have quenched with your showers, will bear you, weary from the road, gladly on his head. Even a mean man, remembering an earlier kindness, does not turn his face from a friend who comes for shelter; how much less one so lofty. |
18 | When you climb his peak, glossy like a braid of hair, with its slopes thick with wild mangoes gleaming ripe, the mountain, dark in the middle and pale all around, will surely look like the breast of the earth — a sight for divine couples. |
19 | Rest a moment in that grove, enjoyed by forest-women, and then, going faster for having shed your water, pass on along the road. You will see the Revā, scattered over the rocky foot of the Vindhya, like painted streaks of ornament on an elephant’s body. |
19a | Weary from the road, Āmrakūṭa, when you come to meet him, will praise you with his lofty head, O cloud. You in turn will quench his summer fire with a downpour; a kindness to the great, moist with goodwill, bears fruit before long. |
20 | Take up her water, scented with the bitter rut-juice of wild elephants, and go on, your rain spent and your speed checked by the rose-apple thickets. The wind will not be able to lift you, cloud, with that water inside you; emptiness makes everything light, fullness gives weight. |
21 | Seeing the kadamba, green and tawny with half-grown filaments, and the plantains opening their first buds along the bank, grazing in the forests and breathing the strong scent of the earth, the deer will point out your path as you let fall your drops. |
22 | When you arrive, at the hour of thunder, the Siddhas will honour you, watching the cātakas skilled at catching drops of water and the cranes forming lines as if counting; their dear companions, startled, will cling to them, trembling. |
23 | Friend, I foresee that, though you are eager to go quickly for my beloved’s sake, you will lose time on the mountain fragrant with kakubha trees. The peacocks will welcome you with their cries, their eyes wet, the corners white; and somehow, though they come out to meet you, you will make up your mind to go quickly on. |
24 | When you draw near, the Daśārṇa country will change: the garden hedges turn pale with ketaka buds pricked open by their spikes, the village shrines busy with crows building their nests, the edges of the rose-apple groves dark with ripe fruit, and the swans staying but a few days. |
25 | Going to their capital, known in all directions as Vidiśā, you will at once gain the full fruit of being a lover: you will drink the sweet water of the Vetravatī, which murmurs charmingly at its banks, its waves rippling, like a face with knitted brows. |
