1 | A certain yakṣa, careless in his duty, had his power taken from him by his master’s curse — a year’s exile, made heavy by parting from his beloved — and made his dwelling in the hermitages of Rāmagiri, whose waters are hallowed by Sītā’s bathing and whose trees give deep, cool shade. |
2 | Parted from his wife, the lovesick yakṣa spent some months on that mountain, his forearm bare where the gold bracelet had slipped. On the first day of Āṣāḍha he saw a cloud clinging to the peak, lovely as an elephant playing at butting a bank. |
3 | Standing before it, the servant of the king of kings, somehow holding back his tears, brooded long to rouse his own eagerness. At the sight of a cloud even a happy man’s mind is unsettled; how much more one whose beloved is far away and who longs to hold her. |
4 | With the month of rains near, wanting to keep his beloved alive and to send news of his well-being by the cloud, he offered it fresh kuṭaja flowers as a welcome-gift, and gladly greeted it with the loving words of a host. |
5 | A cloud — a mere meeting of smoke, light, water and wind — and messages that only creatures of keen faculties can carry: how far apart they are! Not weighing this, in his eagerness the yakṣa begged it; for the love-stricken are by nature pitiable, and cannot tell the living from the lifeless. |
6 | I know that you were born in the world-famous line of the Puṣkara and Āvartaka clouds, and that you are Indra’s own minister, taking whatever shape you wish. It is by fate, far from my kin, that I make this request of you: better a plea refused by the noble than one granted by the base. |
7 | You are the refuge of the scorched, O cloud; carry my message to my beloved, from me, whom the wrath of the lord of wealth has parted from her. You must go to Alakā, the city of the yakṣa lords, whose palaces are washed by the moonlight from the head of Śiva in its outer garden. |
8 | When you have risen onto the path of the wind, travelling women, their hair lifted from their brows, will look up at you and take comfort in hope. When you are gathered, who would neglect a wife who pines in separation — unless, like me, he were another’s servant? |
9 | And you will surely see my wife, your brother’s wife, faithful and still alive, spending her days counting the days till my return. For the bond of hope, like a flower-stalk, holds up the loving hearts of women, which would otherwise at once fall in parting. |
10 | The favourable wind drives you gently on, and this cātaka, your kinsman, sings sweetly on your left; and cranes, who know the moment of conception, will tie themselves in garlands and wait upon you in the sky, a delight to the eye. |
11 | And, hearing your thunder, sweet to the ear, which can make the earth fruitful with mushrooms, the royal swans, longing for Lake Mānasa, with lotus-shoot fibres for their journey-food, will be your companions in the sky as far as Kailāsa. |
12 | Take leave of this dear friend, the lofty mountain, embracing him — his slopes are marked with the footprints of Raghu’s lord, which men revere — who, every time you meet him, shows his love by shedding hot tears born of the long parting. |
