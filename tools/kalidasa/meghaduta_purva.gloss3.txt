26 | There, to rest, settle on the hill called Nīcaiḥ, which seems to thrill at your touch with its full-grown kadamba flowers; from its stone chambers breathes the perfume of the pleasures of courtesans, proclaiming the unbridled youth of the townsmen. |
27 | Rested, go on, sprinkling with fresh drops the jasmine buds in the gardens along the forest rivers, and, by giving shade for a moment to the faces of the flower-pickers, whose ear-lotuses droop as they wipe the sweat from their cheeks, make a passing acquaintance. |
28 | Though the road is crooked for one setting out to the north, do not be averse to the lap of Ujjayinī’s mansions; if you do not delight in the restless side-glances of the city women, startled by the flash of your lightning-garlands, you are cheated of your due. |
29 | On the way, alight on the Nirvindhyā, whose girdle is the line of birds crying at the stirring of her waves, who glides on with a charming stumble and shows the whirlpool of her navel; and taste her within: for a woman’s first word of love to her beloved is coquetry. |
30 | The river Sindhu, her thin water drawn into a braid, pale with the old leaves dropped by the trees on her banks, shows through her state of separation how fortunate you are, fortunate one; you must see to it that she loses her thinness. |
31 | Reaching Avanti, whose village elders are skilled in the tales of Udayana, approach Viśālā, the city I spoke of before, rich in splendour: it is like one shining piece of heaven, brought down by the heaven-dwellers when the fruit of their good deeds had dwindled, with what was left of their merit. |
32 | There the wind from the Śiprā, drawing out the clear, sweet, intoxicated cries of the cranes at dawn, rich with the scent of opening lotuses, kindly to the limbs, takes away women’s weariness after love, like a dear lover coaxing with entreaties. |
33 | There, seeing in the bazaars necklaces, bright pearls by the million, conches and shells, emerald gems green as young grass with sprouting rays, and heaps of coral, one can tell that the oceans are left with nothing but water. |
34 | Here the king of the Vatsas carried off Pradyota’s dear daughter; here was that same king’s golden palm-grove; here Nalagiri, they say, broke his post in pride and ran wild — so the people, who know their kin, entertain visiting relatives with tales. |
35 | Your body fed by the hair-perfuming incense that streams out of the windows, and offered a dance by the house-peacocks in their affection for you, pass your journey’s weariness in her mansions, fragrant with flowers, looking at their beauty, marked with the red dye of the lovely women’s footprints. |
36 | Looked on with reverence by Śiva’s attendants as “the colour of the Lord’s throat,” go to the holy abode of Caṇḍīśvara, teacher of the three worlds, where the garden is shaken by the winds from the Gandhavatī, fragrant with lotus pollen and bitter with the bathing-perfume of young women playing in the water. |
37 | Even if you arrive at Mahākāla at another hour, cloud, you should wait until the sun has gone out of sight; by serving as the drum at the twilight worship of the Trident-bearer you will win the full fruit of your deep thunder. |
38 | There the courtesans, their girdle-bells jingling at the steps of the dance, their hands tired from waving chowries whose handles are inlaid with the sheen of gems, will receive from you the first raindrops, sweet to their nail-marks, and cast on you long glances like lines of bees. |
39 | Then, settling in a circle over the forest of uplifted arms, wearing the twilight glow, red as a fresh hibiscus, take away Paśupati’s wish for the wet elephant-hide at the start of his dance, your devotion seen by Bhavānī, her fear quieted, her eyes steady. |
