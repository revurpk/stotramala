1 | The sky is packed with clouds that have come up — clouds that tear the soil of the hearts of women parted from their lovers; the earth’s dust is laid by the waters, and neither sun nor moon can be seen.
2 | The swans flee in fear of the thundering clouds; the evenings now have no moon; the peacocks, drunk on the fresh rain, cry out at the coming of the clouds, O you whose teeth are like jasmine buds.
3 | By night the cloud-covered sky does not shine, starless; sleep comes to Hari, who takes his ease; and the cloud with Indra’s bow, roaring, rouses the fury of elephants that are like mountains. (The third quarter is translated from the usual text, jalado’dya rasann ibhānām; the print’s reading gives no sense.)
4 | Water, brought by the lightning-bearing clouds, falls on the mountains where serpents cower from the thundering clouds, with a deep, circling roar, into the caves that are beautiful with their strange forms.
5 | Now some man quickly softens the faces of his beloveds, angered by love’s quarrel; the thundering clouds make the travellers long for home, and endless grief rises in their wives.
6 | When the sun’s radiance is hidden, when water falls from the sky and breeds grief, when Love rises to strike at the heart, a woman whose husband is away speaks thus:
7 | O clouds, passing over every other season you have come just when my beloved is gone; you will be the death of me, left without that cruel wretch who keeps to foreign lands.
8 | Tell that rake of a traveller, O clouds — you are the ones who cross the road swiftly: “Give up today your love of another land; or else, what is to be said to that wife of yours?”
9 | The line of swans, too, my lord, has now set out for Lake Mānasa in the sky; the cātaka, too, thirsty, begs for water; and your beloved, traveller, is in distress.
10 | The tender green crops shine, the cātaka finds pure water, the clouds make the flocks of peacocks cry out — what pleasure can you have today without your beloved?
11 | The peacocks, gladdened by the thunder, heap grief on the hearts of women whose husbands are away; and she, that wife of yours, wasted already, is today, with the coming of the clouds, being brought to ruin by Love, whom none can withstand.
12 | Have you no compassion at all, for your beloved, her cheeks pale and her hair falling over them? Only the memory of your virtues protects her, cast into the water of the sea of grief today.
13 | In the forests where the kuṭaja trees are in flower, with the faces of the loverless women longing, with the rivers flowing turbid — why do you not look at me, poor wretch?
14 | With the roads destroyed by rain-water, with Love, while I am without him, laying his hand to the bow of sharp arrows, I, shaken by the deep roar of the clouds — when, friend, shall I be rid of the burning grief of parting from my beloved?
15 | The groves of ketaka seem today, with their sweet scent, to be Love’s dwellings, conquered in the forest, fanned by the winds of the thundering clouds.
16 | It is well that Prajāpati made you so fine, O sarja tree, dwelling-place of Love! With your clusters of blossom you are the best in the forests, and a feast for the eyes of the young.
17 | New kadamba, I bow my head to you, since Love dwells in your smiling flowers; kuṭaja, why do you mock me with your flowers? I have fallen before you, hard to bear.
18 | Best of trees, I am always bowed before you; why do you make my heart burn? At the sight of your blossoms, in this wrong place, I would give up my body at once, O kadamba.
19 | Seeing that the time of honey has come, the bee kisses the jasmine creeper, adorned with white flowers that glisten with the drops of water let fall by the clouds.
20 | The season is truly fruitful for those women who, in the days dark with the thunder of clouds and Indra’s bow, celebrate the festival of love with their lovers, and bring their dear friends close, when the clouds come.
21 | Hearing this speech of the woman burning with the fire of separation, the compassionate one, told in their own thunder by the faithful clouds, came back home after only a few days.
22 | “Thirsty, and holding water to be drunk from the cup of my hands, I swear by the love-making of women devoted in their hearts: to the poet who beats me in yamaka I would carry water in a potsherd (ghaṭakarpara).” (Translated from the usual text, surataiḥ śapeyam; the print has suratairapeyam.)
