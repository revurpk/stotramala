1 | The sky is filled, once the clouds have come, with a gloom that burns the heart of women parted from their lovers; the earth’s dust is laid by the waters, and neither sun nor moon can be seen. |
2 | The swans flee in fear of the thundering clouds; the evenings no longer have the moon; the peacocks, drunk on the fresh rain, cry out; and when the clouds come, the jasmine shows its teeth (its white buds). |
3 | By night the cloud-covered sky shines no more, starless; sleep comes to Hari, who is pleased to serve it; and the cloud with Indra’s bow brings fury to the mountain-like (rutting elephants) and to the mountains. |
4 | Water, brought by the lightning-bearing clouds, falls on the mountains where serpents cower from the thundering clouds, with a deep, circling roar, into the caves that are beautiful with their strange forms. |
5 | Now some man quickly softens the faces of his beloveds, angered by love’s quarrel; the thundering clouds make the travellers long for home, and endless grief rises in their wives. |
6 | With the sun’s disc covered, with the sky streaming in a flood that stirs grief, with Love rising to strike the heart — this is what is said of women whose husbands are away. |
7 | O clouds, you have overcome every season and come just when my beloved has gone; you will kill me, who am cruelly forsaken by one who lives abroad — and him as well, if I am without him. |
8 | Tell that dusty traveller, O clouds, you who fly swiftly along the road: “Give up your love for another land today; or what are we to say of your wife?” |
9 | The line of swans, too, my lord, has now set out for Lake Mānasa in the sky; the cātaka, too, thirsty, begs for water; and your beloved, traveller, is in distress. |
10 | The tender green crops shine, the cātaka drinks pure water, the clouds make the flock of peacocks cry out — what pleasure can there be, today, without a beloved? |
11 | The peacocks, glad at the thunder, the husbands who are away, and the heart’s grief all sing out; and she, though worn thin by the coming of the clouds, is crushed by the mighty god of Love. |
12 | Have you no compassion at all, for your beloved, her cheeks pale and her hair falling over them? Only the memory of your virtues protects her, cast into the water of the sea of grief today. |
13 | In the forests where the kuṭaja trees are in flower, with the faces of the loverless women longing, with the rivers flowing turbid — why do you not look at me, poor wretch? |
14 | With the roads destroyed by rain-water, Love touching his bow with sharp arrows, I, pained by the deep roar of the clouds — when, friend, shall I give up the burning grief of parting from my beloved? |
15 | The groves of ketaka seem today, with their sweet scent, to be Love’s dwellings, conquered in the forest, fanned by the winds of the thundering clouds. |
16 | It is well that Prajāpati made you so very beautiful, O kadamba, dwelling-place of Love! You are the finest of the forest in your blossoms and a feast for the eyes of the young. |
17 | New kadamba, I bow my head to you, since Love dwells in your smiling flowers; kuṭaja, why do you mock me with your flowers? I have fallen before you, hard to bear. |
18 | Best of trees, I am always bowed before you; why do you make my heart burn? At the sight of your blossoms, in this wrong place, I would give up my body at once, O kadamba. |
19 | Seeing the season of the honey, the bee kisses the jasmine creeper adorned with white flowers, shining with drops like thick pearls of water. |
20 | The season is truly fruitful for those women who, in the days dark with the thunder of clouds and Indra’s bow, celebrate the festival of love with their lovers, and bring their dear friends close, when the clouds come. |
21 | Hearing this speech of the woman burning with the fire of separation, the compassionate one, told in their own thunder by the faithful clouds, came back home after only a few days. |
22 | “Taking up water in my thirst, to drink it in the cup of my hands, and not drink the sweetness of the love-making of women, I should be conquered by any poet who surpasses me in yamaka; to him I would carry water in a potsherd (ghaṭakarpara).” |
