1 | In Alakā the palaces can match you in every particular: you have lightning, they have lovely women; you Indra’s bow, they their paintings; you a deep, soft thunder, they drums struck for music; you water within, they floors of jewels; you are lofty, their tops touch the clouds. |
2 | There the women hold a play-lotus in the hand, wear young jasmine twined in the locks, have the beauty of the face made pale with lodhra pollen, a fresh kurubaka in the braid, a graceful śirīṣa at the ear, and in the parting of the hair the kadamba that comes with your arrival. |
3 | There the trees are always in flower, loud with maddened bees; the lotus-ponds always in bloom, girdled by rows of swans; the household peacocks, eager, cry out, their tails always shining; the evenings are always moonlit, lovely with the dark driven away. |
4 | There tears come only from joy; there is no pain but love’s arrows, and that is cured by the longed-for meeting; no parting but from a lovers’ quarrel; and for those wealthy yakṣas there is no age but youth. |
5 | There the yakṣas, coming with their fine women to the terraces of white-gem palaces strewn with flowers of starlight, slowly drink the wine borne by the wishing-trees, whose fruit is love, while the drums are struck to the echo of your deep sounds. |
6 | There the maidens whom the gods desire, fanned by the winds cool with the water of the Mandākinī, their heat warded off by the shade of the mandāra trees on its bank, play with gems hidden away by throwing handfuls of golden sand, which have to be hunted for. |
7 | There, when their lovers in passion tug with restless hands at the silk of women with bimba-red lips, loosened as the knot of the waist-cord slips, the girls, dazed with shyness, fling handfuls of powder at the jewel-lamps — in vain: even reaching the high flames, they do not put them out. |
8 | There clouds like you, led by the ever-moving wind to the upper floors of the mansions, spoil the paintings at once with drops of water and then, as if touched by fear, slip out through the lattices, shredded and clever at imitating smoke. |
9 | There the moonstones, hanging from nets of thread, brighten in the moonbeams at midnight, once you have stopped blocking them, and by dripping clear drops of water take away the weariness of love from the limbs of women embraced by their lovers’ arms. |
10 | There lovers, with inexhaustible treasures in their houses, enjoy themselves, talking together, in the outer garden called Vaibhrāja, in company with the finest of the heavenly courtesans, while Kinnaras with rich red throats sing every day the glory of the lord of wealth. |
11 | There the night road of the women is shown at sunrise by mandāra flowers fallen from their hair in their hurry, by leaf-ornaments, by golden lotuses slipped from their ears, by nets of pearls, and by necklaces whose threads are broken about the breasts. |
12 | There one wishing-tree alone bears all the ornaments of women: garments of many colours, the wine that teaches the eyes their sidelong play, flowers with shoots, a choice of jewels, and lac-dye fit for the setting down of lotus feet. |
13 | There the horses, dark as leaves, rival the sun’s steeds; the elephants, tall as mountains, give rain from their rut as you do; and the foremost warriors, who stand in battle against any ten-headed foe, have no taste for ornaments, for the scars of the Candrahāsa sword are enough. |
14 | There Kāma, thinking that the friend of the lord of wealth lives there in person, for fear mostly does not carry his bow strung with bees; yet his work is done by the clever women’s play of glances with knitted brows, which never miss their marks, the lovers. |
