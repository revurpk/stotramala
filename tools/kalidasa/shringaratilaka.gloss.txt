1 | Her two arms are lotus-stalks, her face a lotus, her loveliness a play of waters; her hips are the bathing-steps, her eyes the fish, her braid the moss; her breasts a pair of chakravāka birds — a lovely lake made by fate for those scorched by the fires of Love’s arrows to bathe in. |
2 | The honeyed night has come, and still my lord has not; let my life go into the fire, and I shall pray for another birth. The hunter at the cuckoo’s snare, Rāhu at the eclipse of the moon, and Love — the light from Śiva’s eye — all are against my beloved. (The text is partly obscure.) |
3 | The Creator made her eye of a blue lotus, her face of a lotus, her teeth of jasmine, her lip of a fresh leaf, her limbs of champaka petals — how then, my love, did he make her heart of stone? |
4 | A single fine wagtail seen on a lotus-leaf gives its beholder command over a four-fold army; what will it do for me, I do not know, when your eyes, those two wagtails, are on your lotus face. |
5 | Those who by chance see even one wagtail on a lotus become poets and widely famous kings; those who see the pair of wagtails in the lotus of your face — they all, innocent girl, are tormented by the net of Love’s arrows: what wonder? |
6 | Go quickly into the house, love, and do not stand outside: it is the hour of the eclipse of the cool-rayed moon; seeing your spotless face, Rāhu will surely swallow your moon-face and leave the full moon. |
7 | The pattern of musk-leaves is not worn from your cheeks, sandal is not rubbed from your breast, the eye-salve is not washed off, the colour of betel on your lip is not spoiled — why are you angry, you whose gait is a rutting elephant’s? Or is your husband a child? |
8 | “I am not (cold), my lord is not angry, nor asleep, nor ugly, nor old, nor a child, nor thin, nor sick, nor a fool.” Struck by Love’s arrow, a young, moon-faced woman, when her lover’s teacher released her, he went before her and came after her, bewildered. (The text is obscure.) |
9 | When her lover at last came after so long, we spent half the night, friend, in telling tales of the lands; then, when I was angry with a lovers’ quarrel, the east grew red like a co-wife. |
10 | Praiseworthy are a hundred blows on dry wood, praiseworthy the fierce sun, more praiseworthy the pain, with the heaps of mud, praiseworthy the great heat of fires — for the joy of the swing of a lover’s arms against the side of her breasts is not got, jar-bearer, without pains. |
11 | “Why, why do you come near my face and kiss it by force, shameless? Have you no shame? Let go the edge of my garment, rogue! Why do you cheat me with oaths, cheat? I am tired from your night-wakefulness; go to that beloved of yours — what pleasure have bees in a heap of discarded flowers?” |
12 | “My husband has gone to trade, and no news is heard of him; this morning his mother, who had a baby, has gone to her son-in-law’s house; I am a young girl alone at night — how can I stay? It is evening now, traveller: go elsewhere.” |
13 | “This night is full of dense clouds and fearful darkness; my husband, worn out by the toil of his work, is asleep; I am a young girl trembling from fear of rogues; the village has been plundered by thieves: traveller, give up your sleep.” |
14 | “Where are you off to, brother, to a doctor’s house? To cure what ailment? Have you no beloved at home, friend, who destroys every disease? She destroys the wind-humour by the pressing of her breast-jars, bile by the nectar of her mouth, and phlegm — alas — by the toil of love-play.” |
15 | “The lotus whose beauty matches your eyes has sunk in the water; the moon, which resembles the shade of your face, is hidden by clouds; the royal swans that followed your gait have gone — fate does not allow me even this small comfort of resemblance to you.” |
16 | The moon scorches like a fierce sun, the gentle breeze strikes like a thunderbolt, garlands are like heaps of needles, sandal-paste is sparks, light is like darkness, even life by fate is a burden — alas, the time of separation from the beloved is like the hour at the end of an age. |
17 | “My lord, this is my petition: spend some days there. This place is now unfit to live in; even the moon’s rays burn.” |
18 | “Dear one, sprinkle your body with sandal juices and somehow pass two or three days; I shall take you on my lap, embrace you with my arms, and make even the sun’s rays cool.” |
19 | “The line of flames of the fire of Love inside — is it relieved here by smearing of sandal paste? The mud smeared on the potter’s kiln only increases the heat and does not quench it.” |
20 | (The text is obscure.) Seeing the beauty of the eyes of courtesans, the best of the black antelopes have left the country; those whose breasts excel are the rutting elephants; fools mostly do not give up their pride even when defeated. |
21 | Her breasts pressed in a close embrace, the cheeks damp with sweat, the lip bitten, little gasps let out, the eyebrows quivering, the hands in motion, a charm of varied murmured flatteries and cries — the very home of the courtesan’s composure: the blessed man gets this delight of the flower-armed god. |
22 | Blessed is he who spends the night, wearied by the toil of love, resting his chest on the saffron-moist pair of his beloved’s breasts, broad as the temples of a rutting elephant, within a cage of her arms, and who gets a moment’s sleep. |
23 | “O Rohiṇī, you are the moon’s wife: restrain this ill-bred husband, friend! He comes through the window into my bedroom and touches my hips — is this proper in a family?” |
24 | “Some dull-witted fool here has said that liberation is something without pleasure or pain, without qualities; but in my view, liberation is the loosening of the girdle-knot of a moon-faced, wine-eyed woman, swaying with the smiling youth of Love.” |
25 | “Seeing this pair of breasts fallen, why, long-eyed one, do you shed sweat in vain? Whatever is stiff, thoughtless, a trouble to people, and too high, falls — what wonder is there in this?” |
26 | “O mango-blossom of Love, with long lovely eyes that reach to your ears — where are you going, taking my heart away? What is it that is unroyal and shines here as if royal?” |
27 | “If somehow, by fate, she should slip on a hard path and break her slender waist, let us not be blamed; see, her eyes have gone to her ears, as if to tell us that the way is blocked by her broad, close breasts.” |
28 | “If you have anger in your heart, lotus-eyed one, I grieve — what other conflict is there? Give me the embrace you gave me before, and give back the kisses I gave you.” |
29 | “Give up your pride, proud lady, my beloved goddess: false words are being heard. What anger can there be with your own servant if you take what I say as true? Bind me quickly with your arms, bite me, beat me with your full breasts; and if it is my fault, strike with the arrows of your glances.” |
30 | “Why do you look at me, with a pot on your hip, with lovely half-closed eyes in your face? Look at another man, worthy of your fortune; I do not court a woman with a pot-marked hip.” |
31 | “You speak truly: I do not think of the torment of the flower-banner’s arrow with the eyes you offered; my servant, of your form, has been broken off today — ‘Is it he or not?’ is my doubt.” (The text is obscure.) |
