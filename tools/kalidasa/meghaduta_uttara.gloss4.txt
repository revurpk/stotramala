44 | “In the priyaṅgu creepers I see your limbs, in the startled doe your glance, in the moon the lustre of your face, in the peacocks’ plumage your hair, in the thin ripples of the river your play of brows; but alas, fierce one, nowhere do I find all of you together.” |
45 | “When I paint you on a rock in mineral colours, angry with love, and then try to paint myself fallen at your feet, tears welling again and again blot out my sight; even there cruel fate cannot bear our meeting.” |
45a | “Far from your face, girl, fragrant as earth sprinkled with showers, the god of the five arrows torments me, though I am thin already. Think how these days at the end of summer will pass, with the sun’s heat scattered by clouds spread across the quarters.” (This verse is not in all manuscripts, and its text is uncertain.) |
46 | “When I somehow get you in my dreams, and stretch out my arms into the air to embrace you, the goddesses of the place who watch me often let fall tears as big as pearls on the tree-shoots.” |
47 | “Those winds from the Snow Mountain that blow from the south, splitting at once the shoot-sheaths of the deodar trees, fragrant with their sap, I embrace, virtuous one, thinking that perhaps they touched your limbs before.” |
48 | “How can the three-watch night be shortened to a moment? How can the day be mild in its heat in every state? So, restless-eyed one, my mind, with wishes so hard to fulfil, is left helpless by the pangs of parting from you, hot to the core.” |
49 | “I sustain myself by my own thoughts, counting myself fortunate; you too, auspicious one, must not fall into utter dejection. Whose happiness is unbroken, or whose sorrow is wholly unrelieved? Our condition goes down and up like the rim of a wheel.” |
50 | “My curse ends when the bearer of the Śārṅga bow rises from his serpent-bed; pass the four months that remain with your eyes shut. Afterwards we shall both enjoy every wish, multiplied by our parting, in the nights of ripened autumn moonlight.” |
51 | “And he said again: ‘Once, as you lay in bed with your arms about my neck, you fell asleep and then woke up weeping aloud; when I asked and asked, you said, laughing inwardly: “In a dream, deceiver, I saw you making love to some woman!”’” |
52 | “By this token know that I am well, and, dark-eyed one, do not mistrust me because of gossip. They say love is destroyed in separation, but love, deprived of enjoyment, gathers its sweetness for the thing desired and becomes a heap of affection.” |
53 | “Having comforted your friend in this way, her grief keen in this first parting, return quickly from that mountain whose peak the bull of the three-eyed god tore up, and sustain my life, loose as a jasmine flower at dawn, with her words in answer, bringing news of her welfare and carrying a token.” |
54 | “Gentle one, will you do this kindness for a friend? I do not take your silence for a refusal, for I think you steadfast; you give water to the cātakas when they ask, though without a word; for the good, the answer to those who ask is to do what is wanted.” |
55 | “Having done this kindness for me, though my request was improper, whether out of friendship or out of pity for me, bereft, go, cloud, to whatever lands you please, your beauty swollen by the rains; and may you never be parted, even for a moment, from your lightning.” |
