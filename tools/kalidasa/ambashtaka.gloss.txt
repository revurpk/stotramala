1 | May the daughter of the Mountain King — she whom all the women of the sky-goers serve as handmaids in the kadamba groves, whose feet are flecked with the countless rays of the gems on the lovely tips of the diadems of the host of gods, whose breast-cloth is fragrant with sandal — spread in me, by the juice of the betel in her gracious mouth, a flow of poetry swifter in its rush than a troop of mares.
2 | May Śailarāja’s daughter, who is a flame of fire to the cotton-heaps of fears that overflow every bank, who drives away Time (death) with the tumult of her own praise, a cloud nourishing the water of well-being, broad in the breast, dark as a rain-cloud in her hair, playing in the Kadamba forest and ever bowing to the one with the trident, shine in my heart.
3 | May the Mountain’s daughter dwell wherever my mind comes to rest — she with her matchless parrot, whose feet give sure protection to the host of the gods, the eaters of the sacrifice, with Indra and Yama at their head; (the third quarter is obscure: perhaps) whose mount outruns the wind, whose companions are goddesses, friends for their lovely qualities; whose form is brilliant with gems, and who is skilled in granting sons and the like.
4 | May the Blessed Goddess lift me out of the well of sin — she the dust of whose feet is a stairway to heaven for Dvaipāyana and the other sages, whose weapon is the curse; who is skilled at dispelling the burning pain of those absorbed in repeating her sin-destroying mantra; who dwells among the kadamba trees, her hair fragrant with incense; surpassing in beauty, a jewel-lamp to the line of the Mountain King.
5 | May that Kālī make my mind a bee in the service of the lotus of her feet — she who plays with her companions among the rows of priyaka (kadamba) trees (two words here are obscure); a mongoose to the serpent of fear; heavy with black tresses; by the dust of whose feet the best of sages shine; who wears a palm-leaf in her ringed ear, and a tilaka bright on her forehead.
6 | O heart, why do you set your desire on this body — a mine of foulness (the word is doubtful), which feeds herons and their like with its blood and is the portion of the carrion birds? Come to the Mountain’s daughter, the enemy of that mire: her feet are sharp chisels to the rock of doubt, and bees hum in a line over the flowers that shine there; her lotus face is like a moon without its mark. (The last quarter is two syllables short in the print; it is translated from the usual text.)
7 | May the Mother grant me well-being here in the kadamba forest — (the opening words are corrupt in the print; the usual text has: with her neck she mocks, as it were, the conch); a lute of bright fresh gourd at her side; her heavy breasts pressed by the moon-fair face of Bāhuleya (Kārttikeya); with the dark gleam of musk-paste; long-haired, bimba-lipped; before whom the host of gods, led by the wielder of the thunderbolt, bow.
8 | May the Mountain’s daughter grant me well-being, a splendour that eclipses even the sun, and, soon, union with her feet — she on whose wrist a parrot gleams; who delights beyond measure in Bhava, the kinsman of her heart; who is pledged, too, to the upholding of the world; whose noble home is in the ocean of nectar; whose braid is ringed by bees blinded again and again by the taste of its fragrance.
