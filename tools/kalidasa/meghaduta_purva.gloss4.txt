40 | At night, on the king’s road, where the light is shut out by darkness thick enough to be pierced by a needle, show the way to the women going to their lovers’ houses with a flash of lightning bright as a streak on a gold touchstone; but do not roar and pour down rain — they are easily frightened. |
41 | Spend that night, with your lightning-wife worn out by flashing so long, on the roof-terrace of some house where the pigeons sleep, and when the sun is seen again, go on with the rest of the road; those who have undertaken a friend’s business do not dawdle. |
42 | At that hour the tears of women whose lovers have been unfaithful are to be dried by those lovers; so leave the sun’s path quickly. He too returns to wipe the dew from the lotus-pond’s lotus-face, and if you block his rays he will be bitterly angry. |
43 | Into the water of the Gambhīrā, clear as a mind, your reflection, though only a shadow, will find entry, being naturally lovely; so out of mere firmness you should not make vain her glances, white as lilies, that flash like the leaping of quick fish. |
44 | Take away her blue water-garment, which seems held a little in the hand of the cane-branches at the edge, slipped from the hips of her banks; as you hang over her, your leaving will somehow come hard, friend: who that has known the taste of it could abandon a woman whose thighs are bare? |
45 | The cool wind, delightful with the scent of earth breathed out under your showers, sweetly sounding in the trunk-holes of the elephants as they drink it, and ripening the wild figs of the forest, will blow gently as you set out toward Devagiri. |
46 | There, turning yourself into a cloud of flowers, bathe Skanda, who dwells there always, with showers of blossoms wet with the water of the heavenly Ganges; for that brilliance, greater than the sun’s, was gathered into the mouth of Fire by the moon-crowned god, to guard the armies of Indra. |
47 | Afterwards make his peacock dance with your thunder, doubled by the mountain’s caves — the peacock whose fallen feather, ringed with bands of light, Bhavānī in love for her son wears at her ear beside the lotus petal, and whose eye-corners are washed with the glow of Śiva’s moon. |
48 | When you have worshipped the god born in the reeds and gone on, the Siddha couples with their vīṇās leaving your path for fear of your drops, descend to honour the glory of Rantideva, which took shape on earth as a river, born of the sacrifice of cows. |
49 | When you bend to take up her water, thief of Viṣṇu’s colour, the sky-travellers, lowering their eyes, will surely see her stream, though broad, thin from afar, like a single string of pearls of the earth with a great sapphire in the middle. |
50 | Crossing her, go on, making your own reflection the object of curiosity to the eyes of the women of Daśapura, whose play of brows is well known, whose glances, as the lashes are raised, gleam dark and dappled, and which steal the beauty of bees following a flung jasmine. |
51 | Then, passing in your shadow over the land of Brahmāvarta, visit the field of the Kurus, witness to the battle of the warriors, where the wielder of Gāṇḍīva rained hundreds of sharp arrows on the faces of the kings, as you rain showers on lotuses. |
52 | The plough-bearer, shunning battle out of kindness to his kin, gave up the liquor he loved, marked with the reflection of Revatī’s eyes, and served those waters; go down to the Sarasvatī’s waters, gentle one, and you too will be clean within, dark only in colour. |
53 | From there go on by Kanakhala to the daughter of Jahnu, who came down from the king of mountains, the stair to heaven for Sagara’s sons; with her foam she seems to laugh at the frown on Gaurī’s face, and with hands of waves clasping the moon she took hold of Śambhu’s hair. |
