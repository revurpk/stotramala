## Lesson XXXIV
¶ **366. III. Secondary Adjective Compounds.** A compound with a noun as final member very often wins secondarily the value of an adjective, being inflected in the three genders to agree with the noun which it qualifies, and used in all the constructions of an adjective. The two divisions of this class have been given above (§ 349).
¶ **367.** Certain changes are sometimes necessary in the stem of the final member to make possible the inflection in different genders. Masc. and neut. stems in ⟨a⟩, and fem. in ⟨ā⟩, generally interchange; thus, from ⟨su⟩ + ⟨hasta⟩ comes the compound ⟨suhasta⟩ ‘with excellent hands’, nom. sing. ⟨suhastas⟩, ⟨-stā⟩, ⟨-stam⟩; so also (from ⟨tri⟩ + ⟨jihvā⟩) ⟨trijihvas⟩, ⟨-hvā⟩, ⟨-hvam⟩; and (from ⟨su⟩ + ⟨phala⟩ n.) ⟨suphalas⟩, ⟨-lā⟩, ⟨-lam⟩. The same holds good for masc., fem., and neut. stems in ⟨i⟩ and ⟨u⟩, and stems in consonants.
¶ **368.** But often a fem. in ⟨ī⟩ is used by the side of a masc. and neut. in ⟨a⟩; thus, ⟨dviparṇa⟩ ‘two-leaved’, f. ⟨dviparṇī⟩.
¶ **369.** Very frequently the suffix ⟨ka⟩ (attenuated into an element of indefinite value) is added to a pure possessive compound, to help the conversion of the compounded stem into an adjective; especially to fem. stems in ⟨ī⟩ and ⟨ū⟩, and to stems in ⟨ṛ⟩; and in general, where the final of the stem is less usual or manageable in adjective inflection. Thus, ⟨bahunadīka⟩ ‘rich in rivers’; ⟨mṛtabhartṛkā⟩ f., ‘whose husband is dead’, i. e. ‘widow’; ⟨mahāyaśas⟩ (nom. masc. and fem. ⟨-yāḥ⟩) or ⟨mahāyaśaska⟩.
¶ **370.** Sometimes the possessive-making suffix ⟨in⟩ is added to secondary adjective compounds, without effect upon the meaning; thus, ⟨gardabhanādin⟩ (= ⟨-nāda⟩) ‘having an ass’s voice.’

¶ **371. A. Possessive compounds.** The possessives are determinative compounds to which are given both an adjective inflection (as just shown), and also an adjective meaning of a kind best defined by adding ‘having’ to the meaning of the determinative. Thus, the dependent ⟨devarūpa⟩ n., ‘beauty of a god’, becomes the possessive ⟨devarūpa⟩, ⟨-ā⟩, m. f. n., ‘having the beauty of a god’; the descriptive ⟨dīrghabāhu⟩ m., ‘long arm’, becomes the possessive ⟨dīrghabāhu⟩ m. f. n, ‘having long arms’.∗
¶ **372.** Dependent compounds are, by comparison, not often thus turned into possessives. But possessively used descriptives are extremely frequent and various; and some kinds of combination which are rare in proper descriptives are very common as possessives.
¶ **373.** An adjective as prior member takes the masculine stem-form, even though referring to a feminine noun in the final member; thus, ⟨rūpavadbhārya⟩ (from ⟨bhāryā⟩) ‘possessing a beautiful wife’.
¶ **374.** As prior members are found:
¶ 1. Adjectives proper; thus, ⟨anyarūpa⟩ ‘of other form’. — 2. Participles; thus, ⟨hatamātṛka⟩ ‘whose mother is slain’. — 3. Numerals; thus, ⟨caturmukha⟩ ‘four-faced’; ⟨trilocana⟩ ‘three-eyed’. — 4. Nouns with *quasi*-adjective value; thus, ⟨hiraṇyahasta⟩ ‘gold-handed’. Especially common is the use of a noun as prior member to qualify the other appositionally, or by way of equivalence. These may well be called **appositional possessives.** Thus, ⟨kṛṣṇanāman⟩ ‘having “Kṛṣṇa” as name’; ⟨vīrapuruṣa⟩ ‘having men who are heroes’; ⟨cāracakṣus⟩ ‘using spies as eyes’; ⟨tvaddūta⟩ ‘having thee as messenger’. — 5. Adverbial elements (especially inseparable prefixes);
¶ ∗ This class of compounds is called by the natives ⟨bahuvrīhi⟩; the name is an example of the class, meaning ‘having much rice’. — The possessive may generally, in accented texts, be distinguished from the original determinative by a difference of accent.

¶ thus, ⟨ananta⟩ ‘endless’; ⟨aputra⟩ ‘childless’; ⟨suputra⟩ ‘with excellent sons’; ⟨durgandhi⟩ ‘ill-savored’. The associative prefix ⟨sa⟩ (less often ⟨saha⟩) is treated like an adjective element; thus, ⟨sarūpa⟩ ‘of like form’; ⟨saputra⟩ or ⟨sahaputra⟩ ‘with a son’, or ‘having one’s son along with one’; ⟨sānukūla⟩ (⟨sa⟩ + ⟨anukūla⟩ n.) ‘favorable’. — 6. Ordinary verbal prefixes; thus, ⟨praśravas⟩ ‘of wide fame’; ⟨vyaṅga⟩ ‘limbless’; ⟨nirbala⟩ ‘powerless’; ⟨unmukha⟩ ‘with uplifted face’. — 7. Ordinary adverbs; thus, ⟨ihacitta⟩ ‘with mind directed hither’.
¶ **375.** Certain words, very frequent in the compounds mentioned at § 374, 4, have in part won a peculiar application.
¶ 1. Thus with ⟨ādi⟩ ‘beginning’ (or the derivatives ⟨ādya⟩ or ⟨ādika⟩) are made compounds signifying the person or thing designated along with others — such a person or thing *et cetera*. Thus, ⟨devā indrādayaḥ⟩ ‘the gods having Indra as first’, i. e. ‘the gods Indra, etc.’ Often the qualifying noun is omitted; thus, ⟨annapānādīni⟩ ‘food, drink, etc.’ — 2. Words like ⟨pūrva⟩ (⟨pūrvaka⟩) etc., are used in the same way, to denote accompaniment; chiefly adverbially. — 3. The noun ⟨artha⟩ ‘object’, ‘purpose’, is used at the end of a compound, oftenest as a neut. subst. (acc. or instr. or loc.), to signify ‘for the sake of’, and the like; thus, ⟨damayantyartham⟩ ‘for Damayantī’s sake’; ⟨śayyārtham⟩ ‘for a bed’. (See below, § 379). — 4. ⟨antara⟩ (as neut. subst.) often means ‘other’ in possessives; thus, ⟨deśāntara⟩ n., ‘another region’ (lit. ‘that which has a difference of region’).
¶ **376.** In appositional possessives, the final member, if it designate a part of the body, sometimes signifies the part to which belongs what is designated by the prior member: that on or in which it is. Thus ⟨maṇigrīva⟩ ‘with necklace on neck’. Such compounds are commonest with words meaning hand; thus, ⟨asipāṇi⟩ ‘with sword in hand’; ⟨laguḍahasta⟩ ‘with club in hand’.
¶ **377.** The possessives are not always used with the simple

¶ value of qualifying adjective. Often they have a pregnant sense, and become the equivalents of dependent clauses; or the ‘having’ implied in them becomes about equivalent to our ‘having’ as a sign of past action. Thus, ⟨prāptayauvana⟩ ‘possessing attained adolescence’, i. e. ‘having reached adolescence’; ⟨anadhigataśāstra⟩ ‘with unstudied books’, i. e. ‘one who has neglected study’; ⟨gataprāṇa⟩ ‘whose breath is gone’, i. e. ‘lifeless’; ⟨āsannamṛtyu⟩ ‘to whom death is come near’.
¶ **378. B. Compounds with governed final member.**
¶ 1. **Participial** compounds, exclusively Vedic.
¶ 2. **Prepositional** compounds. Thus may conveniently be called those compounds in which the prior member is a particle with true prepositional value, and the final member a noun governed by it. Thus, ⟨atirātra⟩ ‘lasting over night’; ⟨atimātra⟩ ‘beyond measure’, ‘excessive’; ⟨apikarṇa⟩ ‘next the ear’.
¶ **379. Adjective compounds as nouns and adverbs.** Compound adjectives, like simple ones, are freely used substantively as abstracts and collectives, especially in the neuter, and less often in the feminine; and they are also much used adverbially, particularly in the acc. sing. neuter.
¶ **380.** The substantively used possessive compounds having a numeral as prior member, with some of the strictly adjective compounds, are treated by the Hindus as a separate class, and called *dvigu*∗. Examples of such numeral abstracts and collectives are: ⟨triyuga⟩ n., ‘the three ages’; ⟨triyojana⟩ n., ‘space of three leagues’. Feminines of like use occur in the later language; thus ⟨trilokī⟩ (by the side of ⟨-loka⟩ n.,) ‘the three worlds’.
