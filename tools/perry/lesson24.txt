## Lesson XXIV
¶ **263. Declension. Stems in ⟨ant⟩ (or ⟨at⟩), cont’d. B. Stems in ⟨mant⟩ (⟨mat⟩) and ⟨vant⟩ (⟨vat⟩).** Adjectives formed with these suffixes are possessives. They are declined precisely alike∗; and differ in inflection from the participles in ⟨ant⟩ only by lengthening the ⟨a⟩ in the nom. sing. masc. The feminine is made in ⟨ī⟩; thus, ⟨śrīmatī⟩. In the dual neut. ⟨n⟩ is never inserted. Thus, ⟨śrīmant⟩, ‘rich’, ‘celebrated:’

¶ **264.** A stem ⟨bhavant⟩∗ (to be carefully distinguished from the pres. part. act. ⟨bhavant⟩ of ⟨bhū⟩; the two differ only in accent in Perry’s print: bhávant and bhavánt) is frequently used in respectful address as a substitute for the pronoun of the second person. It is construed with the verb in the third person. Its nom. sing. masc. is ⟨bhavān⟩ (fem. ⟨bhavatī⟩); and ⟨bhos⟩, the contracted form of its older voc. ⟨bhavas⟩, is a common exclamation of address: “you, sir!”, “ho, there!”; and is often doubled.∗∗
¶ **265. Derivative stems in ⟨an⟩.** These are made by the suffixes ⟨an⟩, ⟨man⟩, and ⟨van⟩, and are, with one or two exceptions, masc. and neut. only. The stem has a triple form. In the strong cases of the masc. the vowel of the suffix is lengthened to ⟨ā⟩, in the weakest cases it is in general dropped; in the middle cases the final ⟨n⟩ is dropped, and it is also lost in the nom. sing. of all genders. In the neuter, the nom.-acc. pl., as being strong cases, lengthen the vowel of the suffix; the same cases in the dual (as weakest cases) lose ⟨a⟩ — but this only optionally. After the ⟨m⟩ or ⟨v⟩ of ⟨man⟩ and ⟨van⟩, when these are preceded by a consonant, the ⟨a⟩ is retained in all the weakest cases, to avoid too great an accumulation of consonants. Examples: ⟨rājan⟩ m., ‘king’; ⟨nāman⟩ n., ‘name’; ⟨ātman⟩ m., ‘soul, self’; ⟨brahman⟩ n., ‘devotion’.
¶ ∗ Probably contracted from ⟨bhagavant⟩ ‘blessed’.
¶ ∗∗ ⟨bhos⟩ loses its final ⟨s⟩ before all vowels and all sonant consonants; thus, ⟨bho bho ṛṣe⟩.

¶ **266. Euphonic rules.** Final ⟨k⟩, ⟨ṭ⟩ and ⟨p⟩ remain unaltered before initial surd consonants; before sonants, whether vowel or consonant, they become respectively ⟨g⟩, ⟨ḍ⟩, ⟨b⟩. Before nasals they may be still further assimilated, becoming the nasals ⟨ṅ⟩, ⟨ṇ⟩, ⟨m⟩. Thus, ⟨parivrāṭ na⟩ becomes either ⟨parivrāḍ na⟩ or ⟨parivrāṇ na⟩; ⟨samyak na⟩ becomes ⟨samyag na⟩ or ⟨samyaṅ na⟩. The latter method is much more usual.
¶ **267.** Before initial ⟨h⟩ a final mute is made sonant; and then the ⟨h⟩ may either remain unchanged, or be converted into the sonant aspirate corresponding to the preceding letter; thus, either ⟨samyag hastaḥ⟩ or ⟨samyag ghastaḥ⟩; either ⟨tasmād hastāt⟩ or ⟨tasmād dhastāt⟩. In practice the latter method is almost invariably followed.
¶ ∗ When a dental mute comes in contact with a lingual or palatal mute or sibilant, the dental is usually assimilated, becoming lingual or palatal respectively. Thus, ⟨tiṣṭhati⟩ from ⟨ti-stha-ti⟩; ⟨rājñā⟩ instead of ⟨rājnā⟩.

E 24 | The king of Pāṭaliputra is by birth a Śūdra; let him not marry the beautiful daughter of the ascetic Mitrātithi. | pāṭaliputrasya rājā janmanā śūdro mitrātither yater duhitaraṃ sundarīṃ na pariṇayatu | పాటలీపుత్ర రాజు పుట్టుకతో శూద్రుడు; అతడు యతి మిత్రాతిథి సుందరమైన కూతురిని వివాహమాడకూడదు.
