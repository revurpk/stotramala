## Lesson XXIX
¶ **304. Gerund, or Absolutive.** The gerund is made in classical Sanskrit by one of the suffixes ⟨tvā⟩ and ⟨ya⟩.
@137
¶ **305. A. ⟨tvā⟩.** To uncompounded roots is added the suffix ⟨tvā⟩. It is usually added directly to the root, but sometimes with the vowel ⟨i⟩ interposed. With regard to the use of ⟨i⟩, and to the form of root before it, this formation closely agrees with that of the participle in ⟨ta⟩ or ⟨na⟩. A final root-consonant is treated as before ⟨ta⟩. Roots which make the past pass. part. in ⟨na⟩ generally reject ⟨i⟩ before ⟨tvā⟩.
¶ Examples. 1. Without inserted ⟨i⟩: ⟨jñātvā⟩, ⟨jitvā⟩, ⟨nītvā⟩, ⟨śrutvā⟩, ⟨bhūtvā⟩; ⟨sthitvā⟩ from ⟨sthā⟩, ⟨hitvā⟩ from 1⟨dhā⟩ ‘place’ (cf. ⟨hita⟩) and from ⟨hā⟩, ⟨dattvā⟩ from ⟨dā⟩ (cf. ⟨datta⟩), ⟨gītvā⟩ from ⟨gā⟩; ⟨uktvā⟩ from ⟨vac⟩, ⟨yuktvā⟩ from ⟨yuj⟩; ⟨gatvā⟩ from ⟨gam⟩, ⟨matvā⟩ from ⟨man⟩, ⟨vittvā⟩ from 2⟨vid⟩ ‘find’; ⟨tīrtvā⟩ from ⟨tṛ⟩ (cf. ⟨tīrṇa⟩), ⟨pūrtvā⟩ from ⟨pṛ⟩ (cf. ⟨pūrṇa⟩); ⟨dṛṣṭvā⟩ from ⟨dṛś⟩, ⟨sṛṣṭvā⟩ from ⟨sṛj⟩, ⟨iṣṭvā⟩ from ⟨yaj⟩ (cf. § 295, 2), ⟨buddhvā⟩ from ⟨budh⟩, ⟨labdhvā⟩ from ⟨labh⟩, ⟨dagdhvā⟩ from ⟨dah⟩.
¶ 2. With inserted ⟨i⟩: ⟨viditvā⟩ from 1⟨vid⟩ ‘know’, ⟨uṣitvā⟩ from ⟨vas⟩ ‘dwell’, ⟨śayitvā⟩ from ⟨śī⟩ (cf. ⟨śayita⟩), ⟨gṛhītvā⟩ from ⟨grah⟩ (cf. ⟨gṛhīta⟩).
¶ **306.** Some verbs make both forms; thus, from ⟨khan⟩ either ⟨khanitvā⟩ or ⟨khātvā⟩; from ⟨bhram⟩ either ⟨bhramitvā⟩ or ⟨bhrāntvā⟩.
¶ **307.** Causatives and denominatives in ⟨aya⟩ make ⟨ayitvā⟩; thus, ⟨cur⟩, ⟨corayitvā⟩; ⟨taḍ⟩, ⟨tāḍayitvā⟩; ⟨sthāpayati⟩, ⟨sthāpayitvā⟩.
¶ **308. B. ⟨ya⟩.** Roots in composition with prepositions (or sometimes with elements of other kinds, as adverbs or nouns) take the suffix ⟨ya⟩, before which ⟨i⟩ is never inserted. A root which ends in a short vowel adds ⟨t⟩ before ⟨ya⟩. Thus, ⟨pariṇīya⟩, ⟨anubhūya⟩; — ⟨vijitya⟩, ⟨saṃstutya⟩, ⟨adhītya⟩ (⟨adhi-i⟩) ⟨adhikṛtya⟩.
¶ **309.** Roots in ⟨am⟩ and ⟨an⟩ whose pass. part. ends in ⟨ata⟩ form this gerund in ⟨atya⟩; thus, ⟨-gatya⟩, ⟨-hatya⟩. But such ⟨am⟩-roots (not ⟨an⟩-roots) may preserve the nasal; thus, ⟨-gamya⟩. Final changeable ⟨ṛ⟩ becomes ⟨īr⟩ or ⟨ūr⟩; thus, ⟨-tīrya⟩, ⟨-pūrya⟩. Final ⟨ā⟩ remains unaltered; thus, ⟨ādāya⟩. Some roots show a weak form before
@138
¶ this suffix; thus, ⟨pragṛhya⟩, ⟨saṃpṛcchya⟩; ⟨procya⟩ (⟨pra-ucya⟩) from ⟨pra-vac⟩; ⟨anūdya⟩ from ⟨anu-vad⟩; ⟨vyūhya⟩ from ⟨vi-vah⟩.
¶ **310.** Causals and denominatives in ⟨aya⟩ reject those syllables; thus, ⟨pracorayati⟩, ⟨pracorya⟩; ⟨pratāḍya⟩; ⟨prasthāpya⟩; ⟨avaghātya⟩; ⟨ānāyayati⟩ (⟨ā-nī⟩), ⟨ānāyya⟩. But if the root ends in a single consonant and encloses short ⟨a⟩ which is not lengthened in the causative, then the gerund of the caus. ends in ⟨ayya⟩, to distinguish it from the gerund of the simple verb; thus, ⟨ava-gam⟩, ger. ⟨avagamya⟩; caus. ⟨avagamayati⟩, ger. ⟨avagamayya⟩.
¶ **311.** The gerund or absolutive is used generally as logical adjunct to the subject of a clause. It denotes an action accompanying or (usually) preceding that which is signified by the verb of the clause. (In the later language it is not always confined to the *grammatical* subject of the clause as an adjunct.) It has thus virtually the value of an indeclinable participle, present or past, qualifying the actor whose action it describes.
¶ Thus, ⟨tad ākarṇya chāgaṃ tyaktvā snātvā svagṛhaṃ gataḥ⟩ “having heard this, having abandoned the goat, having bathed, he went to his own house”.∗
¶ **312.** The gerunds of some verbs have not much more than prepositional value; thus, ⟨ādāya⟩ ‘having taken’, i. e. ‘with’, like Greek labōn, echōn; ⟨muktvā⟩ ‘having released’, i. e. ‘without’, ‘except’.
¶ **313.** Before all gerunds may be used the privative ⟨an⟩ or ⟨a⟩; thus, ⟨alabdhvā⟩ “without having received”; ⟨anāhūya⟩ “without having summoned.”
