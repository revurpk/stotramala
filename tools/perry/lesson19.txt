¶ **229.** The nom. sing. masc. ⟨sas⟩, and its compound ⟨eṣas⟩, lose their final ⟨s⟩ before *any* consonant; before vowels, and at the end of a sentence, they follow the usual euphonic rules. Thus, ⟨sa gacchati⟩, ⟨sa tiṣṭhati⟩; ⟨sa icchati⟩; ⟨so 'sti⟩; ⟨gacchati saḥ⟩.
¶ **230.** The third personal pronoun is used oftenest as a weak or indefinite demonstrative, especially as antecedent to a relative; and often like the English “definite article.”
¶ **231.** Like ⟨ta⟩ are declined: (*a*) ⟨eta⟩, ‘this’, formed by prefixing ⟨e⟩ to the forms of ⟨ta⟩, throughout; thus, nom. sing. m. ⟨eṣas⟩, f. ⟨eṣā⟩, n. ⟨etad⟩; (*b*) the relative pronoun (and adj.) ⟨ya⟩, ‘which, who’; (*c*) comparatives and superlatives from pronominal roots, such as ⟨katara⟩, which (of the two)?’ and ⟨katama⟩ ‘which (of the many)?’ So ⟨yatara⟩ and ⟨yatama⟩; ⟨ekatama⟩ ‘one of many’; ⟨anya⟩ ‘other’, with its comparative ⟨anyatara⟩; and ⟨itara⟩ ‘different’. — Yet other words are so inflected, but with ⟨am⟩ instead of ⟨ad⟩ in nom.-acc.-voc. sing. neut.: as, ⟨sarva⟩, ⟨viśva⟩, ‘all’; ⟨eka⟩ ‘one’, in pl. ‘some’; ⟨ubhaya⟩, f. ⟨-yī⟩ (only sing. and pl.), ‘both’.
¶ **232.** The interrogative pronoun ⟨ka⟩ (for which the Hindus give the base as ⟨kim⟩) follows precisely the declension of ⟨ta⟩, except nom.-acc. sing. neut. ⟨kim⟩; nom. sing. m. ⟨kas⟩, f. ⟨kā⟩.
¶ **233.** A number of words follow the pronominal declension in some of their significations, or optionally; but in other senses, or without known rule, lapse into the adjective inflection. Such are comparatives and superlatives from prepositional stems, as ⟨adhara⟩ ‘lower’, ⟨adhama⟩ ‘lowest’; ⟨para⟩ ‘chief’, ⟨pūrva⟩ ‘earlier’, ⟨uttara⟩ ‘upper’,
@99
¶ ‘northern’, ⟨dakṣiṇa⟩ ‘southern’, etc. Occasional forms of the pronominal declension are met with from numeral adjectives, and from other words having somewhat of a numeral character, as ⟨alpa⟩ ‘few’, ⟨ardha⟩ ‘half’, etc.
¶ **234. Peculiarities in the use of relative pronouns, etc.** The Sanskrit often puts the relative clause before the antecedent clause, and inserts the substantive to which the relative refers into the same clause with the relative, instead of leaving it in the antecedent clause. In translating into Sanskrit, a relative clause is to be placed either before or after the whole antecedent clause; but not inserted into the antecedent clause, as is done in English. Thus, “the mountain which we saw yesterday is very high” would be in Sanskrit either: ⟨yaṃ parvataṃ vayaṃ hyo 'paśyāma so 'tīva tuṅgaḥ⟩, or: ⟨sa parvato 'tīva tuṅgo yaṃ hyo 'paśyāma⟩; but not ⟨sa parvato yaṃ vayaṃ hyo 'paśyāma⟩, etc., according to the English idiom.
¶ **235.** The relative word may stand anywhere in its clause; thus, ⟨śiva ādir yeṣāṃ te devāḥ⟩: “the gods whose chief is Çiva”. Sometimes relative or demonstrative adverbs are used as equivalents of certain case-forms of relative or demonstrative pronouns; thus, ⟨yatra vane⟩ = ⟨yasmin vane⟩.
¶ **236.** The repetition of the relative gives an indefinite meaning: ‘whosoever, whatever’. The same result is much more commonly attained by adding to the relative the interrogative pronoun, with (or, less usually, without) one of the particles ⟨ca⟩, ⟨cana⟩, ⟨cid⟩, ⟨api⟩, ⟨vā⟩. Sometimes the interrogative alone is used with these particles in a similar sense. Thus; ⟨yad yad eṣā kathayati⟩ “whatever this (woman) relates”; ⟨yo yasya bhāvaḥ syāt⟩ “whatever any one’s disposition may be”; ⟨yasmai kasmai cid yacchati⟩ “he gives to some one or other”; ⟨yasmāt kasmāc cin na labhate⟩ “he takes from no one whatever”.
