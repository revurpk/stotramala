# English key to Nirukta adhyāya 1, one khaṇḍa a line: number | heading | gloss. Original.
1 | The word-list and the four kinds of words | The Nighaṇṭu is the traditional list of Vedic words, "nighaṇṭu" because they are taken from the hymns (nigama). Words are of four kinds: nouns, verbs, prefixes and particles. A verb has becoming as its chief sense and tells an action as a sequence ("he goes, he cooks"); a noun tells a being, or the action fixed as a thing ("going, cooking"). Audumbarāyaṇa holds that speech lasts only while it is in the sense of hearing.
2 | Words as names; the six modifications of being | On Audumbarāyaṇa's view the fourfold division fails, since words do not arise together; but words are used to name things because they are wide-reaching and brief, and so the gods are named as men are. Vārṣyāyaṇi's six modifications of being: it is born, it exists, it changes, it grows, it wanes, it perishes, each explained.
3 | The prefixes | All other modifications are forms of these six. Śākaṭāyana holds that prefixes on their own express no meaning and only bring out the meaning of a noun or verb; Gārgya, that they have various meanings of their own. The senses of the prefixes, from ā ("hither") to adhi ("over, lordship").
4 | The particles: comparison and conjunction | Particles fall in various senses: comparison, conjunction, and filling out the line. Iva, na, cit and nu as particles of comparison, each shown in use; then ca, ā and vā.
5 | More particles; Agastya's offering | Vā, aha, ha, u, hi, kila, mā, khalu, śaśvat and nūnam in their senses. Agastya, having set out an oblation for Indra, meant to give it to the Maruts; Indra came and complained.
6 | Indra's complaint explained | Indra's verse (Ṛgveda 1.170.1), "There is nothing today, nothing tomorrow; who knows what is strange? …", explained word by word, with nūnam as a filler.
7 | Nūnam as a filler; sīm; tva | A verse with nūnam as a filler (Ṛgveda 2.11.21) is explained: dakṣiṇā, magha, hasta, bhaga, vīra. Sīm as "all round" or as a filler, or as a limit; tva ("one, another") as a pronoun.
8 | The four priests; tva | Ṛgveda 10.71.11 on the offices of the four priests, hotṛ, udgātṛ, brahman and adhvaryu, with the derivation of each. Some take tva for a particle; Yāska shows it declined like a noun.
9 | Tva in the plural; particles as fillers | Ṛgveda 10.71.7: friends with eyes and ears, yet unequal in quickness of mind, some like pools reaching the mouth, some the armpit. Akṣi, karṇa, āsya, dadhna and hrada explained. In metrical texts some particles only fill out the line: kam, īm, id, u.
10 | The fillers shown in use | Kam, īm, id and u shown as fillers in quotations, and iva as well. Na joined with id expresses fear.
11 | Na in fear; naraka | A verse on those who fear falling into hell (naraka), with naraka explained; na cet in a question. So the particles fall in various senses; the four kinds of words are done.
12 | Are all nouns derived from verbs? | Śākaṭāyana and the etymologists hold that all nouns come from verbs; Gārgya and some grammarians, that not all do, only those whose accent and form fit a derivation. Objections: then everyone who does an act would bear its name, and a thing would have as many names as it has acts.
13 | Further objections | People would name things by the most fitting derivation; derivations are made up after the word exists (pṛthivī "because it was spread out", but who spread it?); Śākaṭāyana made words out of pieces of other words; and a thing comes before its action.
14 | The objections answered | Each objection answered in turn: names are given to some who share an act and not to others (a carpenter, a wandering ascetic), some derivatives are rare, and the faults of a particular etymologist are not faults of the science.
15 | Is a mantra meaningful? Kautsa's case | Without this science the meaning of the mantras is not understood; it completes grammar. Kautsa holds the mantras meaningless: their words and order are fixed, the Brāhmaṇa supplies their sense, some make no sense or contradict each other, they tell a hearer what he already knows, and some are obscure.
16 | The answer to Kautsa | The mantras have meaning, for their words are those of ordinary speech, and the Brāhmaṇa itself prizes a rite whose form matches its mantra. Each of Kautsa's points has a parallel in ordinary speech, and obscurity is the hearer's fault: it is not the post's fault if the blind man does not see it.
17 | The division of words, deities, and knowledge | Without the Nirukta the words of a text cannot be divided (avasāya, ava sāya); the continuous text rests on the separate words; deities are known by the marks of the mantras. Knowledge is praised and ignorance blamed.
18 | The verses on knowing the meaning | Verses: one who learns the Veda without its meaning is a post, a mere bearer of a load; what is learnt without understanding never blazes, like dry fuel without fire. Sthāṇu and artha explained.
19 | Speech reveals herself to the one who understands | Ṛgveda 10.71.4: one sees and does not see speech, hears and does not hear her; to another she shows herself as a wife to her husband. The praise of the one who knows the meaning.
20 | The seers, the word-list, and the deities | Ṛgveda 10.71.5 on one who has heard speech without fruit or flower. The seers saw dharma directly and handed the mantras on; later men, flagging in that teaching, compiled this book and the Vedāṅgas. Words that come incidentally in a mantra of another deity (aśvaṃ na tvā vāravantam, mṛgo na bhīmaḥ) belong to the word-lists; the names of the deities chiefly praised make up the daivata, to be explained later.
