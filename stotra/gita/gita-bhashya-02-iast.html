Bhagavad Gītā · Chapter 2

Sāṅkhya Yoga · the yoga of knowledge · with Śaṅkara's bhāṣya

Kṛṣṇa answers Arjuna's grief with the teaching of the undying Self and the discipline of action without attachment, and closes with the portrait of the sage of steady wisdom (sthitaprajña). Śaṅkara's commentary begins at 2.10–11 with a long introduction on the two paths, knowledge and action, which sets the frame for the whole bhāṣya.

bhāṣya

atra “dṛṣṭvā tu pāṇḍavānīkam” (1.2) iti ārabhya yāvat na yotsya iti govindamuktvā tūṣṇīṃ babhūva ha” (2.9) ityetadantaḥ prāṇināṃ śokamohādi saṃsārabījabhūtadoṣodbhava kāraṇa pradarśanārthatvena vyākhyeyaḥ granthaḥ. tathā hi – arjunena rājyaguruputramitrasuhṛtsvajana sambandhibāndaveṣu “aham eṣām” “mama ete” ityevaṃ bhrāntipratyayanimittasnehavicchedādi nimittau ātmanaḥ śokamohau pradarśitau – “kathaṃ bhīṣmamahaṃ saṅkhye” (2.4) ityādinā. śokamohābhyāṃ hi abhibhūtavivekavijñānaḥ svata eva kṣattradharme yuddhe pravṛtto'pi tasmāt yuddhāt upararāma. paradharmaṃ ca bhikṣājīvanādikaṃ kartuṃ pravavṛte. tathā ca sarvaprāṇināṃ śokamohādi doṣāviṣṭa cetasāṃ svabhāvataḥ eva svadharmaparityāgaḥ pratiṣiddha sevā ca syāt. svadharme pravṛttānāmapi teṣāṃ vāñmanaḥ kāyādīnāṃ pravṛttiḥ phalābhisandhipūrvikā eva sāhaṅkārā ca bhavati. tatra evaṃ sati dharmādharmopacayāt iṣṭāniṣṭajanma sukhaduḥkhaprāptilakṣaṇaḥ saṃsāraḥ anuparataḥ bhavati. ityataḥ saṃsāra bījabhūtau śokamohau. tayośca sarvakarmasannyāsapūrvakāt ātmajñānāt nānyataḥ nivṛttiḥ iti tat upadidikṣuḥ sarvalokānugrahārtham arjunaṃ nimittīkṛtya āha bhagavān vāsudevaḥ – aśocyānityādi.

Here the text from 'Seeing the army of the Pāṇḍavas' (1.2) up to 'saying "I will not fight" to Govinda, he fell silent' (2.9) is to be explained as showing the cause of the arising of grief, delusion and the other faults that are the seed of saṃsāra. For in the passage beginning 'How shall I fight Bhīṣma in battle?' (2.4) Arjuna displays his own grief and delusion, which arise from affection and the fear of separation, occasioned by the mistaken notions 'I am theirs' and 'they are mine' regarding kingdom, teachers, sons, friends, well-wishers, kinsmen, relations and connections. Overpowered by grief and delusion, his discrimination and understanding clouded, he withdrew from battle, though he had entered upon it of his own accord as the dharma of a kṣatriya, and set about taking up another's dharma, such as living by alms. So too all living beings whose minds are possessed by grief, delusion and the other faults naturally abandon their own dharma and resort to what is forbidden; and even when they engage in their own dharma, the activity of their speech, mind and body is directed to its fruit and accompanied by egoism. This being so, merit and demerit accumulate, and saṃsāra — birth in desirable and undesirable states, the experience of pleasure and pain — never ceases. Grief and delusion, then, are the seed of saṃsāra, and they cease through nothing but the knowledge of the Self preceded by the renunciation of all action. Wishing to teach this for the good of all the world, the Lord Vāsudeva, taking Arjuna as the occasion, spoke the verse beginning 'You grieve for those who should not be grieved for'.

atra kecit āhuḥ – sarvakarmasannyāsapūrvakāt ātmajñānaniṣṭhāmātrāt eva kevalāt kaivalyaṃ na prāpyate eva. kiṃ tarhi? agnihotrādi śrautasmārta karma sahitāt jñānāt kaivalyaprāptiḥ iti sarvāsu gītāsu niścitaḥ arthaḥ iti. jñāpakaṃ ca āhuḥ asya arthasya– “atha cettvamimaṃ dharmyaṃ saṅgrāmaṃ na kariṣyasi” (2.33), “karmaṇyevādhikāraste” (2.47), “kuru karmaiva tasmāttvam” (4.15) ityādi.hiṃsādi yuktatvāt vaidika karma adharmāya iti iyam api āśaṅkā na kāryā. katham? kṣāttraṃ karma yuddhalakṣaṇaṃ gurubhrātṛputrādi hiṃsālakṣaṇaṃ atyantakrūram api svadharmaḥ iti kṛtvā na adharmāya; tadakaraṇe ca – “tataḥ svadharmaṃ kīrtiṃ ca hitvā pāpamavāpsyasi” (2.33). iti bruvatā yāvajjīvādi śruti coditānāṃ paśvādihiṃsālakṣaṇānāṃ ca karmaṇāṃ prāgeva na adharmatvamiti suniścitaṃ uktaṃ bhavati – iti.

Here some say: liberation is not attained through steadfastness in the knowledge of the Self alone, preceded by the renunciation of all action. What then? The settled meaning of the whole Gītā, they say, is that liberation comes from knowledge combined with the rites of śruti and smṛti, such as the agnihotra. As evidence they cite: 'But if you will not fight this righteous battle' (2.33), 'Your right is to action alone' (2.47), 'Therefore perform action' (4.15) and so on. Nor, they say, should one suspect that Vedic rites lead to adharma because they involve harm and the like. How so? Because the Lord says that the kṣatriya's action, war, though extremely cruel in that it involves killing teachers, brothers, sons and others, is not adharma since it is his own dharma, and that by not doing it 'abandoning your own dharma and honour, you will incur sin' (2.33). By saying this he has declared all the more firmly that the rites enjoined by śruti for life and the like, even those involving the killing of animals, are not adharma.

tat asat; jñānakarmaniṣṭhayoḥ vibhāgavacanāt buddhidvayāśrayayoḥ. “aśocyān” (2.11) ityādinā bhagavatā yāvat “svadharmamapi cāvekṣya” (2.31) ityetadantena granthena yatparamārthātmatattva nirūpaṇaṃ kṛtam, tat sāṅkhyam. tadviṣayā buddhiḥ, ātmanaḥ janmādi ṣaḍvikriyābhāvāt akartā ātmā iti prakaraṇārthanirūpaṇāt yā jāyate, sā sāṅkhyabuddhiḥ. sā yeṣāṃ jñānināṃ ucitā bhavati, te sāṅkhyāḥ, etasyāḥ buddheḥ janmanaḥ prāk ātmanaḥ dehādi vyatiriktatvakartṛttvabhoktṛttvādyapekṣaḥ dharmādharmaviveka pūrvakaḥ mokṣasādhanānuṣṭhānalakṣaṇaḥ yogaḥ tadviṣayā buddhiḥ, yogabuddhiḥ. sā yeṣāṃ karmiṇāṃ ucitā bhavati te yoginaḥ. tathā ca bhagavatā vibhakte dve buddhī nirdiṣṭe “eṣā te'bhihitā sāṅkhye buddhiryoge tvimāṃ śṛṇu” (2.39) iti. tayośca sāṅkhyabuddhyāśrayāṃ jñānayogena niṣṭhāṃ sāṅkhyānāṃ vibhaktāṃ vakṣyati – “purāvedātmanā mayā proktā” (3.3) iti. tathā ca yogabuddhyāśrayāṃ karmayogena niṣṭhāṃ vibhaktāṃ vakṣyati – “karmayogena yoginām” (3.3) iti. evaṃ sāṅkhyabuddhiṃ yogabuddhiṃ ca āśritya dve niṣṭhe vibhakte bhagavatā eva ukte jñānakarmaṇoḥ kartṛtvākartṛtvaikatvānekatva buddhyāśrayayoḥ yugapat ekapuruṣāśrayatvāsambhavaṃ paśyatā.

That is wrong, because the two steadfastnesses, of knowledge and of action, resting on two different understandings, are declared separately. The exposition of the truth of the supreme Self that the Lord gives from 'You grieve for those who should not be grieved for' (2.11) up to 'Considering also your own dharma' (2.31) is Sāṅkhya. The understanding concerned with it — which arises from ascertaining the meaning of that section, that the Self is not an agent because it is free from the six modifications beginning with birth — is the Sāṅkhya understanding, and those wise men to whom it is suited are the Sāṅkhyas. Before this understanding arises, there is Yoga: the practice of the means to liberation, preceded by discrimination between dharma and adharma and presupposing that the self is distinct from the body and is an agent and an experiencer. The understanding concerned with that is the yoga-understanding, and those men of action to whom it is suited are the yogins. Thus the Lord has pointed out two distinct understandings: 'This understanding has been set out for you according to Sāṅkhya; now hear it according to Yoga' (2.39). And of these he will speak of the steadfastness of the Sāṅkhyas through the yoga of knowledge, resting on the Sāṅkhya understanding, as separate: 'taught by me of old' (3.3); and likewise of the steadfastness of the yogins through the yoga of action, resting on the yoga-understanding, as separate: 'through the yoga of action for the yogins' (3.3). So the Lord himself has declared two separate steadfastnesses, resting on the Sāṅkhya and the yoga understandings, seeing that knowledge and action, which rest on the understandings of non-agency and agency, of oneness and manyness, cannot belong to one person at the same time.

yathā etadvibhāga vacanaṃ tathaiva darśitaṃ śātapathīyabrāhmaṇe – “etameva pravrājino lokamicchanto brāhmaṇāḥ pravrajanti” iti sarvakarmasannyāsaṃ vidhāya taccheṣeṇa – “kiṃ prajayā kariṣyāmo yeṣāṃ no'yamātmā'yaṃ lokaḥ” (bṛ.u.4.4.22) iti. tatraiva ca prāg dāraparigrahāt “puruṣaḥ” “ātmā” prākṛtaḥ dharmajijñāsottarakālaṃ lokatrayasādhanaṃ – putraṃ, dviprakāraṃ ca vittaṃ – “mānuṣaṃ”, “daivaṃ ca” ; tatra mānuṣaṃ karmarūpaṃ pitṛlokaprāptisādhanaṃ, vidyāṃ ca daivaṃ vittaṃ devalokaprāptisādhanaṃ “sokāmayata” iti avidyākāmavataḥ eva sarvāṇi karmāṇi śrautādīni darśitāni. tebhyaḥ (vyutthāya, pravrajanti iti) vyutthānam ātmānam eva lokam icchataḥ akāmasya vihitam, tadetadvibhāgavacanam anupapannaṃ syāt, yadi śrautakarmajñānayoḥ samuccayaḥ abhipretaḥ syāt bhagavataḥ.

This separation is shown in the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa too. Having enjoined the renunciation of all action — 'Desiring this very world, the Self, brāhmaṇas renounce and wander forth' — it adds by way of explanation: 'What shall we do with offspring, we whose world is this Self?' (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.22). And there too it is shown that before marriage the natural man, the 'self', after the enquiry into dharma, desires the means to the three worlds — a son, and wealth of two kinds, human and divine; of these the human wealth, ritual action, is the means of reaching the world of the ancestors, and the divine wealth, knowledge, is the means of reaching the world of the gods — 'he desired'; so all the rites of śruti and the rest are shown to belong only to one who has ignorance and desire. Rising above them — 'they rise and wander forth' — is enjoined for one without desire, who desires the Self alone as his world. This teaching of separation would make no sense if the Lord had intended a combination of Vedic ritual and knowledge.

na ca arjunasya praśnaḥ upapannaḥ bhavati “jyāyasī cetkarmaṇaste” (3.1) ityādiḥ. eka puruṣānuṣṭheyatvāsambhavaṃ buddhikarmaṇoḥ bhagavatā pūrvaṃ anuktaṃ kathaṃ arjunaḥ aśrutaṃ buddheśca karmaṇaḥ jyāyastvaṃ bhagavati adhyāropayet mṛṣaiva “jyāyasī cetkarmaṇaste matā buddhiḥ” (3.1) iti.

Nor would Arjuna's question, 'If you hold that understanding is superior to action' (3.1), make sense. If the Lord had not earlier said that understanding and action cannot be practised by one person, how could Arjuna falsely attribute to him what he had not heard — the superiority of understanding to action — saying, 'If you hold that understanding is superior to action'?

kiñca – yadi buddhikarmaṇoḥ sarveṣāṃ samuccayaḥ uktaḥ syāt, arjunasyāpi saḥ uktaḥ eva iti, “yacchreya etayorekaṃ tanme brūhi suniścitam” (5.1) iti kathamubhayoḥ upadeśe sati anyataraviṣayaḥ eva praśnaḥ syāt? na hi pittapraśamanārthinaḥ vaidyena madhuraṃ śītaṃ ca bhoktavyam iti upadiṣṭe “tayoranyataratpittapraśamanakāraṇaṃ brūhi” iti praśnaḥ sambhavati. atha arjunasya bhagavaduktavacanārthavivekānavadhāraṇanimittaḥ praśnaḥ kalpyate, tathāpi bhagavatā praśnānurūpaṃ prativacanaṃ deyaṃ, “mayā buddhikarmaṇoḥ samuccayaḥ uktaḥ, kimartham itthaṃ tvaṃ bhrānto'si” iti. na tu punaḥ prativacanamananurūpampṛṣṭāt anyat eva “dve niṣṭhe mayā purā prokte” (3.3) iti vaktuṃ yuktam.

Moreover, if the combination of understanding and action had been taught for everyone, then it was taught to Arjuna as well; and when both had been taught, how could he ask about one of them only: 'Tell me for certain which one of these two is better' (5.1)? When a physician has told a patient who wants to cure his bile that he should eat what is sweet and cool, the patient does not ask, 'Tell me which one of the two cures bile'. And if it is supposed that Arjuna asked because he had not grasped the meaning of the Lord's words, even then the Lord should have given an answer fitting the question: 'I have taught a combination of understanding and action; why are you confused like this?' It would not be right to give an answer that does not fit, about something other than what was asked: 'The twofold steadfastness was taught by me of old' (3.3).

nāpi smārtenaiva karmaṇā buddheḥ samuccaye'bhiprete vibhāgavacanādi sarvam upapannam kiñca kṣatriyasya yuddhaṃ smārtaṃ karma svadharma iti jānataḥ “tat kiṃ karmaṇi ghore māṃ niyojayasi” (3.1) iti upālambhaḥ anupapannaḥ.

Nor does everything — the teaching of separation and the rest — make sense if a combination of understanding with smṛti ritual alone is intended. Moreover, Arjuna knew that war is the smṛti duty of a kṣatriya, his own dharma, and so his complaint, 'Why then do you urge me into this terrible deed?' (3.1), would make no sense.

tasmād gītāśāstre īṣanmātreṇāpi śrautena smārtena vā karmaṇā ātmajñānasya samuccayaḥ na kenaciddarśayituṃ śakyaḥ.

Therefore no one can show in the Gītā even the slightest combination of the knowledge of the Self with ritual action, whether of śruti or of smṛti.

yasya tu ajñānādrāgādi doṣato vā karmaṇi pravṛttasya yajñena, dānena, tapasā vā viśuddhasattvasya jñānam utpannaṃ paramārthatattva viṣayaṃ “ekamevedaṃ sarvaṃ brahma akartṛ ca” iti, tasya karmaṇi karmaprayojane ca nivṛtte'pi lokasaṅgrahārthaṃ yatnapūrvaṃ yathā pravṛttiḥ tathaiva karmaṇi pravṛttasya yatpravṛtti rūpaṃ dṛśyate na tat karma yena buddheḥ samuccayaḥ syāt, yathā bhagavataḥ vāsudevasya kṣātradharmaceṣṭitaṃ na jñānena samuccīyate puruṣārthasiddhaye; tadvat tatphalābhisandhyahaṅkārābhāvasya tulyatvāt viduṣaḥ. tattvavittu “nāhaṃ karomī”ti manyate, na ca tatphalam abhisandhatte. yathā ca svargādi kāmārthinaḥ agnihotrādi kāmasādhanānuṣṭhānāya āhitāgneḥ kāmye eva agnihotrādau pravṛttasya sāmikṛte vinaṣṭe'pi kāme tadeva agnihotrādi anutiṣṭhato'pi na tat kāmyam agnihotrādi bhavati. tathā ca darśayati bhagavān “kurvannapi na lipyate” (5.7), “na karoti na lipyate” (13.31) iti tatra tatra. yacca “pūrvaiḥ pūrvataraṃ kṛtam” (4.15), “karmaṇaiva hi saṃsiddhimāsthitā janakādayaḥ” (3.20) iti, tattu pravibhajya vijñeyam. tatkatham? yadi tāvatpūrve janakādayaḥ tattvavidaḥ api pravṛttakarmāṇaḥ syuḥ, te lokasaṅgrahārthaṃ “guṇā guṇeṣu vartante” (3.28) iti jñānenaiva saṃsiddhiṃ āsthitāḥ. karmasannyāse prāpte'pi karmaṇā sahaiva saṃsiddhim āsthitāḥ na karmasannyāsaṃ kṛtavantaḥ ityarthaḥ. atha na te tattvavidaḥ – īśvarasamarpitena karmaṇā sādhanabhūtena saṃsiddhiṃ, sattvaśuddhiṃ, jñānotpatti lakṣaṇāṃ vā saṃsiddhim āsthitāḥ janakādayaḥ iti vyākhyeyam. etameva artham vakṣyati bhagavān “sattvaśuddhaye karma kurvanti” (5.11) iti. “sva karmaṇā tamabhyarcya siddhiṃ vindati mānavaḥ”. (18.46) ityuktvā siddhiṃ prāptasya ca punaḥ jñānaniṣṭhāṃ vakṣyati “siddhiṃ prāpto yathā brahma” (18.50) ityādinā. tasmāt gītāśāstre kevalāt eva tattvajñānāt mokṣaprāptiḥ, na karma samuccitāt iti niścitaḥ arthaḥ. yathā ca ayamarthaḥ tathā prakaraṇaśaḥ vibhajya tatra tatra darśayiṣyāmaḥ.

But consider one who took to action out of ignorance or through faults such as attachment, whose mind was then purified by sacrifice, gift or austerity, and in whom knowledge of the supreme truth arose — 'all this is Brahman alone, and it is not an agent'. For him action and the purpose of action have ceased; yet if, for the sake of maintaining the world, he continues to act with care just as before, what is seen as his activity is not action of a kind that could be combined with knowledge. Just so, the kṣatriya conduct of the Lord Vāsudeva is not combined with knowledge for the attainment of the human goal; and it is the same with the knower, since in him too the seeking of fruit and egoism are absent. The knower of truth thinks 'I do nothing' and does not seek the fruit. Again, when a man who desires heaven or the like has set up the sacred fires in order to perform the agnihotra and other rites as means to his desire, and has begun a desire-prompted agnihotra, then if the desire perishes when the rite is half done and he still carries on with that agnihotra, it is no longer a desire-prompted rite. So too the Lord shows here and there: 'though acting, he is not tainted' (5.7), 'he neither acts nor is tainted' (13.31). As for 'done by the ancients of old' (4.15) and 'Janaka and others attained perfection through action alone' (3.20), these must be understood by distinguishing. How? If Janaka and the others of old were knowers of truth who were engaged in action, they attained perfection through knowledge alone, with the understanding that 'the guṇas move among the guṇas' (3.28), for the sake of maintaining the world; though renunciation of action had come to them, they attained perfection while still acting, and did not renounce action — that is the sense. If on the other hand they were not knowers of truth, the verse must be explained as meaning that Janaka and others attained perfection — purity of mind, or the arising of knowledge — through action offered to the Lord as a means. The Lord will state this very meaning: 'they perform action for the purification of the self' (5.11). And having said 'worshipping him through his own duty, a man attains perfection' (18.46), he will go on to speak again of steadfastness in knowledge for one who has attained that perfection, in 'Learn from me how, having attained perfection, he attains Brahman' (18.50). Therefore the settled meaning of the Gītā is that liberation comes from the knowledge of truth alone, not from knowledge combined with action. How this is so we shall show section by section in each place.

Arjuna, then, his mind confused about dharma, holding false knowledge, sunk in a great ocean of grief, could be lifted out of it by nothing but the knowledge of the Self. Seeing this, the Lord Vāsudeva, wishing to raise him up, led him towards the knowledge of the Self and said:

'You grieve for those who should not be grieved for' and so on. Those who should not be grieved for are Bhīṣma, Droṇa and the others, because of their good conduct and because in their true nature they are eternal. You have grieved for them, thinking, 'They will die because of me; without them, what shall I do with the pleasures of kingdom?' And you speak the words of the wise, of men of understanding. So you show in yourself both folly and learning, which are contradictory, like a madman — that is the sense. For the wise, the knowers of the Self, do not grieve for those whose breath has gone, the dead, nor for those whose breath has not gone, the living. Paṇḍitas are those whose understanding (paṇḍā) is directed to the Self, as the śruti says: 'having done with scholarship' (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.5.1). You grieve for those who in truth are eternal and not to be grieved for; so you are deluded — that is the sense.

That is why cold and heat are mentioned apart from them. Because these contacts come and go, they are impermanent. So endure cold, heat and the rest; bear them patiently; do not feel elation or dejection at them — that is the meaning.

'The unreal has no being…' Of the unreal — of cold, heat and the rest together with their causes, which do not truly exist — there is no being, no existence. For cold, heat and the like with their causes, when examined by the means of knowledge, do not prove to be real things. They are modifications, and a modification is inconstant. The form of a pot and the like, examined by the eye, is unreal because it is not perceived apart from the clay; so every modification is unreal because it is not perceived apart from its cause. An effect such as a pot is unreal because it is not perceived before its origination or after its destruction; and the cause, clay and the like, is unreal too, because it is not perceived apart from its own cause. If it is objected that if these are unreal, everything would be non-existent — no: for everywhere two cognitions are found, the cognition 'real' (sat) and the cognition 'unreal'. That whose cognition does not falter is real; that whose cognition falters is unreal. The distinction between real and unreal thus depending on cognition, everyone everywhere finds two cognitions with a common locus — not like 'blue lotus', but as 'the pot is real', 'the cloth is real', 'the elephant is real', and so everywhere. Of these two cognitions, the cognition of the pot and so on falters, as has been shown; but the cognition 'real' does not. So the object of the cognition of the pot and so on is unreal, because it falters; but the object of the cognition 'real' is not, because it does not falter.

If it is said that when the pot is destroyed and the cognition of the pot falters, the cognition 'real' falters too — no, for the cognition 'real' is seen in cloth and the rest as well; it is a cognition of the qualifier only. If it is said that like the cognition 'real', the cognition 'pot' too is seen in another pot — no, for it is not seen in cloth and the rest. If it is said that the cognition 'real' is not seen either when the pot is destroyed — no, because the qualified thing is absent. The cognition 'real' has the qualifier for its object; when the qualified thing is absent the qualifier cannot apply, so what could it have for its object? But this is not because the cognition 'real' lacks an object.

ekādhikaraṇatvaṃ ghaṭādi viśeṣyābhāve na yuktam iti cet – na; “idam udakam” iti marīcyādau anyatarābhāve'pi sāmānādhikaraṇyadarśanāt. tasmāt dehādeḥ dvandvasya ca sakāraṇasya asato na vidyate bhāvaḥ iti. tathā sataḥ ca ātmanaḥ abhāvaḥ avidyamānatā na vidyate, sarvatra avyabhicārāt iti avocāma. evaṃ ātmānātmanoḥ sadasatoḥ ubhayoḥ api dṛṣṭaḥ upalabdhaḥ antaḥ nirṇayaḥ, sat sadeva asat asadeva iti tu anayoḥ yathoktayoḥ tattvadarśibhiḥ.

If it is said that a common locus is not proper when the qualified thing, the pot and so on, is absent — no, for in a mirage and the like one sees a common locus, 'this is water', even though one of the two is absent. Therefore of the unreal — the body and the rest, and the pairs of opposites, together with their causes — there is no being. And likewise of the real, the Self, there is no non-being, no non-existence, because, as we have said, it never falters anywhere. Thus the final truth about both, the Self and the non-self, the real and the unreal — that the real is only real and the unreal only unreal — has been seen, has been ascertained, by those who see the essence of things.

Those whose habit is to see that are tattvadarśins. The sense is: you too, following the vision of those who see the essence of things, give up grief and delusion, and endure the pairs of opposites — cold, heat and the rest, whether fixed or unfixed in character — having settled in your mind that this is a modification, unreal, appearing falsely like water in a mirage.

'Indestructible…' Indestructible means that whose nature is not to perish. The word 'tu' (but) serves to distinguish it from the unreal. Know that — understand it. What? 'Eternal' and 'indestructible' mean that the Self is untouched by destruction of either kind. Immeasurable means not to be delimited by the means of knowledge such as perception. But surely the Self is delimited by scripture, and before that by perception and the rest? No one first proceeds to determine objects without having known himself as 'I am so'. The Self is not unknown to anyone. Scripture, the final means of knowledge, is authoritative about the Self only in removing the superimposition of what is not its nature, not by making known something unknown. So says the śruti: 'the Brahman that is immediate and direct, the Self that is within all' (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.4.1). Since the Self is thus eternal and unchanging, fight — do not withdraw from battle — that is the meaning. War is not being enjoined here as a duty. Arjuna had already entered upon the battle, and now, held back by grief and delusion, sat silent. So the Lord merely removes what was obstructing his duty. 'Fight', therefore, is only a restatement, not an injunction.

How the Self is unchanging is told in the second mantra:

'It is never born…' It is not born, does not originate: the change in a thing marked by birth does not belong to the Self — that is the meaning. Nor does it die. 'Vā' is used in the sense of 'and': 'and it does not die' denies the last change, marked by destruction. The word 'ever' connects with each denial of change: it is never born, it never dies. Since this Self, having been — having undergone the act of coming into being — will not afterwards cease to be, therefore it does not die; for in the world he who, having been, will cease to be is said to die. From the words 'vā' and 'na' we also take: this Self does not, having not been, come to be, as the body does; therefore it is not born, for he who, having not been, comes to be is said to be born. The Self is not so, and therefore it is not born. Since this is so, it is unborn; and since it does not die, it is eternal.

yadyapi ādyantayoḥ vikriyayoḥ pratiṣedhe sarvāḥ vikriyāḥ pratiṣiddhāḥ bhavanti, tathāpi madhyabhāvinīnāṃ vikriyāṇāṃ svaśabdaiḥ eva tadarthaiḥ pratiṣedhaḥ kartavyaḥ iti anuktānām api yauvanādi samasta vikriyāṇāṃ pratiṣedhaḥ yathā syāt ityāha – śāśvataḥ ityādinā. śāśvataḥ iti apakṣayalakṣaṇā vikriyā pratiṣidhyate. śaśvat bhavaḥ śāśvataḥ. nāpakṣīyate svarūpeṇa, niravayavatvāt. nirguṇatvācca nāpi guṇakṣayeṇa apakṣayaḥ. apakṣayaviparītā api vṛddhilakṣaṇā vikriyā pratiṣidhyate purāṇaḥ iti. yaḥ hi avayavāgamena upacīyate saḥ vardhate, abhinavaḥ iti ca ucyate. ayaṃ tu ātmā niravayavatvāt purāpi navaḥ eva iti purāṇaḥ; na vardhate ityarthaḥ. tathā – na hanyate na vipariṇamyate, ityarthaḥ hanyamāne vipariṇamyamāne'pi śarīre. hantiḥ atra vipariṇāmārthaḥ draṣṭavyaḥ apunaruktatāyai. asmin mantre ṣaṭ bhāvavikārāḥ laukika vastu vikriyāḥ ātmani pratiṣidhyante. sarvaprakāravikriyā rahitaḥ ātmā iti vākyārthaḥ. yasmāt evaṃ tasmāt “ubhau tau na vijānītaḥ” (2.19) iti pūrveṇa mantreṇa asya sambandhaḥ.

Although when the first and last changes are denied all changes are denied, the intervening changes still need to be denied by their own words, and so, in order that all changes not mentioned, such as youth, may be denied, he says 'constant' and the rest. 'Constant' (śāśvata) denies the change marked by decline: śāśvata means what is always. It does not decline in its own form, since it has no parts; and since it has no qualities, it does not decline through the decline of qualities either. The opposite of decline, the change marked by growth, is denied by 'ancient' (purāṇa). What is augmented by the addition of parts grows and is called new; but this Self, having no parts, was new even of old (purā api nava) and so is purāṇa — it does not grow, that is the meaning. And 'it is not slain' means it does not change when the body is slain, that is, when the body changes; 'slay' must be taken here in the sense of change, so as to avoid repetition. In this mantra the six modifications of being, the changes of things in the world, are denied of the Self. The sense of the sentence is that the Self is free from every kind of change. Since this is so, this mantra is connected with the previous one: 'neither understands' (2.19).

'Who knows it to be indestructible…' Knows — understands — to be indestructible, free from the final change, destruction; eternal, free from transformation — this is the connection with 'who knows'. This one, described in the previous mantra as unborn, free from birth, and imperishable, free from decline — how, in what way, does that man, the knower, who is the subject here, slay, perform the act of slaying? Or how does he cause to slay, prompt a slayer? In no way does he slay, in no way does he cause anyone to slay: in both places the question is a denial, since a genuine question is impossible here. The reason — unchangingness — being the same, the Lord intends the purport of this section to be the denial of all action for the knower; the denial of slaying is given only as an example.

Seeing what special reason for the impossibility of action in the knower does the Lord deny actions in saying 'how can that man…'? Surely the unchangingness of the Self has been stated as that special reason for the impossibility of all action? True, it has been stated; but it is not the special reason, because the knower is other than the unchanging Self. One who knows an unchanging post is not thereby incapable of action — if that is the objection, no: for the knower is the Self. Knowership does not belong to the aggregate of body and the rest. So, by elimination, the knower is the Self, which is not a composite and is unchanging; and since action is impossible for that knower, the denial 'how can that man…' is proper.

yathā buddhyādyāhṛtasya śabdādyarthasya avikriyaḥ eva san buddhivṛttyavivekavijñānena avidyayā upalabdhā ātmā kalpyate. evaṃ eva ātmānātmavivekajñānena buddhivṛttyā vidyayā – asatyarūpayā eva paramārthataḥ avikriyaḥ eva ātmā vidvān ucyate. viduṣaḥ karmāsambhavavacanāt yāni karmāṇi śāstreṇa vidhīyante tāni aviduṣaḥ vihitāni iti bhagavataḥ niścayo'vagamyate.

Just as the Self, while remaining unchanging, is imagined through ignorance — through the non-discrimination of the modifications of the intellect — to be the perceiver of sound and the other objects brought to it by the intellect and the rest, so the Self, while in truth remaining unchanging, is called a knower through knowledge, a modification of the intellect that discriminates between Self and non-self — which itself is of an unreal nature. From the statement that action is impossible for the knower, it is understood to be the Lord's conclusion that the actions enjoined by scripture are enjoined for one who does not know.

Objection: knowledge too is enjoined only for one who does not know, since enjoining knowledge on one who already knows would be pointless, like grinding flour already ground. So the distinction that actions are enjoined for the ignorant and not for the knower does not hold. Reply: no, for there is a difference in whether something remains to be done. After the meaning of the injunction of the agnihotra and the like has been understood, the agnihotra must be performed, preceded by the gathering of many requisites, by one who does not know, who has the notion 'I am the agent, this is my duty'. But after the meaning of the teaching about the nature of the Self — 'it is never born' and so on — has been understood, nothing remains to be done in the same way; nothing arises but the knowledge of the oneness and non-agency of the Self: 'I am not the agent, I am not the experiencer'. This is the difference. He, on the other hand, who knows himself as 'I am the agent' will necessarily have the notion 'this is my duty'; with reference to that he is qualified, and so actions apply to him. And he is one who does not know, according to the words 'neither understands' (2.19); while the knower who is distinguished from him is the one of whom action is denied: 'how can that man…' (2.21). Therefore the knower so distinguished, who sees the unchanging Self, and the seeker of liberation, are qualified only for the renunciation of all action. For this very reason the Lord Nārāyaṇa distinguishes the Sāṅkhyas, who know, from the men of action, who do not, and assigns them two steadfastnesses: 'the yoga of knowledge for the Sāṅkhyas, the yoga of action for the yogins' (3.3). So too the Lord Vyāsa said to his son, 'These are the two paths' (Mahābhārata, Śānti 240.6), and 'first the path of action, and afterwards renunciation' (Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 10.62.12, in substance). The Lord will show this same division again and again: 'one deluded by egoism, not knowing the truth, thinks "I am the doer"', 'but the knower of truth… "I do nothing"' (3.27–28); and 'renouncing all actions with the mind, he dwells' (5.13) and so on.

tatra kecit paṇḍitammanyāḥ vadanti – janmādiṣaḍbhāvavikriyārahitaḥ, avikriyaḥ akartā, ekaḥ, aham ātmā iti na kasyacit jñānam utpadyate. yasmin sati sarvakarmasannyāsaḥ upadiśyate, iti. tat na; “na jāyate” ityādi śāstropadeśānarthakyāt. yathā ca śāstropadeśa sāmarthyāt dharmādharmāstitva vijñānaṃ kartuśca dehāntara sambandhavijñānaṃ ca utpadyate, tathā śāstrāt tasyaiva ātmanaḥ avikriyatvākartṛtvaikatvādi vijñānaṃ kasmāt na utpadyate iti praṣṭavyāḥ te. karaṇāgocaratvāt iti cet – na, “manasaivānudraṣṭavyam” (bṛ.u.4.4.19) iti śruteḥ. śāstrācāryopadeśa śamadamādisaṃskṛtaṃ manaḥ ātmadarśane karaṇam. tathā ca tadadhigamāya anumāne āgame ca sati jñānaṃ notpadyate iti sāhasam etat.

Here some who think themselves learned say: no one ever has the knowledge 'I am the Self, one, free from the six modifications beginning with birth, unchanging, not an agent' — the knowledge on whose arising the renunciation of all action is taught. That is wrong, for then scriptural teachings such as 'it is never born' would be pointless. They should be asked: just as by the power of scripture's teaching there arises the knowledge that merit and demerit exist and that the agent is connected with another body, why should there not arise from scripture the knowledge that this same Self is unchanging, not an agent, one and so on? If they say it is beyond the reach of the instruments of knowledge — no, for the śruti says 'it is to be seen by the mind alone' (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.19). The mind refined by the teaching of scripture and teacher and by calm, self-control and the rest is the instrument for seeing the Self. And when inference and scripture are available for knowing it, to say that knowledge does not arise is mere rashness.

jñānaṃ ca utpadyamānaṃ tadviparītaṃ ajñānaṃ avaśyaṃ bādhate iti abhyupagantavyam. tat ca ajñānaṃ darśitaṃ “hantā ahaṃ, hataḥ asmi” iti, “ubhau tau na vijānītaḥ” (2.19) iti. atra ca ātmanaḥ hananakriyāyāḥ kartṛtvaṃ, karmatvaṃ, hetukartṛtvaṃ ca ajñānakṛtaṃ darśitam. tat ca sarvakriyāsu api samānaṃ kartṛtvādeḥ avidyākṛtatvaṃ, avikriyatvāt ātmanaḥ. vikriyāvān hi kartā ātmanaḥ karmabhūtaṃ anyaṃ prayojayati “kuru” iti – tat etat aviśeṣeṇa viduṣaḥ sarvakriyāsu kartṛtvaṃ hetukartṛtvaṃ ca pratiṣedhati bhagavān vāsudevaḥ viduṣaḥ karmādhikārābhāvapradarśanārthaṃ “vedāvināśinaṃ ... kathaṃ sa puruṣaḥ” ityādinā.

It must also be accepted that knowledge, when it arises, necessarily removes the ignorance opposed to it. That ignorance has been shown in 'I am the slayer, I am slain' and in 'neither understands' (2.19). Here the agency of the Self in the act of slaying, its being the object and its being the prompter are shown to be the work of ignorance. The same holds for all actions: agency and the rest are produced by ignorance, because the Self is unchanging. For it is a changeable agent that prompts another, the object of his action, saying 'do this'. The Lord Vāsudeva therefore denies to the knower, without distinction, agency and causal agency in all actions, in order to show that the knower is not qualified for action, in the passage 'who knows it to be indestructible… how can that man…'.

kva punaḥ viduṣaḥ adhikāraḥ iti? etat uktaṃ pūrvameva “jñānayogena sāṅkhyānām” (3.3) iti. tathā ca sarvakarmasannyāsaṃ vakṣyati “sarvakarmāṇi manasā” (5.13) ityādinā. nanu “manasā” iti vacanāt na vācikānāṃ kāyikānāṃ ca sannyāsaḥ iti cet – na, sarvakarmāṇi iti viśeṣitatvāt. mānasānāṃ eva sarvakarmaṇāṃ iti cet – na, manovyāpāra pūrvakatvāt vākkāyavyāpārāṇāṃ, manovyāpārābhāve tadanupapatteḥ, śāstrīyāṇāṃ vākkāyakarmaṇāṃ kāraṇāni mānasāni karmāṇi varjayitvā anyāni sarvakarmāṇi manasā sannyasya iti cet–na, “naiva kurvannakārayan” (5.13) iti viśeṣaṇāt. sarvakarmasannyāsaḥ ayaṃ bhagavatā uktaḥ mariṣyataḥ, na jīvataḥ iti cet – na, “navadvāre pure dehī ... āste” (5.13) iti viśeṣaṇānupapatteḥ. na hi sarvakarmasannyāsena mṛtasya taddehe āsanaṃ sambhavati. akurvataḥ akārayataśca dehe sannyasya iti sambandhaḥ, na dehe āste iti cet – na, sarvatra ātmanaḥ avikriyatvāvadhāraṇāt, āsanakriyāyāḥ ca adhikaraṇāpekṣatvāt, tadanapekṣatvāt ca sannyāsasya. sampūrvastu nyāsaśabdaḥ atra tyāgārthaḥ, na nikṣepārthaḥ. tasmāt gītāśāstre ātmajñānavataḥ sannyāsa eva adhikāraḥ, na karmaṇi iti tatra tatra upariṣṭāt ātmajñāna prakaraṇe darśayiṣyāmaḥ.

For what, then, is the knower qualified? That has already been stated: 'the yoga of knowledge for the Sāṅkhyas' (3.3). And he will speak of the renunciation of all actions in 'renouncing all actions with the mind' (5.13) and so on. If it is said that because of the words 'with the mind' verbal and bodily actions are not renounced — no, because it is qualified by 'all actions'. If it is said: all actions of the mind only — no, because the activities of speech and body are preceded by the activity of the mind and cannot occur without it. If it is said: renouncing with the mind all actions other than the mental ones that cause the scripturally enjoined actions of speech and body — no, because of the qualification 'neither acting nor causing to act' (5.13). If it is said that this renunciation of all actions spoken of by the Lord is for one about to die, not for the living — no, for then the qualification 'the embodied one dwells in the city of nine gates' (5.13) would not fit; one who has died through renouncing all actions cannot dwell in that body. If it is said that the construction is 'renouncing in the body, not acting and not causing to act', and not 'he dwells in the body' — no, because everywhere the Self is determined to be unchanging, because the act of dwelling requires a locus, and because renunciation does not. The word nyāsa with the prefix sam here means giving up, not depositing. Therefore in the Gītā the one who has the knowledge of the Self is qualified only for renunciation, not for action — this we shall show in each place further on, in the sections on the knowledge of the Self.

For throughout the scriptures on the Self, the very marks of one who has reached the goal are taught as the means, since they are to be achieved by effort; what are means to be achieved by effort are also the marks.

'When a man casts off…' He casts off — gives up completely — when, at the time when, all desires, every kind of wanting, Pārtha, that have entered the mind, the heart. When all desires are given up there is no cause for contentment, and only what is needed to sustain the body remains; so one might expect his conduct to be like that of a madman or a drunkard. Therefore it is said: content in the Self alone — in his own inmost nature — by the Self — by himself alone, independent of outer gains, finding sufficiency in nothing else, having gained the nectar of the vision of the supreme truth: then he, the knower, is called one of steady wisdom, whose wisdom born of the discrimination between Self and non-self is steady, established. That is, he who has given up the desires for sons, wealth and worlds, the renouncer who delights in the Self and plays in the Self, is one of steady wisdom. Moreover —

tasmāt therefore yasya whose mahā-bāho O mighty-armed nigṛhītāni are withdrawn sarvaśaḥ on every side indriyāṇi senses indriya-arthebhyaḥ from the objects of the senses tasya his prajñā wisdom pratiṣṭhitā is established

Therefore, mighty-armed one, he whose senses are withdrawn from their objects on every side — his wisdom is firmly established.

bhāṣya

“yatato hyapi” (2.60) ityupanyastasya arthasya anekadhā upapattiṃ uktvā taṃ ca arthaṃ upapādya upasaṃharati –

Having given many reasons for the point introduced in 'For the turbulent senses' (2.60) and established it, he concludes:

tasmāt iti || indriyāṇāṃ pravṛttau doṣaḥ upapāditaḥ yasmāt tasmāt yasya yateḥ he mahābāho! nigṛhītāni sarvaśaḥ sarvaprakāraiḥ mānasādibhedaiḥ indriyāṇi indriyārthebhyaḥ śabdādibhyaḥ tasya prajñā pratiṣṭhitā.

'Therefore…' Since the fault in the activity of the senses has been established, therefore, mighty-armed one, the ascetic whose senses are withdrawn on every side — in every way, mental and the rest — from their objects, sound and the rest, has wisdom established.

yā what niśā is night sarva-bhūtānām for all beings tasyām in that jāgarti is awake saṃyamī the self-restrained yasyām in which jāgrati are awake bhūtāni beings sā that niśā is night paśyataḥ who sees muneḥ for the sage

What is night for all beings, in that the self-controlled one is awake; and where beings are awake, that is night for the sage who sees.

bhāṣya

yo'yaṃ laukikaḥ vaidikaśca vyavahāraḥ saḥ utpannavivekajñānasya sthitaprajñasya avidyākāryatvāt avidyānivṛttau nivartate, avidyāyāḥ ca vidyāvirodhāt nivṛttiḥ ityetaṃ arthaṃ sphuṭīkurvan āha –

All dealings, worldly and Vedic, since they are products of ignorance, cease for one of steady wisdom in whom discriminating knowledge has arisen, when ignorance ceases; and ignorance ceases because it is opposed to knowledge. Making this point clear, he says:

yā niśā iti || yā niśā rātriḥ sarvapadārthānāṃ avivekakarī tamaḥ svabhāvatvāt sarvabhūtānāṃ sarveṣāṃ bhūtānām. kiṃ tat? paramārthatattvaṃ sthita prajñasya viṣayaḥ. yathā naktañcarāṇāṃ ahaḥ eva sat anyeṣāṃ niśā bhavati, tadvat naktañcarasthānīyānāṃ ajñā(ni)nāṃ sarvabhūtānāṃ niśā iva niśā paramārthatattvaṃ. agocaratvāt atadbuddhīnām. tasyāṃ paramārthatattvalakṣaṇāyāṃ ajñānanidrāyāḥ prabuddhaḥ jāgarti saṃyamī saṃyamavān, jitendriyaḥ yogī ityarthaḥ. yasyāṃ grāhyagrāhaka (bheda) lakṣaṇāyāṃ avidyānidrā(śā)yāṃ prasuptāni eva bhūtāni jāgrati iti ucyante(te), yasyāṃ niśāyāṃ prasuptā iva svapna dṛśaḥ, sā niśā avidyārūpatvāt paramārthatattvaṃ paśyataḥ muneḥ iti.

'What is night for all beings…' What is night, darkness, for all beings — making all things indiscernible because it is darkness by nature? It is the supreme truth, the object of one of steady wisdom. Just as what is day for night-roaming creatures is night for others, so the supreme truth is like night for all ignorant beings, who are like the night-roamers, because it lies beyond the reach of those whose understanding is not of it. In that, which is the supreme truth, the self-controlled one — the yogin who has mastered his senses — is awake, having woken from the sleep of ignorance. That in which beings are said to be awake — the night of ignorance marked by the division of perceiver and perceived, in which they are really asleep, like dreamers — that is night for the sage who sees the supreme truth, because it is of the nature of ignorance.

ataḥ karmāṇi avidyāvasthāyāṃ eva codyante, na vidyāvasthāyām. vidyāyāṃ hi satyāṃ udite savitari śārvaraṃ iva tamaḥ praṇāśaṃ upagacchati avidyā. prāk vidyotpatteḥ avidyā pramāṇabuddhyā gṛhyamāṇā kriyākārakaphalabhedarūpā ca satī sarvakarmahetutvaṃ pratipadyate. na apramāṇa buddhyā gṛhyamāṇāyāḥ karmahetutvopapattiḥ. “pramāṇabhūtena vedena mama coditaṃ kartavyaṃ karma”, iti hi karmaṇi kartā pravartate, na “avidyā mātramidaṃ sarvaṃ niśā iva” iti. yasya tu punaḥ “niśevāvidyāmātramidaṃ sarvaṃ bhedajātam” iti jñānaṃ tasyā''tmajñasya sarvakarmasannyāse evādhikāra, na pravṛttau. tathā ca darśayiṣyati “tadbuddhayastadātmānaḥ”(5.17) ityādinā jñānaniṣṭhāyāṃ eva tasya adhikāram.

Therefore actions are enjoined only in the state of ignorance, not in the state of knowledge. For when knowledge comes, ignorance is destroyed, as the darkness of night is when the sun rises. Before knowledge arises, ignorance, taken to be valid and appearing as the distinctions of action, factors of action and result, becomes the cause of all actions; and it could not be the cause of action if it were taken to be invalid. For the agent engages in action thinking 'this action has been enjoined for me by the Veda, which is authoritative', not thinking 'all this is mere ignorance, like night'. But he who knows 'all this multitude of distinctions is mere ignorance, like night' — that knower of the Self is qualified only for the renunciation of all action, not for engagement. And so he will show, in 'their understanding in That, their self in That' (5.17) and so on, that he is qualified only for steadfastness in knowledge.

tatrāpi pravartakapramāṇābhāve pravṛttyanupapattiḥ iti cet na, svātmaviṣayatvāt ātmavijñānasya. na hi ātmanaḥ svātmani pravartakapramāṇāpekṣā, ātmattvāt eva tadantattvāt ca sarva pramāṇānāṃ pramāṇatvasya. na hi ātma svarūpādhigame sati punaḥ pramāṇaprameyavyavahāraḥ sambhavati.pramātṛttvaṃ hi ātmanaḥ nivartayati antyaṃ pramāṇam. nivartayat eva ca apramāṇībhavati svapnakālapramāṇam iva prabodhe. loke ca vastvadhigame pravṛtti hetuttvādarśanātpramāṇasya. tasmāt na ātmavidaḥ karmaṇi adhikāraḥ iti siddham.

If it is objected that even there, in the absence of a valid means of knowledge to prompt it, no engagement is possible — no, for the knowledge of the Self has one's own Self for its object. The Self needs no prompting means of knowledge with regard to itself, both because it is the Self and because all means of knowledge are valid only up to it. When the nature of the Self has been realised, dealings involving knowledge and its objects are no longer possible. For the final means of knowledge removes the knowership of the Self; and in removing it, it ceases to be a means of knowledge, as the means of knowledge of a dream do on waking. Also, in the world a means of knowledge is not seen to prompt action once its object has been known. Therefore it is established that the knower of the Self is not qualified for action.

āpūryamāṇam ever being filled acala-pratiṣṭham unmoved in its ground samudram the ocean āpaḥ waters praviśanti enter yadvat as tadvat so kāmāḥ desires yam whom praviśanti enter sarve all saḥ he śāntim peace āpnoti attains na not kāma-kāmī one who desires desires

As the waters enter the ocean, which is ever being filled yet stays unmoved in its place, so he into whom all desires enter attains peace — not the desirer of desires.

bhāṣya

viduṣaḥ tyaktaiṣaṇasya sthitaprajñasya yateḥ eva mokṣaprāptiḥ na tu asannyāsinaḥ kāmakāminaḥ ityetam arthaṃ dṛṣṭāntena pratipādayiṣyan āha –

Intending to show by an illustration that liberation is attained only by the knower, the ascetic of steady wisdom who has given up all desires, and not by one who has not renounced and who desires desires, he says:

āpūrya iti. āpūryamāṇaṃ adbhiḥ acalapratiṣṭhaṃ acalatayā pratiṣṭhā avasthitiḥ yasya tam acalapratiṣṭhaṃ samudraṃ āpaḥ sarvato gatāḥ praviśanti svātmasthaṃ avikriyam eva santaṃ yadvat tadvatkāmāḥ viṣayasannidhau api sarvataḥ icchāviśeṣāḥ yaṃ puruṣaṃ (muniṃ), samudram iva āpaḥ, avikurvantaḥ praviśanti sarve ātmani eva pralīyante na svātmavaśaṃ kurvanti saḥ śāntiṃ mokṣaṃ āpnoti, na itaraḥ kāmakāmī kāmyante iti kāmāḥ viṣayāḥ tān kāmayituṃ śīlaṃ yasya sa kāmakāmī, saḥ naiva prāpnoti ityarthaḥ. yasmāt evaṃ tasmāt –

'As the waters enter the ocean…' Just as the waters coming from every side enter the ocean, which is being filled with water and is established immovably, while it remains unchanged in itself; so he into whom all desires — all kinds of wanting, even in the presence of objects — enter, like waters into the ocean, without causing any change; they dissolve in the Self and do not bring him under their sway — he attains peace, liberation. Not the other, the desirer of desires: kāmas are objects, because they are desired; one whose habit is to desire them is a desirer of desires; he does not attain it — that is the meaning. Since this is so —

vihāya abandoning kāmān desires yaḥ who sarvān all pumān the man carati moves about niḥspṛhaḥ free of longing nirmamaḥ without 'mine' nirahaṅkāraḥ without 'I' saḥ he śāntim peace adhigacchati attains

The man who gives up all desires and moves about free from longing, without the sense of 'mine', without the sense of 'I', attains peace.

bhāṣya

vihāya iti. vihāya parityajya kāmān yaḥ sannyāsī pumān sarvān aśeṣataḥ kārtsnyena carati jīvanamātraceṣṭāśeṣaḥ paryaṭati ityarthaḥ. nispṛhaḥ śarīrajīvana mātre'pi nirgatā spṛhā yasya saḥ nispṛhaḥ san nirmamaḥ śarīra jīvanamātrākṣiptaparigrahepi “mama idaṃ” iti abhiniveśavarjitaḥ, nirahaṅkāraḥ vidyāvatvādi nimittātmasambhāvanārahitaḥ ityetat. saḥ evambhūtaḥ sthitaprajñaḥ brahmavit śāntiṃ sarvasaṃsāraduḥkhoparamalakṣaṇāṃ nirvāṇākhyāṃ adhigacchati prāpnoti brahmabhūtaḥ bhavati ityarthaḥ. sā eṣā jñānaniṣṭhā stūyate –

'The man who gives up all desires…' The man, the renouncer, who gives up all desires without remainder, entirely, and moves about — wanders, with only the activity needed to stay alive left to him: that is the meaning — free from longing, without longing even for the mere sustenance of the body; without 'mine', free from clinging to 'this is mine' even in the possessions needed merely to sustain the body; without 'I', free from self-esteem on account of learning and the like. Such a one, of steady wisdom, a knower of Brahman, attains peace — the cessation of all the sorrow of saṃsāra, called nirvāṇa; he becomes Brahman — that is the meaning. This steadfastness in knowledge is praised:

eṣā this brāhmī of Brahman sthitiḥ the state pārtha O Pārtha na not enām this prāpya having attained vimuhyati is one deluded sthitvā abiding asyām in it anta-kāle at the time of the end api even brahma-nirvāṇam the peace of Brahman ṛcchati one reaches

This is the Brāhmī state, Pārtha; attaining it, one is no longer deluded. Remaining in it even at the hour of death, one attains the peace of Brahman (brahmanirvāṇa).

eṣā brāhmī iti. eṣā yathoktā brāhmī brahmaṇi bhavā iyaṃ sthitiḥ sarvaṃ karma sannyasya brahma svarūpeṇa eva avasthānaṃ ityetat. he pārtha! na enāṃ sthitiṃ prāpya labdhvā vimuhyati mohaṃ prāpnoti. sthitvā asyāṃ sthitau brāhmyāṃ yathoktāyāṃ antakālepi antye vayasyapi brahmanirvāṇaṃ brahmanirvṛtiṃ mokṣam ṛcchati gacchati. kimu vaktavyaṃ brahmacaryāt eva sannyasya yāvajjīvaṃ yaḥ brahmaṇi eva avatiṣṭhate saḥ brahmanirvāṇaṃ ṛcchati iti?

'This is the Brāhmī state…' This, as described, is the Brāhmī state, the state that is in Brahman: abiding as Brahman itself, having renounced all action. Pārtha, having attained this state, one is no longer deluded. Remaining in this Brāhmī state as described, even at the hour of death, even in the last part of life, one attains the peace of Brahman, liberation. What need be said, then, of one who renounces from the stage of studentship itself and abides in Brahman all his life — that he attains the peace of Brahman?

Thus, in the Bhagavad Gītā — the Upaniṣad, the knowledge of Brahman, the scripture of yoga, the dialogue of Śrī Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna — within the Bhīṣma Parva of the Mahābhārata, the collection of a hundred thousand verses by Vyāsa, ends the second chapter, Sāṅkhya Yoga.
