Bhagavad Gītā · Chapter 13

Kṣetrakṣetrajñavibhāga Yoga · the field and the knower of the field · with Śaṅkara's bhāṣya

The field (kṣetra) — the body with all it contains — and the knower of the field (kṣetrajña), whom Kṛṣṇa declares to be himself in all fields. The chapter sets out the qualities called knowledge, the object of knowledge (Brahman), and prakṛti and puruṣa. Śaṅkara's commentary on 13.2 is one of the longest and most closely argued passages of the bhāṣya. The book, like most editions of Śaṅkara's text, has 34 verses here (it does not include the extra opening verse some recensions carry).

bhāṣya · the chapter's opening

saptame adhyāye sūcite dve prakṛtī īśvarasya – triguṇātmikā aṣṭadhā bhinnā aparā saṃsārahetutvāt, parā ca anyā jīvabhūtā kṣetrajñalakṣaṇā īśvarātmikā, yābhyāṃ prakṛtibhyām īśvaraḥ jagadutpattisthitilayahetutvaṃ pratipadyate. tatra kṣetrakṣetrajñalakṣaṇaprakṛtidvaya nirūpaṇadvāreṇa tadvataḥ īśvarasya tattvanirdhāraṇārthaṃ kṣetrādhyāyaḥ ārabhyate. atītānantarādhyāye ca “adveṣṭā sarvabhūtānām” (12.13) ityādinā yāvadadhyāyaparisamāptiḥ tāvattattvajñānināṃ sannyāsināṃ niṣṭhā yathā te vartante ityetaduktam. kena punaḥ te tattvajñānena yuktāḥ yathoktadharmācaraṇād bhagavataḥ priyāḥ bhavanti ityevamarthaḥ ca ayam adhyāyaḥ ārabhyate – prakṛtiśca triguṇātmikā sarvakāryakaraṇaviṣayākāreṇa pariṇatā puruṣasya bhogāpavargārthakartavyatayā dehendriyādyākāreṇa saṃhanyate. so'yaṃ saṅghātaḥ idaṃ śarīram, tadetat śrī bhagavānuvāca.

In the seventh chapter two natures of the Lord were indicated: the lower, made of the three guṇas, divided eightfold, because it is the cause of saṃsāra; and the other, the higher, which has become the living being, consisting of the knower of the field, of the Lord's own nature — the two natures by which the Lord becomes the cause of the arising, continuance and dissolution of the world. Now the chapter on the field begins, to determine the reality of the Lord who possesses them, by examining the two natures, the field and the knower of the field. And in the preceding chapter, from 'without hatred towards all beings' (12.13) to its end, the steadfastness of renouncers who know the truth — how they live — was told. This chapter also begins to show by what knowledge of the truth they become dear to the Lord through practising the qualities described. Nature, made of the three guṇas, transformed into all effects, instruments and objects, is combined into the form of body, senses and the rest, so as to serve the puruṣa's enjoyment and liberation. This aggregate is this body; this the Blessed Lord stated:

śrī bhagavānuvāca

idaṃ śarīraṃ kaunteya kṣetramityabhidhīyate |
etadyo vetti taṃ prāhuḥ kṣetrajña iti tadvidaḥ
|| 1 ||

word by word · translation

idam this śarīram body kaunteya O son of Kuntī kṣetram the field iti thus abhidhīyate is called etat this yaḥ who vetti knows tam him prāhuḥ they call kṣetra-jñaḥ the knower of the field iti thus tat-vidaḥ those who know them

The Blessed Lord said: This body, son of Kuntī, is called the field; he who knows it is called the knower of the field by those who know them.

bhāṣya

idamiti – idamiti sarvanāmnā uktaṃ viśinaṣṭi “śarīram” iti. he kaunteya! kṣatatrāṇāt, kṣayāt, kṣaraṇāt, kṣetravadvā asmin karmaphalaniṣpatteḥ kṣetram iti. iti śabdaḥ evaṃ– śabdapadārthakaḥ. kṣetram iti evaṃ abhidhīyate, kathyate. etat śarīraṃ kṣetraṃ yaḥ vetti vijānāti āpādatalamastakaṃ jñānena viṣayīkaroti svābhāvikena aupadeśikena vā vedanena viṣayīkaroti vibhāgaśaḥ, taṃ veditāraṃ prāhuḥ kathayanti kṣetrajñaḥ iti. iti śabdaḥ evaṃśabdapadārthakaḥ eva pūrvavat. kṣetrajña iti evam āhuḥ. ke? tadvidaḥ tau kṣetrakṣetrajñau ye vidanti vijānanti te tadvidaḥ.

'This body, Kaunteya…' What is referred to by the pronoun 'this' he specifies as 'the body'. O Kaunteya, it is called the field — because it protects from decay, because it decays, because it wastes away, or because, as in a field, the fruit of action ripens in it. 'Iti' means 'thus': it is thus called, spoken of, as 'the field'. He who knows this body, the field — who makes it the object of his knowledge from the soles of the feet to the crown of the head, by natural knowledge or by knowledge gained through teaching, distinguishing its parts — him, the knower, they call the knower of the field. 'Iti' again means 'thus', as before: they call him thus, the knower of the field. Who? Those who know them — those who know the field and the knower of the field.

kṣetrajñaṃ cāpi māṃ viddhi sarvakṣetreṣu bhārata |
kṣetrakṣetrajñayorjñānaṃ yattad jñānaṃ mataṃ mama

word by word · translation

kṣetra-jñam the knower of the field ca and api also mām me viddhi know sarva-kṣetreṣu in all fields bhārata O Bhārata kṣetra-kṣetrajñayoḥ of the field and its knower jñānam knowledge yat which tat that jñānam is knowledge matam view mama my

Know me also as the knower of the field in all fields, O Bhārata. The knowledge of the field and of the knower of the field — that I hold to be knowledge.

bhāṣya

evaṃ kṣetrakṣetrajñau uktau. kim etāvanmātreṇa jñānena jñātavyau iti? na ityucyate –

Thus the field and the knower of the field have been described. Are they to be known only by this much knowledge? No, it is said:

kṣetrajñamiti – kṣetrajñaṃ yathoktalakṣaṇaṃ cāpi māṃ parameśvaram asaṃsāriṇaṃ, viddhi jānīhi. sarvakṣetreṣu yaḥ kṣetrajñaḥ brahmādistambaparyantānekakṣetropādhipravibhaktaḥ taṃ nirastasarvopādhibhedaṃ sadasadādiśabdapratyayāgocaraṃ viddhi ityabhiprāyaḥ. he bhārata! yasmāt kṣetrakṣetrajñeśvara'yāthātmya vyatirekeṇa na jñānagocaram anyat avaśiṣṭam asti tasmāt kṣetrakṣetrajñayoḥ jñeyabhūtayoḥ yat jñānaṃ kṣetrakṣetrajñau yena jñānena viṣayīkriyete tad jñānaṃ, samyag jñānam iti matam abhiprāyaḥ mama īśvarasya viṣṇoḥ.

'And know me too as the knower of the field…' Know me too, the supreme Lord, not subject to saṃsāra, as the knower of the field, as described. The knower of the field in all fields, seemingly divided by the adjuncts of the many fields from Brahmā down to a clump of grass — know him as free of all distinction of adjuncts, beyond the reach of words and notions such as being and non-being: that is the purport. O Bhārata, since apart from the true nature of the field, the knower of the field and the Lord nothing else remains to be known, therefore the knowledge of the field and the knower of the field, the objects to be known — the knowledge by which they are made known — that knowledge is right knowledge: this is the view of me, the Lord Viṣṇu.

nanu sarvakṣetreṣu eka eva īśvaraḥ na anyaḥ tadvyatiriktaḥ bhoktā vidyate cet, tataḥ īśvarasya saṃsāritvaṃ prāptam; īśvaravyatirekeṇa vā saṃsāriṇaḥ anyasya abhāvāt saṃsārābhāvaprasaṅgaḥ, tacca ubhayam aniṣṭam, bandhamokṣataddhetuśāstrānarthakyaprasaṅgāt, pratyakṣādi pramāṇavirodhācca. pratyakṣeṇa tāvat sukhaduḥkhataddhetulakṣaṇaḥ saṃsāraḥ upalabhyate. jagadvaicitryopa labdheḥ dharmādharmanimittaḥ saṃsāraḥ anumīyate. sarvam etat anupapannam ātmeśvaraikatve.

Objection: if in all fields there is one Lord alone, and no enjoyer other than him exists, then the Lord becomes subject to saṃsāra; or else, since there is no one in saṃsāra other than the Lord, it follows that there is no saṃsāra. Both are unacceptable: the scriptures about bondage, liberation and their causes would be pointless, and it would conflict with perception and the other means of knowledge. For saṃsāra, consisting of pleasure, pain and their causes, is perceived directly; and from the variety of the world, saṃsāra caused by merit and demerit is inferred. All this is impossible if the self and the Lord are one.

na, jñānājñānayoḥ anyatvena upapatteḥ – “dūramete viparīte viṣūcī avidyā yā ca vidyeti jñātā” (kaṭha.u.2.4). tathā ca tayoḥ vidyā'vidyayoḥ phalabhedopi viruddhaḥ nirdiṣṭaḥ – “śreyaśca preyaśca” (kaṭha.u.2.2) iti. vidyāviṣayaḥ śreyaḥ, preyastu avidyākāryam iti. tathā ca vyāsaḥ – “dvāvimāvatha panthānau” (śāṃ.pa.240.6) ityādi “imau dvāveva panthānau” ityādi ca. iha ca dve niṣṭhe ukte. avidyā ca kāryeṇa saha hātavyā iti śrutismṛtinyāyebhyaḥ avagamyate. śrutayaḥ tāvat – “iha cedavedīdatha satyamasti na cedihāvedīnmahatī vinaṣṭiḥ” (kena.u.2.5), “tamevaṃ vidvānamṛta iha bhavati nānyaḥ panthā vidyate'yanāya” (puruṣasūkte. tai.ā.3.12, śve.u.3.8), “vidvānna bibheti kutaścana” (tai.u.2.4) aviduṣaḥ tu – “atha tasya bhayaṃ bhavati” (tai.u.2.7), “avidyāyāmantare vartamānāḥ” (kaṭha.u.2.5), “brahma veda brahmaiva bhavati” (muṃ.u.3.2.9), “anyosāvanyo'hamasmīti na sa veda yathā paśurevaṃ sa devānām” (bṛha.u.1.4.10), ātmavit yaḥ – “saḥ idaṃ sarvaṃ bhavati”. (bṛ.u.1.4.10), “yadā carmavat” (śve.u.6.20) ityādyāḥ sahasraśaḥ. smṛtayaśca – “ajñānenāvṛtaṃ jñānaṃ tena muhyanti jantavaḥ” (5.15) “ihaiva tairjitaḥ sargo yeṣāṃ sāmye sthitaṃ manaḥ” (5.25), “samaṃ paśyan hi sarvatra” (13.28) ityādyāḥ.

No: this is explained by the difference between knowledge and ignorance. 'Widely apart and opposed are these two, ignorance and what is known as knowledge' (Kaṭha 2.4). And the difference of their fruits too is declared to be opposed: 'the good and the pleasant' (Kaṭha 2.2) — the good belongs to knowledge, the pleasant is the effect of ignorance. So too Vyāsa: 'these two paths' (Śāntiparvan 240.6) and 'these are the only two paths'. And here too two kinds of steadfastness have been taught. That ignorance is to be abandoned together with its effects is understood from śruti, smṛti and reason. The śrutis: 'if one knows it here, there is truth; if one does not know it here, great is the loss' (Kena 2.5); 'knowing him thus one becomes immortal here; there is no other path for going' (Puruṣasūkta, Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 3.12; Śvetāśvatara 3.8); 'the knower fears nothing' (Taittirīya 2.4), but of the ignorant, 'then he has fear' (Taittirīya 2.7); 'abiding in the midst of ignorance' (Kaṭha 2.5); 'he who knows Brahman becomes Brahman' (Muṇḍaka 3.2.9); 'he who thinks "he is one, I am another" does not know; he is like an animal to the gods' (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.4.10); he who knows the Self 'becomes all this' (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.4.10); 'when men roll up the sky like a hide' (Śvetāśvatara 6.20) — and thousands more. And the smṛtis: 'knowledge is covered by ignorance; by that creatures are deluded' (5.15), 'even here creation is conquered by those whose mind abides in sameness' (5.19), 'seeing the same everywhere' (13.28) and so on.

nyāyataśca – “sarpān kuśāgrāṇi tathodapānaṃ jñātvā manuṣyāḥ parivarjayanti | ajñānatastatra patanti kecid jñāne phalaṃ paśya yathā viśiṣṭam” (śāṃ.pa.201.16) tathā ca dehādiṣu ātmabuddhiḥ avidvān rāgadveṣādiprayuktaḥ dharmādharmānuṣṭhānakṛt “jāyate mriyate” ca ityavagamyate. dehādivyatiriktātmadarśinaḥ rāgadveṣādiprahāṇāt tadapekṣa dharmādharmapravṛttyupaśamanāt mucyante iti na kenacit pratyākhyātuṃ śakyaṃ nyāyataḥ. tatra evaṃ sati kṣetrajñasya īśvarasyaiva sataḥ avidyākṛtopādhibhedataḥ saṃsāritvamiva bhavati, yathā dehādyātmatvam ātmanaḥ. sarvajantūnāṃ hi prasiddhaḥ dehādiṣu anātmasu ātmabhāvaḥ niścitaḥ avidyākṛtaḥ, yathā sthāṇau puruṣaniścayaḥ. na ca etāvatā puruṣadharmaḥ sthāṇoḥ bhavati, sthāṇudharmo vā puruṣasya; tathā na caitanyadharmaḥ dehasya, dehadharmo vā cetanasya, evaṃ sukhaduḥkhamohātmakatvādiḥ ātmanaḥ na yuktaḥ avidyākṛtatvāviśeṣāt, jarāmṛtyuvat.

And from reason: 'Knowing snakes, the tips of kuśa grass and wells, men avoid them; some fall into them through ignorance; see how superior is the fruit of knowledge' (Śāntiparvan 201.16). So it is understood that the ignorant man, who takes the body and the rest to be the Self, driven by attachment, aversion and the like, performing merit and demerit, is born and dies; and that those who see the Self as distinct from the body and the rest are freed, because attachment, aversion and the like are abandoned and the activity of merit and demerit that depends on them ceases. No one can refute this by reason. That being so, the knower of the field, though he is the Lord himself, seems to be in saṃsāra through the distinction of adjuncts made by ignorance — just as the Self seems to be the body and the rest. For it is well known that all creatures firmly identify with the body and the rest, which are not the Self — an identification made by ignorance, like the conviction that a post is a man. Yet the man's attributes do not thereby belong to the post, nor the post's to the man; likewise consciousness is not an attribute of the body, nor the body's attributes of the conscious being. So being of the nature of pleasure, pain, delusion and the like does not properly belong to the Self, since it too is made by ignorance, like old age and death.

na, atulyatvāt iti cet – sthāṇupuruṣau jñeyau eva santau jñātrā anyonyasmin adhyastau avidyayā; dehātmanoḥ tu jñeyajñātroḥ eva itaretarādhyāsaḥ iti na samaḥ dṛṣṭāntaḥ, ataḥ dehadharmaḥ jñeyaḥ api jñātuḥ ātmanaḥ bhavati iti cet –

Objection: no, the cases are not alike. Post and man are both objects known, mutually superimposed by a knower through ignorance; but body and Self are mutual superimpositions of the known and the knower themselves. So the illustration is not parallel, and therefore an attribute of the body, though an object, does belong to the Self, the knower.

na, acaitanyādiprasaṅgāt, yadi hi jñeyasya dehādeḥ kṣetrasya dharmāḥ sukhaduḥkha mohecchādveṣādayaḥ jñātuḥ ātmanaḥ bhavanti tadā jñeyasya kṣetrasya dharmāḥ kecit ātmanaḥ bhavanti avidyādhyāropitāḥ, jarāmaraṇādayastu na bhavanti iti viśeṣahetuḥ vaktavyaḥ. “na bhavanti” ityasti anumānam avidyādhyāropitatvāt, jarāmaraṇādivat iti, heyatvāt upādeyatvācca ityādi. tatraivaṃ sati kartṛtvabhoktṛtvalakṣaṇaḥ saṃsāraḥ jñeyasthaḥ jñātari avidyayā adhyāropitaḥ iti, na tena jñātuḥ kiñcidduṣyati. yathā bālaiḥ adhyāropitena ākāśasya talamalinatvādinā. evaṃ ca sati sarvakṣetreṣvapi sataḥ bhagavataḥ kṣetrajñasya īśvarasya saṃsāritvagandhamātram api na āśaṅkyam (na śakyaṃ vaktum). na hi kvacidapi loke avidyādhyastena dharmeṇa kasyacit upakāraḥ apakāro vā dṛṣṭaḥ.

No: that would make the Self non-conscious and so on. If the attributes of the known field, body and the rest — pleasure, pain, delusion, desire, aversion and so on — belonged to the knower, the Self, then a special reason would have to be given why some attributes of the known field, superimposed through ignorance, belong to the Self, while others, old age, death and the rest, do not. There is an inference that they do not: because they are superimposed through ignorance, like old age and death; and because they are things to be avoided or sought, and so on. That being so, saṃsāra, consisting of agency and enjoyership, resides in the known and is superimposed on the knower by ignorance; and the knower is not in the least tainted by it — as space is not tainted by the surface and dirt superimposed on it by children. So not even a trace of saṃsāra can be suspected (or said) of the Blessed Lord, the knower of the field, though he is present in all fields. For nowhere in the world is anyone seen to be helped or harmed by an attribute superimposed through ignorance.

yattu uktaṃ “na samo dṛṣṭāntaḥ iti” tat asat. katham? avidyādhyāsamātraṃ hi dṛṣṭāntadārṣṭāntikayoḥ sādharmyaṃ vivakṣitam; tat na vyabhicarati. “yattu jñātari vyabhicarati” iti manyase tasyāpi anaikāntikatvaṃ darśitaṃ jarādibhiḥ. avidyāvattvāt kṣetrajñasya saṃsāritvam iti cet, na avidyāyāḥ tāmasatvāt. tāmaso hi pratyayaḥ āvaraṇātmakatvāt avidyā, viparītagrāhakaḥ, saṃśayopasthāpakaḥ vā agrahaṇātmako vā. vivekaprakāśabhāve tadabhāvāt. tāmase ca āvaraṇātmake timirādidoṣe sati agrahaṇādeḥ avidyāmātrasya (avidyātrayasya) upalabdheḥ.

As for the claim that 'the illustration is not parallel', that is wrong. How? The likeness intended between illustration and illustrated is simply superimposition through ignorance, and that does not fail. And if you think it fails in the case of the knower, its inconclusiveness has been shown by old age and the rest. Objection: the knower of the field is in saṃsāra because he has ignorance. No, because ignorance is tāmasa. For ignorance is a tāmasa cognition, being of the nature of covering: it apprehends wrongly, or raises doubt, or consists in non-apprehension; for when the light of discrimination appears, it is gone. And when there is a tāmasa defect of covering, such as an eye disease, all of ignorance — non-apprehension and the rest (the three kinds of ignorance) — is observed.

atrāha – evaṃ tarhi jñātṛdharmaḥ avidyā? na, karaṇe cakṣuṣi taimirikatvādi doṣopalabdheḥ. yattu manyase jñātṛdharmaḥ avidyā, tadeva ca avidyādharmavattvaṃ kṣetrajñasya saṃsāritvam; tatra yaduktam īśvara eva kṣetrajñaḥ na saṃsārī ityetat ayuktam iti – tat na; yathā karaṇe cakṣuṣi viparītagrāhakādidoṣasya darśanāt. na viparītādigrahaṇaṃ tannimitto vā taimirikatvādidoṣaḥ grahītuḥ cakṣuṣaḥ saṃskāreṇa timire apanīte grahītuḥ adarśanāt na grahītuḥ adharmaḥ yathā, tathā sarvatraiva agrahaṇaviparītasaṃśayapratyayāḥ tannimittāḥ, karaṇasyaiva kasyacit bhavitum arhanti. na jñātuḥ kṣetrajñasya. saṃvedyatvācca teṣāṃ pradīpaprakāśavat na jñātṛdharmatvam. saṃvedyatvādeva svātmavyatiriktasaṃvedyatvam. sarvakaraṇaviyoge ca kaivalye sarvavādibhiḥ avidyādidoṣavattvānabhyu pagamāt. ātmanaḥ yadi kṣetrasya agnyuṣṇavat svaḥ dharmaḥ, tataḥ na kadācidapi tena viyogaḥ syāt. avikriyasya ca vyomavat sarvagatasya amūrtasya ātmanaḥ kenacit saṃyogaviyogānupapatteḥ siddhaṃ kṣetrajñasya nityam eva īśvaratvam. “anāditvānnirguṇatvāt” (13.31) iti īśvaravacanācca.

Here someone says: then ignorance is an attribute of the knower? No: defects such as the disease of the eye are seen in the instrument, the eye. You may think that ignorance is an attribute of the knower, and that the knower of the field, having the attribute of ignorance, is in saṃsāra, so that the statement 'the Lord himself is the knower of the field and is not in saṃsāra' is wrong. That is not so: defects such as wrong apprehension are seen in the instrument, the eye. Wrong apprehension and the like, and their cause, the eye disease, do not belong to the perceiver; when the disease is removed by treating the eye, they are not seen in the perceiver; so they are not attributes of the perceiver. Likewise everywhere, non-apprehension, wrong apprehension and doubt, and their causes, can only belong to some instrument, not to the knower, the knower of the field. And because they are perceived, like the light of a lamp, they are not attributes of the knower; being perceived, they are perceived by something other than themselves. And in aloneness, when separated from all instruments, no school admits that the Self has ignorance or other defects. If it were the Self's own attribute, like heat of fire, the Self could never be separated from it. And since the Self is changeless, all-pervading like space and formless, union and separation with anything are impossible for it; so it is established that the knower of the field is eternally the Lord — and by the Lord's words too, 'being beginningless and without qualities' (13.31).

nanvevaṃ sati saṃsārasaṃsāritvābhāve śāstrānarthakyādidoṣaḥ syāditi cet – na sarvaiḥ abhyupagatatvāt. sarvaiḥ hi ātmavādibhiḥ abhyupagataḥ doṣaḥ na ekena parihartavyaḥ bhavati. katham abhyupagataḥ iti? muktātmanāṃ hi saṃsārasaṃsāritvavyavahārābhāvaḥ sarvaireva ātmavādibhiḥ iṣyate. na ca teṣāṃ śāstrānarthakyādidoṣaprāptiḥ abhyupagatā. tathā naḥ, kṣetrajñānām īśvaraikatve sati śāstrānarthakyaṃ bhavatu, avidyāviṣaye ca arthavattvam, yathā dvaitavādināṃ sarveṣāṃ bandhāvasthāyām eva śāstrādyarthavatvaṃ na muktāvasthāyāṃ, evam.

Objection: if so, since there is neither saṃsāra nor anyone in saṃsāra, the fault of the scriptures being pointless and the like would arise. No, because it is admitted by all. A fault admitted by all who hold that there is a Self need not be removed by one alone. How is it admitted? All who hold there is a Self accept that for liberated selves there is no dealing with saṃsāra and those in it; and they do not admit that the fault of the scriptures being pointless arises for them. So for us too, when the knowers of the field are one with the Lord, let the scriptures be pointless [for the liberated], and let them have meaning within the sphere of ignorance — just as for all dualists the scriptures have meaning only in the state of bondage and not in the state of liberation.

nanu ātmanaḥ bandhamuktāvasthe paramārthataḥ eva vastubhūte dvaitināṃ naḥ sarveṣām. ataḥ heyopādeyatatsādhanasadbhāve śāstrādyarthavattvaṃ syāt, advaitināṃ punaḥ dvaitasya aparamārthatvāt, avidyākṛtatvāt, bandhāvasthāyāśca ātmanaḥ aparamārthatve nirviṣayatvāt śāstrādyānarthakyam iti cet, na ātmanaḥ avasthābhedānupapatteḥ. yadi tāvat ātmanaḥ bandhamuktāvasthe yugapat syātāṃ krameṇa vā? yugapat tāvat virodhāt na sambhavataḥ, sthitigatī iva ekasmin. kramabhāvitve ca nirnimittatvaṃ sannimittatvaṃ vā? nirnimittatve anirmokṣaprasaṅgaḥ. sa nimittatve ca svataḥ abhāvāt aparamārthatvaprasaṅgaḥ. tathā ca sati abhyupagamahāniḥ.

Objection: for all of us dualists the states of bondage and liberation of the Self are real things in truth; so, since what is to be avoided, what is to be sought and their means really exist, the scriptures have meaning. For the non-dualists, however, since duality is not ultimately real but made by ignorance, and the state of bondage of the Self is not ultimately real, the scriptures would have no object and be pointless. No: because different states of the Self are impossible. If the Self has states of bondage and liberation, are they simultaneous or successive? Simultaneous they cannot be, being contradictory, like standing and moving in one thing. If successive, are they uncaused or caused? If uncaused, there could be no liberation. If caused, since they would not be intrinsic, they would not be ultimately real; and then your position is abandoned.

kiṃ ca – bandhamuktāvasthayoḥ paurvāparyānirūpaṇāyāṃ bandhāvasthā pūrvaṃ prakalpyā anādimatī, antavatī ca. tacca pramāṇaviruddham. tathā mokṣāvasthā sādimatī anantā ca pramāṇaviruddhā eva abhyupagamyate. na ca avasthāvataḥ avasthāntaraṃ gacchataḥ nityatvam upapādayituṃ śakyam. atha anityatvadoṣa parihārāya bandhamuktāvasthābhedaḥ na parikalpyate ataḥ dvaitināmapi śāstrānarthakyādidoṣaḥ aparihāryaḥ eva, iti samānatvāt na advaitavādinā parihartavyo doṣaḥ.

Moreover, when the order of the states of bondage and liberation is determined, the state of bondage must be imagined as first, beginningless and yet having an end — which conflicts with the means of knowledge; likewise the state of liberation is admitted as having a beginning and no end — which also conflicts with the means of knowledge. Nor can the eternity of something that has states and passes from one state to another be established. If, to avoid the fault of impermanence, a difference of the states of bondage and liberation is not imagined, then for the dualists too the fault of the scriptures being pointless is unavoidable. Since it is common to both, the non-dualist need not remove it.

na ca śāstrānarthakyam, yathāprasiddhāvidvatpuruṣaviṣayatvāt śāstrasya, aviduṣāṃ hi phalahetvoḥ anātmanoḥ ātmadarśanaṃ na viduṣām. viduṣāṃ hi phalahetubhyām ātmanaḥ anyatvadarśane sati tayoḥ ahamiti ātmadarśanānupapatteḥ. na hi atyantamūḍhaḥ unmattādiḥ api jalāgnyoḥ chāyāprakāśayoḥ vā aikātmyaṃ paśyati, kimuta vivekī. tasmāt na vidhipratiṣedhaśāstraṃ tāvat phalahetubhyām ātmanaḥ anyatvadarśinaḥ bhavati. na hi “devadatta tvam idaṃ kuru” iti kasmiṃścit karmaṇi niyukte viṣṇumitraḥ “ahaṃ niyuktaḥ” iti tatrasthaḥ niyogaṃ śṛṇvannapi pratipadyate. niyogaviṣayavivekāgrahaṇāttu upapadyate pratipattiḥ. tathā phalahetvoḥ api.

Nor are the scriptures pointless, since they concern the ignorant man as commonly found. For it is the ignorant, not the knowers, who see the Self in the fruit and the means, which are not the Self. For the knowers, when they see the Self as other than fruit and means, cannot see 'I' in them. Not even an utterly deluded madman sees water and fire, or shade and light, as one; how much less a man of discrimination. So the scriptures of injunction and prohibition do not apply to one who sees the Self as other than fruit and means. When Devadatta is enjoined to some action — 'Devadatta, do this' — Viṣṇumitra, standing there and hearing the injunction, does not think 'I am enjoined'. But if he fails to discern to whom the injunction is addressed, that understanding is possible. So too with fruit and means.

nanu prākṛtasambandhāpekṣayā yuktāḥ eva pratipattiḥ śāstrārthaviṣayā phalahetubhyām anyātmadarśane'pi sati – iṣṭaphalahetau pravartitaḥ asmi, aniṣṭaphalahetoḥ ca nivartitaḥ asmi iti; yathā pitṛputrādīnām itaretarātmānyatvadarśane satyapi anyonyaniyoga pratiṣedhārthapratipattiḥ? (bṛ.u.1.5.17).

Objection: even when one sees the Self as other than fruit and means, an understanding of the meaning of scripture is still proper, in view of the natural connection: 'I am impelled towards the means of a desired fruit, and turned away from the means of an undesired fruit' — just as father and son, though each sees himself as other than the other, still understand each other's commands and prohibitions (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.5.17)?

na vyatiriktātmadarśana pratipatteḥ prāgena phalahetvo ātmābhimānasya siddhatvāt. pratipannaniyogapratiṣedhārthaḥ. hi phalahetubhyām anyatvaṃ pratipadyate, na pūrvam. tasmāt vidhipratiṣedha śāstram avidvadviṣayam iti siddham.

No: because before the understanding that the Self is distinct, identification with fruit and means is already established. It is only after one has understood the meaning of injunction and prohibition that one comes to understand that one is other than fruit and means, not before. So it is established that the scriptures of injunction and prohibition concern the ignorant.

nanu “svargakāmo yajeta” (āpa.śrau. 10.2.1) “kalañjaṃ na bhakṣayet” ityādau ātmavyatirekadarśinām apravṛttau kevaladehādyātmadṛṣṭīnāṃ ca – ataḥ kartuḥ abhāvāt śāstrānarthakyam iti cet?

Objection: in 'one who desires heaven should sacrifice' (Āpastamba Śrautasūtra 10.2.1), 'one should not eat kalañja' and the like, those who see the Self as distinct do not act, and those who see only the body and the rest as the Self [do not believe in another world]; so for lack of an agent the scriptures are pointless?

na, yathā prasiddhitaḥ eva pravṛttinivṛttyupapatteḥ. īśvarakṣetrajñai katvadarśī brahmavit tāvat na pravartate. tathā nairātma vādyapi “nāsti paralokaḥ” iti na pravartate. yathāprasiddhi tastu vidhipratiṣedhaśāstra śravaṇānyathā'nupa pattyā anumitātmastitvaḥ ātmaviśeṣānabhijñaḥ karmaphalaṃ sañjātatṛṣṇaḥ śraddadhānatayā ca pravartate iti sarveṣāṃ naḥ pratyakṣam, ataḥ na śāstrānarthakyam.

No: activity and abstention are explained just as is commonly seen. The knower of Brahman, who sees the oneness of the Lord and the knower of the field, does not act; likewise the one who denies a Self does not act, thinking 'there is no other world'. But as commonly found, one who has inferred the existence of a Self because the scriptures of injunction and prohibition could not otherwise make sense, without knowing the Self's particular nature, in whom thirst for the fruits of action has arisen, acts out of faith. This is plain to all of us; so the scriptures are not pointless.

vivekinām apravṛttidarśanāt tadanugāminām apravṛttau śāstrānarthakyam iti cet –

Objection: since men of discrimination are seen not to act, if those who follow them also do not act, the scriptures become pointless?

na, kasyacideva vivekopapatteḥ. anekeṣu hi prāṇiṣu kaścideva vivekī syāt, yathā idānīm. na ca vivekinām anuvartante mūḍhāḥ, rāgādidoṣatantratvāt pravṛtteḥ, abhicaraṇādau ca pravṛttidarśanāt. svabhāvyācca pravṛtteḥ. “svabhāvastu pravartate” (5.14) iti hi uktam. tasmāt avidyāmātraṃ saṃsāraḥ yathādṛṣṭaviṣayaḥ eva, na kṣetrajñasya kevalasya avidyā tatkāryaṃ ca. na mithyājñānaṃ paramārthavastu dūṣayituṃ samartham. na hi ūṣaradeśaṃ snehena paṅkīkartuṃ śaknoti marīcyudakaṃ, tathā avidyā kṣetrajñasya na kiñcit kartuṃ śaknoti. ataśca idamuktaṃ “kṣetrajñaṃ cāpi māṃ viddhi” (13.2), “ajñānenāvṛtaṃ jñānam” (5.15) iti ca.

No, because discrimination arises in only a few. Among many living beings only someone may be discriminating, as now. Nor do the deluded follow the discriminating, since activity is governed by faults such as attachment, and activity is seen even in sorcery and the like; and activity is natural — for it has been said, 'it is nature that works' (5.14). Therefore saṃsāra is mere ignorance, having just what is seen as its object; neither ignorance nor its effects belong to the knower of the field in himself. False knowledge cannot taint a real thing. The water of a mirage cannot make salt ground muddy with its wetness; just so ignorance can do nothing to the knower of the field. And therefore this was said: 'and know me too as the knower of the field' (13.2), and 'knowledge is covered by ignorance' (5.15).

atha kimidaṃ saṃsāriṇām iva “aham evam” “mamaiva idam” iti paṇḍitānām api?

Then what is this — that even the learned, like those in saṃsāra, think 'I am thus' and 'this is mine'?

śṛṇu; idaṃ tatpāṇḍityaṃ, yat kṣetra eva ātmadarśanam. yadi punaḥ kṣetrajñam avikriyaṃ paśyeyuḥ, tataḥ na bhogaṃ karma vā ākāṅkṣeyuḥ “mama syāt” iti. vikriyaiva bhogakarmaṇī. atha evaṃ sati, phalārthitvāt avidvān pravartate. viduṣaḥ punaḥ avikriyātma darśinaḥ phalārthitvābhāvāt pravṛttyanupapattau kāryakāraṇasaṅghātavyāpāroparame nivṛttiḥ upacaryate. idaṃ ca anyat pāṇḍityaṃ kasya cit astu – kṣetrajñaḥ īśvaraḥ eva, kṣetraṃ ca anyat kṣetrajñasya eva viṣayaḥ. ahaṃ tu saṃsārī sukhī duḥkhī ca. saṃsāroparamaśca mama kartavyaḥ kṣetrakṣetrajñavijñānena dhyānena ca īśvaraṃ kṣetrajñaṃ sākṣātkṛtvā tatsvarūpa avasthānena iti. yaśca evaṃ budhyate, yaśca bodhayati, nāsau kṣetrajñaḥ iti. evaṃ manvānaḥ yaśca sa paṇḍitāpasadaḥ saṃsāramokṣayoḥ śāstrasya ca arthavattvaṃ karomi iti, ātmahā svayaṃ mūḍhaḥ, anyāṃśca vyāmohayati – śāstrārthasampradāyarahitatvāt śrutahānim aśrutakalpanāṃ ca kurvan. tasmāt asampradāyavit sarvaśāstravidapi mūrkhavadeva upekṣaṇīyaḥ.

Listen: this is their learning — seeing the Self in the field. If they saw the knower of the field as changeless, they would not crave enjoyment or action, thinking 'may it be mine'. Enjoyment and action are themselves change. That being so, the ignorant man acts because he seeks fruit; but for the knower, who sees the changeless Self, there is no seeking of fruit and so no activity, and when the activity of the aggregate of body and senses ceases, withdrawal is spoken of figuratively. And let someone have this other kind of learning: 'the knower of the field is the Lord, and the field, something else, is the object of the knower of the field; but I am in saṃsāra, happy and unhappy; and I must end saṃsāra by realising the Lord, the knower of the field, through knowledge of the field and its knower and through meditation, and abiding in his nature.' He who understands so, and he who teaches so — 'he is not the knower of the field' — thinking 'I give meaning to saṃsāra, liberation and the scriptures', that worst of the learned is a slayer of the Self, himself deluded, and deludes others; for, lacking the traditional understanding of the meaning of scripture, he discards what is taught and imagines what is not taught. Therefore one who does not know the tradition, though he knows all the scriptures, is to be ignored like a fool.

yaduktam – “īśvarasya kṣetrajñai katve saṃsāritvaṃ prāpnoti, kṣetrajñānāṃ ceśva raikatve saṃsāriṇaḥ “abhāvāt saṃsārābhāvaprasaṅgaḥ” iti – etau doṣau pratyuktau “vidyā'vidyayoḥ vailakṣaṇyābhyupagamāt” iti. katham? avidyāparikalpitadoṣeṇa tadviṣayaṃ vastu pāramārthikaṃ na duṣyati iti. tathā ca dṛṣṭāntaḥ darśitaḥ – marīcyambhasā ūṣaradeśaḥ na paṅkīkriyate iti. saṃsāriṇaḥ abhāvāt saṃsārabhāvaprasaṅgadoṣo'pi saṃsārasaṃsāriṇoḥ avidyākalpitatvopapattyā pratyuktaḥ.

As for what was said — 'if the Lord is one with the knower of the field, he becomes subject to saṃsāra; and if the knowers of the field are one with the Lord, there being no one in saṃsāra, it follows that there is no saṃsāra' — these two faults have been answered by admitting the difference between knowledge and ignorance. How? A real thing is not tainted by a fault imagined by ignorance with regard to it. And the illustration has been shown: salt ground is not made muddy by the water of a mirage. The fault that saṃsāra would cease for lack of anyone in saṃsāra is also answered, since saṃsāra and the one in saṃsāra are explained as imagined by ignorance.

nanu avidyāvattvameva kṣetrajñasya saṃsāritvadoṣaḥ. tatkṛtaṃ ca sukhitva duḥ khitvādi pratyakṣam upalabhyate? iti cet –

Objection: but having ignorance is itself the fault of the knower of the field being in saṃsāra; and the being happy, unhappy and the rest that it produces is perceived directly?

na, jñeyasya kṣetradharmatvāt, jñātuḥ kṣetrajñasya tatkṛtadoṣānupapatteḥ. yāvat kiñcit kṣetrajñasya doṣajātam avidyamānam āsañjayasi tasya jñeyatvopapatteḥ kṣetradharmatva meva na kṣetrajñadharmatvam. na ca tena kṣetrajñaḥ duṣyati, jñeyena jñātuḥ saṃsargānupapatteḥ. yadi hi saṃsargaḥ syāt jñeyatvameva na upapadyeta. yadi ātmanaḥ dharmaḥ avidyāvattvaṃ duḥkhitvādi ca kathaṃ bhoḥ pratyakṣam upalabhyate? kathaṃ vā kṣetrajñadharma? “jñeyaṃ ca tat sarvaṃ kṣetraṃ jñātaiva kṣetrajñaḥ” ityavadhārite, avidyāduḥkhitvādeḥ kṣetrajñaviśeṣaṇatvaṃ kṣetrajñadharmatvaṃ, tasya ca pratyakṣopa labhyatvam iti viruddham ucyate avidyāmātrāvaṣṭambhāt kevalam.

No: what is known is an attribute of the field, and a fault produced by it cannot belong to the knower, the knower of the field. Whatever host of faults you attach to the knower of the field, where they do not exist, since they are known they are attributes of the field, not of the knower of the field. Nor is the knower of the field tainted by them, since the knower cannot be in contact with the known; if there were contact, it could not be known at all. If having ignorance and being unhappy and so on were attributes of the Self, how, sir, could they be perceived directly? And how would they be attributes of the knower of the field? When it has been determined that 'all that is known is the field and the knower alone is the knower of the field', to say that ignorance, unhappiness and the rest qualify the knower of the field, are its attributes, and are perceived directly, is contradictory — said only by clinging to ignorance.

atrāha – sā avidyā kasyeti. yasya dṛśyate tasyaiva. kasya dṛśyate iti? atrocyate – “avidyā kasya dṛśyate” iti praśnaḥ nirarthakaḥ. katham? dṛśyate cet avidyā, tadvantamapi paśyasi. na ca tadvati upalabhyamāne “sā kasyeti” praśnaḥ yuktaḥ. na hi gomati upalabhyamāne “gāvaḥ kasyeti” praśnaḥ arthavān bhavati. nanu viṣamaḥ dṛṣṭāntaḥ – gavāṃ tadvataśca pratyakṣatvāt sambandhaḥ api pratyakṣaḥ iti praśnaḥ nirarthakaḥ. na tathā avidyā tadvān ca pratyakṣau, yataḥ praśnaḥ nirarthakaḥ syāt. apratyakṣeṇa avidyāvatā avidyāsambandhe jñāte kiṃ tava syāt? avidyāyāḥ anarthahetutvāt parihartavyā syāt. yasya avidyā saḥ tāṃ parihariṣyati. nanu mamaiva avidyā? jānāsi tarhi avidyāṃ tadvantaṃ ca ātmānam. jānāmi, na tu pratyakṣeṇa. anumānena cet jānāsi kathaṃ sambandhagrahaṇam? na hi tava jñātuḥ jñeyabhūtayā avidyayā tatkāle sambandhaḥ grahītuṃ śakyate, avidyāyāḥ viṣayatvenaiva jñātuḥ upayuktatvāt. na ca jñātuḥ avidyāyāḥ ca sambandhasya yaḥ grahītā, jñānaṃ ca anyat tadviṣayaṃ sambhavati, anavasthā prāpteḥ. yadi jñātrā'pi jñeyasambandho jñāyeta anyaḥ jñātā kalpyaḥ syāt, tasyāpi anyaḥ tasyāpi anyaḥ iti anavasthā aparihāryā. yadi punaḥ avidyā jñeyā, anyadvā jñeyaṃ jñeyameva. tathā jñātā'pi jñātaiva, na jñeyaṃ bhavati. yadā ca evam avidyāduḥkhitvādyaiḥ na jñātuḥ kṣetrajñasya kiñcit duṣyati.

Here someone asks: whose is that ignorance? Of him in whom it is seen. In whom is it seen? To this it is said: the question 'in whom is ignorance seen?' is meaningless. How? If ignorance is seen, you see its possessor too; and when its possessor is perceived, the question 'whose is it?' is not proper — when the owner of cows is seen, the question 'whose are the cows?' has no point. Objection: the illustration is not parallel; since the cows and their owner are perceived, their relation too is perceived, so the question is meaningless; but ignorance and its possessor are not perceived in that way, so the question is not meaningless. If you know the relation of ignorance to an unperceived possessor of ignorance, what good is it to you? Since ignorance is the cause of misfortune, it would have to be got rid of; he who has the ignorance will get rid of it. Objection: but the ignorance is mine. Then you know ignorance and yourself as its possessor. I know it, but not by perception. If you know it by inference, how do you grasp the relation? For at that moment you, the knower, cannot grasp your relation to ignorance, which is the object known, since the knower is engaged only in having ignorance as its object. Nor can there be a separate grasper of the relation between knower and ignorance, and another knowledge having that as its object, for that would lead to an infinite regress. If the relation of knower to known were to be known by the knower, another knower would have to be imagined, and another for him, and another for him — the regress is unavoidable. Whether ignorance or anything else is known, what is known is only known; likewise the knower is only the knower and never becomes the known. And so the knower, the knower of the field, is not in the least tainted by ignorance, unhappiness and the rest.

nanu ayameva doṣaḥ yat doṣavat kṣetravijñātṛtvam? na. vijñāna svarūpasyaiva avikriyasya vijñātṛtvopacārāt. yathā uṣṇatāmātreṇa agneḥ taptakriyopacāraḥ tadvat. yathā ca atra bhagavatā kriyākārakaphalātmatvābhāvaḥ ātmani svata eva darśitaḥ avidyādhyāropitaḥ eva kriyākārakādiḥ ātmani upacaryate tathā tatra tatra “ya enaṃ vetti hantāram” (2.19), “prakṛteḥ kriyamāṇāni guṇaiḥ karmāṇi sarvaśaḥ” (3.27), “nādatte kasyacit pāpam” (5.15) ityādiprakaraṇeṣu darśitaḥ, tathaiva ca vyākhyātam asmābhiḥ. uttareṣu ca prakaraṇeṣu darśayiṣyāmaḥ.

Objection: but this very thing is the fault — that he is the knower of a faulty field? No: knowership is ascribed figuratively to the changeless one whose nature is knowing itself, just as the act of heating is ascribed to fire merely because of its heat. And just as here the Lord has shown that the Self in itself is not of the nature of action, factors and fruit, and that action, factors and the rest, superimposed by ignorance, are only figuratively ascribed to the Self, so it has been shown in such passages as 'he who thinks this one a slayer' (2.19), 'actions are done in every way by the guṇas of nature' (3.27), 'he takes on the sin of no one' (5.15), and we have explained them accordingly. And we shall show it in the passages that follow.

hanta! tarhi ātmani kriyākārakaphalātmatāyāḥ svataḥ abhāve avidyayā ca adhyāropitatve “karmāṇi avidvatkartavyānyeva, na viduṣām”, iti prāptam?

Well then, if the Self in itself is not of the nature of action, factors and fruit, and these are superimposed by ignorance, it follows that actions are to be done only by the ignorant, not by the knowers?

satyam evaṃ prāptam. etadeva ca “na hi dehabhṛtā śakyam” (18.11) ityatra darśayiṣyāmaḥ. sarvaśāstropasaṃhāraprakaraṇe ca “samāsenaiva kaunteya! niṣṭhā jñānasya yā parā” (18.50) ityatra viśeṣataḥ darśayiṣyāmaḥ. alam atra bahuprapañceneti upasaṃhriyate.

True, that follows. And this we shall show at 'for it is not possible for an embodied being' (18.11), and especially in the section concluding the whole teaching, at 'in brief, Kaunteya, the supreme steadfastness in knowledge' (18.50). Enough of further elaboration here; so it is concluded.

'What that field is…' 'That' refers to what was pointed out as 'this body'. 'Constancy in the knowledge of the Self…' Knowledge concerning the Self and the like is knowledge of the Self; constancy in it is being always in it. Seeing the purpose of the knowledge of the truth: the knowledge of the truth arises from the ripening of the cultivation of humility and the other means of knowledge (13.7); its purpose is liberation, the cessation of saṃsāra; reflecting on that is seeing the purpose of the knowledge of the truth — for by reflecting on the fruit of the knowledge of the truth one is drawn to practise its means. This — from humility to seeing the purpose of the knowledge of the truth — is declared to be knowledge, because it serves knowledge. Ignorance is what is other than this, the opposite of what has been described — pride, ostentation, violence, impatience, crookedness and the rest; this ignorance is to be known so as to be avoided, since it is the cause of engaging in saṃsāra.

Having made him attentive by stirring interest — 'what is to be known, whose fruit is immortality, is told by me' — he says: that which is to be known is said to be neither being nor non-being. Objection: after girding up with such great preparation and proclaiming at the top of his voice 'I will declare what is to be known', to say 'it is said to be neither being nor non-being' is unfitting? No, it is fitting. How? In all the Upaniṣads the Brahman to be known is pointed out only by negating particulars — 'not this, not this' (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.3.6), 'not gross, not subtle' (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.8.8) — as 'it is not this', since it is beyond speech. Objection: but there is no thing that is not denoted by the word 'is'; and if it is not denoted by 'is', the knowable does not exist; and 'it is to be known' and 'it is not denoted by the word "is"' are contradictory. It is certainly not non-existent, since it is not an object of the notion 'is not'. Objection: but all notions are accompanied by the notion 'is' or 'is not'; that being so, the knowable too must be an object of a cognition accompanied either by 'is' or by 'is not'. No: being beyond the senses, it is not the object of a cognition accompanied by either notion. A thing grasped by the senses, such as a pot, may be the object of a cognition accompanied by 'is' or by 'is not'; but this knowable, being beyond the senses and knowable only through the word of scripture, is not, like a pot, the object of a cognition accompanied by either notion. Hence it is said 'neither being nor non-being'. As for the claim that 'it is to be known' and 'it is said to be neither being nor non-being' are contradictory, they are not, according to the śruti 'it is other than the known and beyond the unknown' (Kena 1.3). Objection: then the śruti too has contradictory meaning — as when, having begun the hall for the sacrifice, it says 'who knows whether one exists in that world or not?' (Taittirīya Saṃhitā 6.1.1). No: the śruti that it is other than the known and the unknown aims at teaching something that must certainly be known; whereas 'whether in that world' and so on is an arthavāda, supplementary to an injunction.

And it stands to reason that Brahman is not denoted by words such as 'being' and 'non-being'. Every word, uttered to make a thing known and heard by the hearers, conveys its meaning only through genus, action, quality or relation, depending on the grasping of the convention; not otherwise, for that is never seen. For example, 'cow' or 'horse' through genus; 'cooks' or 'reads' through action; 'white' or 'black' through quality; 'wealthy' or 'owner of cows' through relation. But Brahman has no genus, so it is not denoted by words like 'being'; nor has it qualities, by which a quality-word might denote it, since it is without qualities; nor is it denoted by an action-word, since it is actionless, according to the śruti 'without parts, without action, tranquil' (Śvetāśvatara 6.19); nor is it related to anything, since it is one. Being non-dual, not an object, and the Self, it is right that it is denoted by no word — and by śrutis such as 'from which words turn back' (Taittirīya 2.4.1, 2.9.1).

As the knowers of the tradition say: 'the attributeless is expounded through superimposition and its negation'. Hands, feet and the rest, known everywhere as limbs of all bodies, do their work by virtue of the existence of the power of the knowable; so they are marks of the existence of the knowable and are spoken of figuratively as 'of the knowable'. The elements that make up the body and the five objects — the modifications born of nature described before — are included here in the word 'effect'. And the guṇas born of nature, consisting of pleasure, pain and delusion, are included in the word 'instrument', since they reside in the instruments. The producing of those effects and instruments — nature is said to be the cause in that, as their originator. Thus, by producing effect and instrument, nature is the cause of saṃsāra. On the reading 'kāryakāraṇa', effect and cause: a thing's transformation is its effect, and the modified thing is the cause; 'in the producing of these, modification and modified, effect and cause'. The witness: being near, he sees, himself not engaged — as, when the priests and the sacrificer are engaged in the sacrificial acts, another, standing aside, not engaged, skilled in the science of sacrifice, observes the merits and faults of the activity of priests and sacrificer; so the one not engaged in the activities of body and senses, other than them, different in nature, sees those body and senses in their activity from close by: he is the witness. Or else: body, eye, mind, understanding and self are seers; of these the outermost seer is the body; starting from it, the innermost, the inmost one, nearest, is the Self, the seer beyond which there is nothing more inward; seeing from the closest nearness, he is the witness. Or he is the witness, like the overseer of a sacrifice, because he makes everything his object. And the approver: approving is consenting, satisfaction in the actions of those who act; he who does it is the approver. Or else the approver: though himself not engaged in the activities of body and senses, he seems as if engaged, in accord with them; hence approver. Or, being their witness, he never stops them when they are engaged in their activities; hence approver. The supporter: support is the holding of their own nature by the combined body, senses, mind and understanding, which reflect consciousness, caused by their serving the purpose of another, the Self that is consciousness; since this is done by the conscious Self, the Self is called the supporter. The enjoyer: as heat with fire, through his nature of eternal consciousness, the understanding's notions of pleasure, pain and delusion, concerning all objects, are seen arising as if seized by the conscious Self and distinct; so the Self is called the enjoyer. The great Lord: being the Self of all and independent, he is great and the Lord. The supreme Self: supreme in relation to the body and the rest up to the understanding, imagined through ignorance to be the inmost Self; the Self marked by being the witness and the rest is the supreme Self. He is also called, spoken of in śruti, by the word 'supreme Self'. Where is he? In this body, the supreme puruṣa, beyond the unmanifest — he who will be described in 'but the highest puruṣa is another, called the supreme Self' (15.17), who was introduced in 'and know me too as the knower of the field' (13.2), and is here explained and concluded.

This Self, with the marks described —

'He who thus knows…' He who knows the puruṣa thus, in the manner described, directly as his own Self — 'this I am' — and nature, as described, which is ignorance, together with the guṇas, its modifications, as turned back, brought to nothing, by knowledge — he, however he may live, in whatever way, when this body of the knower falls, is not born again, does not arise for another body; does not take another body, that is. Objection: although the absence of rebirth after the arising of knowledge has been stated, still it is not right that the actions done before the arising of knowledge, those that come after it, and those done in many past births should perish without giving their fruit; so there should be three births, since the loss of what has been done is not right — as with the actions that have begun to bear fruit and have started the present birth; and no difference among actions is known. So the three kinds of action should start three births; or all together should start one birth. Otherwise, if what has been done were lost, there would be no confidence in anything, and the meaning of scripture would be void. So it is wrong to say 'he is not born again'. No: from hundreds of śrutis — 'and his actions are exhausted' (Muṇḍaka 2.2.8), 'he who knows Brahman becomes Brahman' (Muṇḍaka 3.2.9), 'for him the delay lasts only so long' (Chāndogya 6.14.2), 'all his actions are burnt up like the tuft of reed in fire' (Chāndogya 5.24.3, in substance) — the burning of all the knower's actions is declared. Here too the burning of all actions has been told in 'as fuel' (4.37), and he will tell it again (18.66). And it stands to reason: actions start the sprout of another birth because of the seed of the afflictions, ignorance and desire. Here too the Lord has said in many places that actions done with egoism and purpose produce fruit, and not others. And: 'as seeds burnt by fire do not sprout again, so the Self is not again joined [to a body] by afflictions burnt by knowledge' (Vanaparvan 200.100).

Granted that actions done after the arising of knowledge are burnt by knowledge, since they coexist with knowledge; but it is not right that actions done in this birth before knowledge arose, and in many past births, should be burnt. No: because of the qualification 'all actions' (4.37). If you say 'all actions that come after knowledge', no: there is no reason for restricting it. As for the claim that 'just as the actions that started the present birth are not destroyed even when knowledge exists, because they have already begun to give fruit, so it is not right that actions that have not begun to bear fruit should be destroyed' — that is wrong. How? Because those have begun their fruit, like a released arrow. As an arrow released earlier to hit a target continues even after hitting it, stopping only when it falls as its momentum runs out, so the action that started the body continues as before, even when its purpose of keeping the body going has ended, until the momentum of its impressions is spent. But that same arrow, not yet released, without momentum, though fitted to the bow, is withdrawn; so actions that have not begun to bear fruit, resting in their own seat, are made seedless by knowledge of the truth. Therefore, when this body of the knower falls, 'he is not born again' is rightly said — this is established.

'Whatever being is born…' Whatever thing, any being whatever, is born, arises — without distinction? No, he says: moving or unmoving — know, understand, that it arises from the union of the field and the knower of the field, bull of the Bharatas. What union of field and knower of the field is meant? The knower of the field cannot have with the field a union that is a particular relation through contact of parts, like a pot with a rope, since like space it has no parts. Nor inherence, as of threads and cloth, since field and knower of the field are not admitted to be cause and effect of one another. It is said: the union of field and knower of the field, object and subject, different in nature, consists in the mutual superimposition of each and its attributes on the other, rooted in failure to discriminate their natures — like the union of rope or mother-of-pearl with the superimposed snake or silver, through absence of the knowledge that discriminates them. This union of field and knower of the field, of the nature of superimposition, is false knowledge. He who, having understood according to scripture the difference in the marks of field and knower of the field, separates the knower of the field as described from the field shown before, like the reed from the muñja grass, and sees the knowable, from which every particular adjunct has been removed by 'it is said to be neither being nor non-being' (13.12), as Brahman itself; and whose understanding is settled that the field, like an elephant (or palace) made by magic, a thing seen in a dream, or a city of the gandharvas, 'appears as real though it is unreal' — his false knowledge departs, since it is contradicted by right vision as described. Since the cause of birth departs, what was said in 'he who thus knows the puruṣa and nature together with the guṇas' (13.23) — that the knower is not born again — has been rightly said.

With 'he is not born again' (13.23), the absence of birth, through the cessation of the seed of saṃsāra, ignorance and the rest, was stated as the fruit of right vision; and the union of field and knower of the field, caused by ignorance, was stated as the cause of birth. So right vision, which removes that ignorance, though already told, is told again in other words:

samamiti – samaṃ nirviśeṣaṃ, tiṣṭhantaṃ sthitiṃ kurvantaṃ, kva? sarveṣu samasteṣu bhūteṣu brahmādisthāvarānteṣu prāṇiṣu, kam? parameśvaraṃ, dehendriya manobuddhyavyaktātmanaḥ apekṣya parameśvaraḥ taṃ sarveṣu bhūteṣu samaṃ tiṣṭhantam. tāni viśinaṣṭi “vinaśyatsu” iti, taṃ ca parameśvaram “avinaśyantam” iti, bhūtānāṃ parameśvarasya ca atyantavailakṣaṇyapradarśanārtham. katham? sarveṣāṃ hi bhāvivikārāṇāṃ janilakṣaṇaḥ bhāvavikāraḥ mūlam, janmottarakālabhāvinaḥ anye sarve bhāvavikārāḥ vināśāntāḥ; vināśāt paraḥ na kaścit asti bhāvavikāraḥ, bhāvābhāvāt. sati hi dharmiṇi dharmāḥ bhavanti. ataḥ antyabhāva vikārābhāvānuvādena tat pūrvabhāvinaḥ sarve bhāvavikārāḥ pratiṣiddhāḥ bhavanti saha kāryaiḥ. tasmāt sarvabhūtavailakṣaṇyam, atyantameva parameśvarasya siddhaṃ, nirviśeṣatvam, ekatvaṃ ca. yaḥ evaṃ yathoktaṃ parameśvaraṃ paśyati, saḥ paśyati.

'Abiding the same in all beings…' The same, without distinction, abiding, standing — where? In all beings, in all creatures from Brahmā down to the immovable. Whom? The supreme Lord — supreme in relation to body, senses, mind, understanding and the unmanifest, which are taken to be the self — abiding the same in all beings. He qualifies the beings as 'perishing' and the supreme Lord as 'not perishing', to show the absolute difference between beings and the supreme Lord. How? Of all the modifications of being, the modification called birth is the root; all the others, coming after birth, end in destruction; after destruction there is no further modification of being, since there is no being; for attributes exist only when the bearer exists. So by mentioning the absence of the last modification of being, all the modifications of being that come before it, together with their effects, are denied. Therefore the absolute difference of the supreme Lord from all beings, his being without distinctions, and his oneness, are established. He who sees the supreme Lord thus, as described, sees.

nanu sarvo'pi lokaḥ paśyati, kiṃ viśeṣaṇena iti? satyam paśyati; kiṃ tu viparītaṃ paśyati. ataḥ viśinaṣṭi – sa eva paśyatīti. yathā timiradoṣadṛṣṭiḥ anekaṃ candraṃ paśyati tamapekṣya ekacandradarśī viśiṣyate – sa eva paśyatīti; tathaiva ihāpi ekam avibhaktaṃ yathoktam ātmānaṃ yaḥ paśyati, saḥ vibhaktānekātmaviparītadarśibhyaḥ viśiṣyate – sa eva paśyatīti. itare paśyantaḥ api na paśyanti, viparītadarśitvāt – anekacandradarśivat ityarthaḥ

Objection: everyone sees; why the qualification? True, they see; but they see wrongly. So he specifies: he alone sees. As one whose sight is defective sees many moons, and compared with him one who sees a single moon is distinguished — he alone sees — so here too he who sees the Self as described, one and undivided, is distinguished from those who wrongly see many divided selves: he alone sees. The others, though seeing, do not see, because they see wrongly, like one who sees many moons — that is the meaning.

na hinastyātmanā''tmānaṃ tato yāti parāṃ gatim

samam the same paśyan seeing hi for sarvatra everywhere samavasthitam equally abiding īśvaram the Lord na not hinasti he harms ātmanā by the self ātmānam the Self tataḥ then yāti he goes parām to the supreme gatim goal

For, seeing the Lord established alike everywhere, he does not harm the Self by the self, and so he goes to the highest goal.

bhāṣya

yathoktasya samyagdarśanasya phalavacanena stutiḥ kartavyā, iti ślokaḥ ārabhyate –

Right vision as described should be praised by stating its fruit; so the verse begins:

samamiti – samaṃ paśyan upalabhamānaḥ yasmāt sarvatra sarvabhūteṣu, samavasthitaṃ tulyatayā avasthitam īśvaram atītānantaraślokoktalakṣaṇamityarthaḥ. samaṃ paśyan. kim? na hinasti hiṃsāṃ na karoti ātmanā svenaiva svam ātmānam. tataḥ tadahiṃsanāt yāti parāṃ prakṛṣṭāṃ gatim mokṣākhyām. nanu naiva kaścit prāṇī svayaṃ svam ātmānaṃ hinasti, katham ucyate aprāptaṃ “na hinasti” iti? yathā “na pṛthivyām nāntarikṣe na divyagniḥ cetavyaḥ” (tai.saṃ. 5.2.7.1) ityādi. naiṣa doṣaḥ. ajñānām ātmatiraskaraṇopapatteḥ. sarvo hi ajñaḥ atyantaprasiddhaṃ sākṣāt aparokṣam ātmānaṃ tiraskṛtya anātmānam ātmatvena parigṛhya, tamapi dharmādharmau kṛtvā upāttam ātmānaṃ hatvā anyam ātmānam upādatte navam, taṃ caiva hatvā anyam, evaṃ tamapi hatvā anyam. ityevam upāttamupāttam ātmānaṃ hantīti ātmahā sarvaḥ ajñaḥ. yastu paramārthātmā, asāvapi sarvadā avidyayā hata iva, vidyamānaphalābhāvāt, iti sarve eva ātmahanaḥ eva avidvāṃsaḥ. yastu itaraḥ yathoktātmadarśī saḥ ubhaya'thāpi ātmanā ātmānaṃ na hinasti na hanti. tataḥ yāti parāṃ gatiṃ, yathoktaṃ phalaṃ tasya bhavatītyarthaḥ.

'For seeing the Lord the same everywhere…' Since, seeing, perceiving, the Lord — with the marks stated in the verse just before — abiding equally everywhere, in all beings, seeing him the same — what? He does not harm, does no violence to, the self by the self, his own Self by himself. Then, from that non-harming, he goes to the supreme, highest, goal called liberation. Objection: no living being harms himself by himself; how is it said 'he does not harm', of what does not happen — as in 'the fire is to be laid neither on earth, nor in the mid-region, nor in heaven' (Taittirīya Saṃhitā 5.2.7.1)? This is no fault, since the ignorant do disregard the Self. Every ignorant man, disregarding the Self, which is utterly evident, direct and immediate, takes what is not the Self as the Self; and, having done merit and demerit, he slays that self he had taken up and takes up another, new one; and slaying that, another; and slaying that too, another. Thus slaying each self he takes up, every ignorant man is a slayer of the Self. And the Self that is the supreme truth is always as if slain by ignorance, since its existing fruit is absent. So all the ignorant are slayers of the Self. But the other, who sees the Self as described, does not harm, does not slay, the Self by the self in either way. Then he goes to the supreme goal: the fruit described is his — that is the meaning.

yaḥ paśyati tathā''tmānamakartāraṃ sa paśyati
|| 29 ||

prakṛtyā by nature eva alone ca and karmāṇi actions kriyamāṇāni are done sarvaśaḥ in every way yaḥ who paśyati sees tathā likewise ātmānam the Self akartāram as a non-doer saḥ he paśyati sees

He who sees that all actions are done by prakṛti alone, and that the Self is not the doer — he sees.

bhāṣya

sarvabhūtastham īśaṃ samaṃ paśyan na hinasti ātmanā ātmānam ityuktam, tadanupapannaṃ, svaguṇakarmavailakṣaṇyabhedabhinneṣu ātmasu, ityetat āśaṅkya āha –

It was said that seeing the Lord the same, abiding in all beings, one does not harm the self by the self. That seems impossible, since selves differ by the differences of their own qualities and actions. Anticipating this, he says:

prakṛtyā iti – prakṛtiḥ bhagavataḥ māyā triguṇātmikā, “māyāṃ tu prakṛtiṃ vidyāt” (śve.u.4.10) iti mantravarṇāt. tayā prakṛtyaiva na anyena, mahadādi kāryakaraṇākārapariṇatayā karmāṇi vāṅmanaḥkāyārabhyāṇi, kriyamāṇāni nirvartyamānāni, sarvaśaḥ sarvaprakāraiḥ yaḥ paśyati upalabhate, tathā ātmānaṃ kṣetrajñam, akartāraṃ sarvopādhivivarjitaṃ, sa paśyati saḥ paramārthadarśī ityabhiprāyaḥ nirguṇasya akartuḥ nirviśeṣasya ākāśasyeva bhede pramāṇānupapattiḥ ityarthaḥ.

'He who sees that actions are done by nature alone…' Nature is the Lord's māyā, made of the three guṇas, according to the mantra 'know māyā to be nature' (Śvetāśvatara 4.10). He who sees, perceives, that actions, begun by speech, mind and body, are done, performed, in every way, by that nature alone and by nothing else — transformed into the forms of effects and instruments, mahat and the rest — and likewise sees the Self, the knower of the field, as a non-agent, free of all adjuncts — he sees: he is a seer of the supreme truth, that is the purport. For that which is without qualities, a non-agent and without distinctions, like space, there is no evidence of difference — that is the meaning.

tata eva ca vistāraṃ brahma sampadyate tadā

yadā when bhūta-pṛthak-bhāvam the separate existence of beings eka-stham abiding in one anupaśyati he sees tataḥ from that eva alone ca and vistāram their expansion brahma Brahman sampadyate he becomes tadā then

When he sees the separate existence of beings as resting in the One, and their expansion from it alone, then he attains Brahman.

bhāṣya

punarapi tadeva samyagdarśanaṃ śabdāntareṇa prapañcayati –

He elaborates the same right vision again in other words:

yadā iti – yadā yasminkāle, bhūtapṛthagbhāvam bhūtānāṃ pṛthagbhāvaṃ pṛthaktvam, ekasmin ātmani sthitam ekastham anupaśyati, śāstrocāryopadeśam anu ātmānaṃ pratyakṣatvena paśyati “ātmaivedaṃ sarvam” (bṛ.u.2.4.6) iti, tataḥ eva ca tasmādeva ca vistāram utpattiṃ vikāsam – “ātmataḥ prāṇaḥ, ātmataḥ āśā, ātmataḥ smaraḥ, ātmataḥ ākāśaḥ ātmataḥ tejaḥ ātmataḥ āpaḥ ātmataḥ āvirbhāvatirobhāvau, ātmataḥ annam” (chāṃ.u.7.26.1) ityevamādiprakāraiḥ vistāraṃ yadā paśyati, brahma sampadyate brahmaiva bhavati tadā tasmin kāle ityarthaḥ.

'When he sees the separate existence of beings…' When, at whatever time, he sees the separateness of beings, their being different, as abiding in one, in the one Self — sees it, following the teaching of scripture and teacher, directly as the Self: 'the Self alone is all this' (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.4.6); and sees their expansion, arising, unfolding, from that alone — in such ways as 'from the Self life, from the Self hope, from the Self memory, from the Self space, from the Self fire, from the Self water, from the Self appearance and disappearance, from the Self food' (Chāndogya 7.26.1) — then, at that time, he attains Brahman, becomes Brahman itself, that is.

|| 31 ||

word by word · translation

anāditvāt being beginningless nirguṇatvāt being without qualities parama-ātmā supreme Self ayam this avyayaḥ imperishable śarīra-sthaḥ dwelling in the body api though kaunteya O son of Kuntī na neither karoti acts na nor lipyate is tainted

Being beginningless and without guṇas, this imperishable supreme Self, son of Kuntī, neither acts nor is tainted, though dwelling in the body.

bhāṣya

ekasya ātmanaḥ sarvadehātmatve taddoṣasambandhe prāpte idam ucyate –

If the one Self is the self of all bodies, it would be connected with their faults; so this is said:

anāditvāt iti – anāditvāt – anādeḥ bhāvaḥ anāditvam, ādiḥ kāraṇam, tat yasya nāsti tat anādi. yat hi ādimat tat svena ātmanā vyeti; ayaṃ tu anāditvāt niravayavaḥ iti kṛtvā na vyeti. tathā nirguṇatvāt. saguṇo hi guṇavyayāt vyeti, ayaṃ tu nirguṇatvāt na vyeti; iti paramātmā ayam avyayaḥ, na asya vyayaḥ vidyate ityavyayaḥ. yataḥ evam ataḥ śarīrastho'pi, śarīreṣu ātmanaḥ upalabdhiḥ bhavatīti śarīrasthaḥ ucyate. tathāpi na karoti karma. tadakaraṇādeva tatphalena na lipyate. yaḥ hi kartā saḥ karmaphalena lipyate. ayaṃ tu akartā, ataḥ na phalena lipyate ityarthaḥ. kaḥ punaḥ deheṣu karoti lipyate ca? yadi tāvat anyaḥ paramātmanaḥ dehī karoti lipyate ca, tataḥ idam anupapannam uktaṃ kṣetrajñeśvaraikatvaṃ “kṣetrajñaṃ cāpi māṃ viddhi” (13.2) ityādi. atha nāsti īśvarāt anyaḥ dehī, kaḥ karoti kaḥ lipyate ca iti vācyam. paro vā nāstīti. sarvathā durvijñeyaṃ durvācyaṃ ceti bhagavatproktaṃ aupaniṣadaṃ darśanaṃ parityaktaṃ vaiśeṣikaiḥ sāṅkhyārhatabauddhaiśca.

'Being beginningless and without qualities…' Beginninglessness is having no beginning — a beginning is a cause, and that which has none is beginningless. What has a beginning perishes in its own nature; but this, being beginningless, is without parts and so does not perish. Likewise because it is without qualities: what has qualities perishes through the loss of qualities; but this, being without qualities, does not perish. So this supreme Self is imperishable; there is no decay for it. Since this is so, though dwelling in the body — it is called dwelling in the body because the Self is perceived in bodies — even so it does no action; and because it does nothing it is not tainted by the fruit. For he who acts is tainted by the fruit of action; but this one is a non-agent and so is not tainted by fruit — that is the meaning. Who, then, acts in bodies and is tainted? If an embodied one other than the supreme Self acts and is tainted, then the oneness of the knower of the field and the Lord stated in 'and know me too as the knower of the field' (13.2) is wrongly stated. If there is no embodied one other than the Lord, it must be said who acts and who is tainted; or else that there is no supreme. Finding it in every way hard to understand and hard to explain, the Vaiśeṣikas, Sāṅkhyas, Jainas and Buddhists have abandoned the Upaniṣadic view taught by the Lord.

tatrāyaṃ parihāraḥ bhagavatā svenaiva uktaḥ “svabhāvastu pravartate” (5.14) iti. avidyāmātrasvabhāvo hi karoti lipyate iti vyavahāraḥ bhavati, na tu paramārthataḥ eva tasmin paramātmani tat asti. ataḥ eva etasmin paramārthasāṅkhyadarśane sthitānāṃ jñānaniṣṭhānāṃ paramahaṃsaparivrājakānāṃ tiraskṛtāvidyāvyavahārāṇāṃ karmādhikāraḥ nāsti iti tatra tatra darśitaṃ bhagavatā.

Here the Lord himself has given the answer: 'it is nature that works' (5.14). For 'it acts and is tainted' is the usage that arises from what is of the nature of ignorance alone; it is not really there in the supreme Self. And so the Lord has shown here and there that the paramahaṃsa renouncers, steadfast in knowledge, who abide in this vision of the supreme truth, the true Sāṅkhya, and have discarded the dealings of ignorance, are not qualified for action.

'As the one sun…' As the one sun, Savitṛ, Āditya, illumines, lights up, this whole world, so the one illumines the whole field, from the unmanifest and the great elements up to steadfastness. Who? The lord of the field, the supreme Self, that is, Bhārata. The illustration of the sun here makes two points about the Self: like the sun, the Self is one in all fields, and it is untainted.

Those who know, with the eye of knowledge, the distinction between the field and the knower of the field, and the release of beings from prakṛti, go to the Supreme.

This verse concludes the whole chapter:

kṣetrakṣetrayoḥ iti – kṣetrakṣetrajñayoḥ yathāvyākhyātayoḥ, evaṃ yathāpradarśitaprakāreṇa, antaram itaretaravailakṣaṇyaviśeṣaṃ, jñānacakṣuṣā śāstrācāryaprasādopadeśajanitam ātmapratyayikaṃ jñānaṃ cakṣuḥ, tena jñānacakṣuṣā, bhūtaprakṛtimokṣaṃ ca – bhūtānāṃ prakṛtiḥ avidyālakṣaṇā avyaktākhyā tasyāḥ bhūtaprakṛteḥ mokṣaṇam abhāvagamanaṃ ca, ye viduḥ jānanti, yānti gacchanti, te, paraṃ paramārthatattvaṃ brahma, na punaḥ deham ādadate ityarthaḥ.

'Those who thus know with the eye of knowledge the difference between the field and its knower…' Those who know, understand, thus, in the way shown, the difference — the particular distinction of each from the other — between the field and the knower of the field as explained, with the eye of knowledge — knowledge arising from the gracious teaching of scripture and teacher, the conviction of the Self, is the eye — and the liberation from the nature of beings — the nature of beings is ignorance, called the unmanifest, and liberation from it is its coming to nothing — they go, reach, the supreme, Brahman, the reality of the supreme truth; they do not take a body again, that is.

Thus, in the Bhagavad Gītā — the Upaniṣad, the knowledge of Brahman, the scripture of yoga, the dialogue of Śrī Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna — within the Bhīṣma Parva of the Mahābhārata, the collection of a hundred thousand verses by Vyāsa, ends the thirteenth chapter, Kṣetrakṣetrajñavibhāga Yoga.
