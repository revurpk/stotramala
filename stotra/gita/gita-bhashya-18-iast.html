Bhagavad Gītā · Chapter 18

Mokṣasannyāsa Yoga · liberation through renunciation · with Śaṅkara's bhāṣya

The summation: renunciation and relinquishment, the five factors of action, the threefold knowledge, action, agent, understanding, steadfastness and happiness, the duties born of nature, the way to Brahman, and the final counsel — 'abandoning all dharmas, take refuge in me alone.' At 18.66 Śaṅkara sets out his concluding survey of the whole teaching (śāstrārthopasaṃhāra), the longest passage of the bhāṣya. Arjuna declares his delusion gone, and Sañjaya closes the dialogue.

bhāṣya · the chapter's opening

sarvasyaiva gītāśāstrasya arthaḥ asminnadhyāye upasaṃhṛtya sarvaśca vedārtho vaktavya ityevamarthaḥ ayamadhyāyaḥ ārabhyate. sarveṣu hi atīteṣvadhyāyeṣu uktaḥ arthaḥ asminnadhyāye avagamyate. arjunastu sannyāsatyāgaśabdārthayoreva viśeṣambubhutsuḥ

This chapter begins in order to gather up the meaning of the whole teaching of the Gītā and to state the whole meaning of the Veda; for the meaning taught in all the preceding chapters is understood in this chapter. But Arjuna, wishing to know the distinction between the meanings of the words sannyāsa and tyāga, [said]:

arjuna uvāca

sannyāsasya mahābāho tattvamicchāmi veditum |
tyāgasya ca hṛṣīkeśa pṛthakkeśiniṣūdana
|| 1 ||

word by word · translation

sannyāsasya of renunciation mahā-bāho O mighty-armed tattvam the truth icchāmi I wish veditum to know tyāgasya of relinquishment ca and hṛṣīkeśa O Hṛṣīkeśa pṛthak separately keśi-niṣūdana O slayer of Keśin

Arjuna said: I wish to know the truth about renunciation, mighty-armed one, and about relinquishment, Hṛṣīkeśa, each distinctly, O slayer of Keśin.

bhāṣya

sannyāsasya iti. sannyāsa śabdārthasya ityetat, he mahābāho! tattvaṃ tasya bhāvastattvaṃ yāthātmyamityetat. icchāmi vedituṃ, jñātuṃ, tyāgasya ca tyāgaśabdārthasya ityetat, hṛṣīkeśa! pṛthak itaretara vibhāgataḥ keśiniṣūdana! keśināmā hayacchadmā kaścit asuraḥ taṃ niṣūditavān bhagavān vāsudevaḥ, tena tannāmnā sambodhyate arjunena.

'Of renunciation, mighty-armed…' Of renunciation — of the meaning of the word sannyāsa, that is — O mighty-armed, I wish to know the truth — its being, its true nature, that is; and of relinquishment — of the meaning of the word tyāga — O Hṛṣīkeśa, separately, distinguishing each from the other, O slayer of Keśin. Keśin was an asura disguised as a horse; the Blessed Lord Vāsudeva slew him, and so Arjuna addresses him by that name.

śrī bhagavānuvāca

kāmyānāṃ karmaṇāṃ nyāsaṃ sannyāsaṃ kavayo viduḥ |
sarvakarmaphalatyāgaṃ prāhustyāgaṃ vicakṣaṇāḥ
|| 2 ||

word by word · translation

kāmyānām desire-prompted karmaṇām of actions nyāsam the giving up sannyāsam as renunciation kavayaḥ the sages viduḥ understand sarva-karma-phala-tyāgam giving up the fruit of all actions prāhuḥ declare tyāgam relinquishment vicakṣaṇāḥ the wise

The Blessed Lord said: The sages understand renunciation to be the giving up of actions done from desire; the discerning declare relinquishment to be the giving up of the fruit of all actions.

bhāṣya

sannyāsa tyāga śabdau tatra tatra nirdiṣṭau, na nirluṭhitārthau pūrveṣu adhyāyeṣu. ataḥ arjunāya pṛṣṭavate tannirṇayāya.

The words sannyāsa and tyāga were used here and there in the earlier chapters, but their meanings were not sifted out. So, to settle them for Arjuna, who asked —

kāmyānāmiti. kāmyānāṃ aśvamedhādīnāṃ karmaṇāṃ nyāsaṃ parityāgaṃ sannyāsaṃ sannyāsaśabdārthaṃ, anuṣṭheyatvena prāptasya ananuṣṭhānaṃ kavayaḥ paṇḍitāḥ kecit viduḥ vijānanti. nityanaimittikānāṃ anuṣṭhīyamānānāṃ sarvakarmaṇāṃ ātmasambandhitayā prāptasya phalasya parityāgaḥ sarvakarmaphalatyāgaḥ taṃ prāhuḥ kathayanti tyāgaṃ tyāgaśabdārthaṃ vicakṣaṇāḥ paṇḍitāḥ. yadi kāmyakarmaparityāgaḥ phalaparityāgo vā arthaḥ vaktavyaḥ, sarvathā parityāgamātraṃ sannyāsatyāgaśabdayoḥ ekārthaḥ syāt, sa ghaṭapaṭaśabdāviva jātyantara – bhūtārthau.

'The sages understand renunciation as the giving up of desire-prompted actions…' Some sages, learned men, understand, know, renunciation — the meaning of the word sannyāsa — as the giving up, the abandonment, of desire-prompted actions such as the horse sacrifice: not performing what would come as something to be performed. The wise, the learned, declare relinquishment — the meaning of the word tyāga — to be the giving up of the fruit of all actions: the abandonment of the fruit, which comes as belonging to oneself, of all actions, obligatory and occasional, as they are performed. Whether the meaning to be stated is giving up desire-prompted actions or giving up fruit, in either case the meaning of the words sannyāsa and tyāga is simply giving up; they are not words of different kinds of meaning like 'pot' and 'cloth'.

nanu nitya naimittikānāṃ karmaṇāṃ phalameva nāstītyāhuḥ kathamucyate. teṣāṃ phalatyāgaḥ, yathā vandhyāyāḥ putra tyāgaḥ.

Objection: they say that obligatory and occasional actions have no fruit at all; how is giving up their fruit spoken of? It is like giving up the son of a barren woman.

naiṣa doṣaḥ, nityānāmapi karmaṇāṃ bhagavatā phalavattvasya iṣṭatvāt. vakṣyati hi bhagavān “aniṣṭamiṣṭaṃ miśraṃ ca” iti “na taṃ sannyāsināmiti” ca, sannyāsināmeva kevalaṃ karmaphalānubandhaṃ darśayan asannyāsināṃ nityakarmaphalaprāptiṃ “bhavatyatyāgināṃ pretye”ti darśayati.

This is no fault, since the Lord holds that obligatory actions too have fruit. For the Blessed Lord will say 'the undesired, the desired and the mixed' and 'but never for renouncers' (18.12); showing that only for renouncers is there no consequence of the fruit of action, he shows with 'it comes after death to those who have not relinquished' (18.12) that non-renouncers obtain the fruit of obligatory action.

tyājyaṃ doṣavadityeke karma prāhurmanīṣiṇaḥ |
yajñadānatapaḥkarma na tyājyamiti cāpare
|| 3 ||

word by word · translation

tyājyam to be given up doṣa-vat as an evil iti thus eke some karma action prāhuḥ say manīṣiṇaḥ thinkers yajña-dāna-tapaḥ-karma acts of sacrifice, charity and austerity na not tyājyam to be given up iti thus ca and apare others

Some wise men say that action should be given up as an evil; others that acts of sacrifice, giving and austerity should not be given up.

bhāṣya

tyājyamiti. tyājyaṃ tyaktavyaṃ, doṣavat doṣaḥ asyāstīti doṣavat. kiṃ tat? karma bandhahetutvātsarva meva. athavā, doṣaḥ yathā rāgādiḥ tyajyate. tathā tyājyamityeke karma prāhuḥ manīṣiṇaḥ paṇḍitāḥ sāṅkhyādi dṛṣṭimāśritāḥ, adhikṛtānāṃ karmiṇāmapīti. tatraiva yajñadānatapaḥkarma na tyājyamiti cāpare.

'Some thinkers say action should be given up as an evil…' To be given up, to be relinquished; evil — having an evil in it; what is? Action — all of it, being a cause of bondage. Or else: as an evil, such as passion, is given up, so action is to be given up — so say some thinkers, learned men holding the views of Sāṅkhya and the like, even for men of action who are qualified for it. And others say acts of sacrifice, charity and austerity are not to be given up.

karmiṇa evādhikṛtāḥ, tān apekṣya ete vikalpāḥ na tu niṣṭhān vyutthāyinaḥ sannyāsinaḥ apekṣya. “jñānayogena sāṅkhyānāṃ niṣṭhā mayā purā prokte”ti (3.3) karmādhikārāt apoddhṛtāḥ ye na tānprati cintā. “nanu karmayogena yogināṃ” (3.3) iti adhikṛtāḥ pūrvaṃ vibhaktaniṣṭhāḥ api iha sarvaśāstrārthopasaṃhāra – prakaraṇe yathā vicāryante, tathā sāṅkhyā api jñānaniṣṭhāḥ vicāryantāṃ iti. na, teṣāṃ mohaduḥkhanimitta tyāganupapatteḥ. nā kāyakleśanimittaṃ duḥkhaṃ sāṅkhyāḥ ātmani paśyanti, icchādīnāṃ kṣetradharmatvena e darśitatvāt. ataḥ te na kāyakleśaduḥkhabhayāt karma parityajanti. nāpi te karmāṇi ātmani paśyanti, yena niyataṃ karma mohāt parityajeyuḥ. guṇānāṃ karma “naiva kiñcit karomi” (5.8) iti hi te sannyasyanti. “sarvakarmāṇi manasā sannyasya” (5.13) ityādibhiḥ tattvavidaḥ sannyāsaprakāraḥ uktaḥ. tasmāt ye anye adhikṛtāḥ karmaṇi anātmavidaḥ, yeṣāṃ ca mohanimittaḥ tyāgaḥ sambhavati kāyakleśabhayācca, te eva tāmasāḥ tyāginaḥ rājasāśca iti nindyante, karmiṇāṃ anātmajñānāṃ karmaphalatyāgastutyartham. “sarvārambha parityāgī ...”, “maunī santuṣṭo yena kenacit. aniketaḥ sthiramatiḥ” (12.16'19) iti guṇātītalakṣaṇe ca paramārthasannyāsinaḥ viśeṣitatvāt. vakṣyati ca “niṣṭhā jñānasya yā parā” iti. (18.50) tasmāt jñānaniṣṭhāḥ sannyāsinaḥ na iha vivakṣitāḥ. karmaphalatyāgaḥ eva sāttvikatvena guṇena tāmasatvādyapekṣayā sannyāsaḥ ucyate, na mukhyaḥ sarvakarmasannyāsaḥ.

These alternatives concern only men of action, who are qualified for it, not those steadfast in knowledge, the renouncers who have risen above action. Those who were taken out of the sphere of qualification for action with 'the steadfastness of the Sāṅkhyas in the yoga of knowledge, declared by me of old' (3.3) are not considered here. Objection: just as those qualified, whose steadfastness was set apart earlier with 'that of the yogins in the yoga of action' (3.3), are considered here in the section summing up the meaning of the whole teaching, so let the Sāṅkhyas, steadfast in knowledge, also be considered. No: for them giving up out of delusion or pain is impossible. The Sāṅkhyas do not see in the Self the pain due to bodily toil, since desire and the rest have been shown to be attributes of the field; so they do not give up action out of fear of the pain of bodily toil. Nor do they see actions in the Self, so that they might give up prescribed action out of delusion. They renounce the action of the guṇas with 'I do nothing at all' (5.8); the knower's way of renunciation has been told in 'renouncing all actions with the mind' (5.13) and the like. Therefore those others, qualified for action, who do not know the Self, and in whom giving up from delusion and from fear of bodily toil is possible — they alone are censured as tāmasa and rājasa relinquishers, in order to praise the giving up of the fruit of action by men of action who do not know the Self. And the true renouncer has been specially described in the marks of one beyond the guṇas — 'renouncing all undertakings' and 'silent, content with whatever comes, homeless, steady of mind' (12.16, 19); and he will say, 'the supreme steadfastness in knowledge' (18.50). Therefore renouncers steadfast in knowledge are not meant here. Only the giving up of the fruit of action is called renunciation, by virtue of its sāttvika quality in contrast with the tāmasa and the rest — not the primary renunciation of all actions.

sarvakarmasannyāsāsambhave ca “na hi dehabhṛtā” (18.11) iti hetu vacanāt mukhya eva iti cet, na, hetuvacanasya stutyarthatvāt. yathā “tyāgācchāntiranantara” miti (12.12) karmaphalatyāga'stutireva yathoktāneka pakṣānuṣṭhānāśaktimantamarjunaṃ ajñaṃ prati vidhānāt, tathedamapi “na hi dehabhṛtā śakya” miti (18.11) karmaphalatyāga stutyarthaṃ na “sarvakarmāṇi manasā sannyasya naiva kurvannakārayannāste” (5.13) ityasya pakṣasya apavādaḥ kenaciddarśayituṃ śakyaḥ. tasmāt karmaṇyadhikṛtā npratyeva eṣa sannyāsatyāgavikalpaḥ. ye tu paramārthadarśinaḥ sāṅkhyāḥ teṣāṃ jñānaniṣṭhāyāmeva sarvakarmasannyāsalakṣaṇāyāṃ adhikāraḥ, nānyatreti na te vikalpārhāḥ. tacca upapāditamasmābhiḥ “vedāvināśinaṃ” (2.21) ityasmin pradeśe, tṛtīyādau ca.

Objection: since renunciation of all actions is impossible, as the reason 'for it is not possible for an embodied being' (18.11) states, the primary sense is meant. No: the statement of reason is for praise. Just as 'from relinquishment comes peace immediately' (12.12) is only praise of giving up the fruit of action, since it is enjoined for Arjuna, an ignorant man unable to follow the various courses described, so this too — 'for it is not possible for an embodied being' (18.11) — is for praise of giving up the fruit of action. No one can show it to be an exception to the position 'renouncing all actions with the mind, he dwells, neither acting nor causing action' (5.13). Therefore this alternative of renunciation and relinquishment concerns only those qualified for action. But the Sāṅkhyas, who see the supreme truth, are qualified only for steadfastness in knowledge, consisting in the renunciation of all action, and for nothing else; so they are not subject to the alternatives. We have established this at 'he who knows it to be indestructible' (2.21), and at the beginning of the third chapter.

niścayaṃ śṛṇu me tatra tyāge bharatasattama |
tyāgo hi puruṣavyāghra trividhassamprakīrtitaḥ
|| 4 ||

word by word · translation

niścayam conclusion śṛṇu hear me my tatra on that tyāge on relinquishment bharata-sattama O best of the Bharatas tyāgaḥ relinquishment hi for puruṣa-vyāghra O tiger among men tri-vidhaḥ threefold samprakīrtitaḥ is declared

Hear my decision about relinquishment, O best of the Bhāratas; for relinquishment, O tiger among men, is declared to be of three kinds.

bhāṣya

tatra eteṣu vikalpabhedeṣu –

Among these different alternatives —

niścayamiti. niścayaṃ śṛṇu avadhāraya, me mama, vacanāt, tatra tyāge tyāgasannyāsa vikalpe yathādarśite, bharatasattama! bharatānāṃ sādhutama! tyāgo hi tyāgasannyāsaśabdavācyo hi ya arthaḥ sa eka eveti abhipretyāha – “tyāgo hi” iti puruṣavyāghra. trividhaḥ triprakāraḥ tāmasādi prakāraiḥ samprakīrtitaḥ śāstreṣu samyak kathitaḥ. yasmāt tāmasādibhedena tyāgasannyāsa śabda vācyā'rtho adhikṛtasya karmiṇo'nātmajñasya trividhaḥ sambhavati na paramārthadarśinaḥ, ityayamartho durjñānaḥ tasmāt atra tattvaṃ nānyo vaktuṃ samarthaḥ. tasmānniściyaṃ paramārthaśāstrārtha viṣayaṃ adhyavasāyaṃ aiśvaraṃ me mattaḥ śṛṇu.

'Hear my conclusion…' Hear, grasp, my conclusion, from my words, about relinquishment there — the alternative of relinquishment and renunciation as shown — O best of the Bharatas. For relinquishment — meaning that the thing denoted by the words tyāga and sannyāsa is one — he says 'for relinquishment', O tiger among men, is declared, well stated in the scriptures, to be threefold, of three kinds, tāmasa and the rest. Since the thing denoted by tyāga and sannyāsa can be threefold, tāmasa and the rest, for the qualified man of action who does not know the Self, but not for one who sees the supreme truth — a matter hard to understand — no one else can tell the truth here; therefore hear from me my sovereign conclusion, the determination concerning the meaning of the scripture of the supreme truth.

yajñadānatapaḥkarma na tyājyaṃ kāryameva tat |
yajño dānaṃ tapaścaiva pāvanāni manīṣiṇām
|| 5 ||

word by word · translation

yajña-dāna-tapaḥ-karma acts of sacrifice, charity and austerity na not tyājyam to be given up kāryam to be done eva indeed tat that yajñaḥ sacrifice dānam charity tapaḥ austerity ca and eva indeed pāvanāni purifiers manīṣiṇām of the wise

Acts of sacrifice, giving and austerity should not be given up; they should be performed. Sacrifice, giving and austerity are purifiers of the wise.

bhāṣya

kaḥ punaḥ asau niścaya iti, āha –

What is that conclusion? He says:

yajña iti. yajñaḥ dānaṃ tapa ityetat trividhaṃ karma na tyājyaṃ na tyaktavyaṃ, kāryaṃ karaṇīyameva tat, kasmāt? yajñaḥ dānaṃ tapaścaiva pāvanāni viśuddhikarāṇi manīṣiṇāṃ phalānabhisandhīnāmityetat.

'Acts of sacrifice, charity and austerity are not to be given up…' Sacrifice, charity and austerity — this threefold action is not to be given up, not to be relinquished; it ought to be done. Why? Sacrifice, charity and austerity are purifiers, causes of purification, for the wise — for those who do not aim at fruit, that is.

kartavyānīti me pārtha niścitaṃ matamuttamam
|| 6 ||

word by word · translation

etāni these api even tu but karmāṇi actions saṅgam attachment tyaktvā giving up phalāni fruits ca and kartavyāni are to be done iti thus me my pārtha O Pārtha niścitam settled matam view uttamam highest

But even these actions should be performed giving up attachment and fruits. This, Pārtha, is my decided and highest view.

bhāṣya

etānyapi iti. etānyapi tu karmāṇi yajñadānatapāṃsi pāvanāni uktāni, saṅgaṃ āsaktiṃ teṣu, tyaktvā phalāni ca teṣāṃ parityajya kartavyāni iti anuṣṭheyānīti, me mama, niścitaṃ matamuttamam. “niścayaṃ śṛṇu me tatra” (18.4) iti pratijñāya, pāvanatvaṃ ca hetumuktvā, “etānyapi tu karmāṇi kartavyāni” iti etat “niścitaṃ matamuttamam” yiti pratijñātārthopasaṃhāra eva. na apūrvārthaṃ vacanam “etānyapi” iti prakṛtasannikṛṣṭārthatvopapatteḥ sāsaṅgasya phalārthino bandhahetavaḥ. “etānyapi karmāṇi mumukṣoḥ kartavyāni” iti apiśabdasya arthaḥ. na tu anyāni karmāṇi apekṣya “etānyapi” iti ucyate. anye tu varṇayanti – nityānāṃ karmaṇāṃ phalābhāvāt “saṅgaṃ tyaktvā phalāni” iti nopapadyate. ataḥ “etānyapi” yiti yāni kāmyāni karmāṇi nityebhyaḥ anyāni, etānyapi kartavyāni, kimuta yajñadānatapāṃsi nityāni iti, tadasat, nityānāmapi karmaṇāṃ yiha phalavattvasya upapāditatvāt “yajño dānaṃ tapaścaiva pāvanāni, ityādinā vacanena. nityānyapi karmāṇi bandhahetutvāśaṅkayā jihāsoḥ mumukṣoḥ kuta kāmyeṣu prasaṅgaḥ? “dūreṇa hyavaraṃ karma” (2.49) iti ca ninditatvāt, yajñārthātkarmaṇo'nyatra (3.9) iti ca kāmyakarmaṇāṃ bandhahetutvasya niścitatvāt, “traiguṇyaviṣayā vedāḥ” (2.45) “traividyā māṃ somapāḥ” (9.20) “kṣīṇe puṇye martyalokaṃ viśanti” (9.21) iti ca, dūravyavahitatvācca, na kāmyeṣu “etānyapi” iti vyapadeśaḥ.

'But even these actions…' But even these actions — sacrifice, charity and austerity, said to be purifiers — are to be done, practised, giving up attachment, clinging, to them, and abandoning their fruits: this is my settled and highest view. Having promised 'hear my conclusion about it' (18.4) and stated the reason, that they are purifying, 'even these actions are to be done… this is my settled and highest view' just concludes what was promised. 'Even these' is not a statement of something new, since it properly refers to what is under discussion and close at hand: they are causes of bondage for one with attachment who seeks fruit; 'even these actions are to be done by the seeker of liberation' is the meaning of 'even'. 'Even these' is not said in relation to other actions. Others explain: since obligatory actions have no fruit, 'giving up attachment and fruits' does not fit them; so 'even these' refers to desire-prompted actions, other than the obligatory — even these are to be done, how much more the obligatory sacrifice, charity and austerity. That is wrong, since it has been established here that obligatory actions too have fruit, by the words 'sacrifice, charity and austerity are purifiers'. For a seeker of liberation who wishes to give up even obligatory actions, suspecting them to be causes of bondage, how could desire-prompted actions come in? And since they are censured in 'action is far inferior' (2.49), since their being causes of bondage is settled in 'other than action for the sake of sacrifice' (3.9), and in 'the Vedas deal with the three guṇas' (2.45), 'knowers of the three Vedas, soma-drinkers' (9.20), 'when their merit is exhausted they enter the world of mortals' (9.21), and since they are far removed from the context, desire-prompted actions are not referred to by 'even these'.

niyatasya tu sannyāsaḥ karmaṇo nopapadyate |
mohāttasya parityāgastāmasaḥ parikīrtitaḥ
|| 7 ||

word by word · translation

niyatasya prescribed tu but sannyāsaḥ renunciation karmaṇaḥ of action na not upapadyate is proper mohāt out of delusion tasya of it parityāgaḥ the giving up tāmasaḥ tāmasa parikīrtitaḥ is declared

The renunciation of obligatory action is not proper; giving it up out of delusion is declared to be tāmasa.

bhāṣya

tasmādajñasyādhikṛtasya mumukṣoḥ –

Therefore, for the ignorant seeker of liberation who is qualified —

niyatasya iti. niyatasya tu nityasya sannyāsaḥ parityāgaḥ karmaṇo, nopapadyate, tasya pāvanatvasya iṣṭatvāt. mohāt ajñānāt tasya niyatasya parityāgaḥ – niyataṃ ca avaśyaṃ kartavyaṃ, tyajyate ca, iti hi pratiṣiddhaṃ. ato mohanimittaḥ parityāgaḥ tāmasaḥ parikīrtitaḥ mohaśca tamaḥ iti. kiñca –

'But renunciation of prescribed action is not proper…' But renunciation, abandonment, of prescribed, obligatory, action is not proper, since it is held to be purifying. Giving it up out of delusion, out of ignorance — what is prescribed must certainly be done, yet it is given up — is contradictory. So giving up caused by delusion is declared tāmasa, for delusion is tamas. Moreover —

duḥkhamityeva yatkarma kāyakleśa bhayāttyajet |
sa kṛtvā rājasaṃ tyāgaṃ naiva tyāgaphalaṃ labhet
|| 8 ||

word by word · translation

duḥkham painful iti as eva merely yat which karma action kāya-kleśa-bhayāt from fear of bodily toil tyajet one gives up saḥ he kṛtvā making rājasam a rājasa tyāgam relinquishment na not eva at all tyāga-phalam the fruit of relinquishment labhet would gain

He who gives up an action because it is painful, out of fear of bodily hardship, performs a rājasa relinquishment and does not gain its fruit.

bhāṣya

duḥkhamiti. duḥkhamityeva yatkarma kāyakleśabhayāt śarīraduḥkhabhayāt tyajet, sa kṛtvā rājasaṃ rajo nirvartyaṃ tyāgaṃ naiva tyāgaphalaṃ jñānapūrvakasya sarvakarmatyāgasya phalaṃ mokṣākhyaṃ na labhet na labheta.

'He who gives up action merely as painful…' He who gives up action merely as painful, out of fear of bodily toil, of physical pain, makes a rājasa relinquishment, produced by rajas, and does not gain the fruit of relinquishment — does not obtain the fruit called liberation that belongs to the renunciation of all action preceded by knowledge.

yasya whose na not ahaṅkṛtaḥ of 'I am the doer' bhāvaḥ the notion buddhiḥ understanding yasya whose na not lipyate is tainted hatvā slaying api though saḥ he imān these lokān worlds na not hanti slays na nor nibadhyate is bound

He who is free from the sense of 'I am the doer', whose understanding is not tainted — though he slays these people, he does not slay and is not bound.

bhāṣya

kaḥ punassumatiḥ yassamyakpaśyatītyucyate –

Who, then, is of good understanding, who sees rightly? It is said:

yasya iti. yasya śāstrācāryopadeśa nyāyasaṃskṛtātmanaḥ na bhavati ahaṃ kṛtaḥ “ahaṃ kartā” ityevaṃlakṣaṇaṃ bhāvaḥ bhāvanā pratyayaḥ – ete eva pañca adhiṣṭhānādayaḥ avidyayā ātmani kalpitāḥ sarvakarmaṇāṃ kartāraḥ, na ahaṃ ahaṃ tu tadvyāpārāṇāṃ sākṣibhūtaḥ “aprāṇo hyamanāḥ śubhro hyakṣarātparataḥ paraḥ” (muṃ.u.2.12) kevalaḥ avikriyaḥ ityevaṃ paśyati iti etat – buddhi antaḥkaraṇaṃ yasya ātmanaḥ upādhibhūtā na lipyate na anuśayinī bhavati – “idaṃ ahaṃ akārṣaṃ, tena ahaṃ narakaṃ gamiṣyāmi”, ityevaṃ yasya buddhiḥ na lipyate – saḥ sumatiḥ, saḥ paśyati. hatvā api saḥ imān lokān, sarvān imān prāṇinaḥ ityarthaḥ, na hanti hananakriyāṃ na karoti, na nibadhyate nāpi tatkāryeṇa adharmaphalena sambadhyate.

'He who is free from the notion of "I"…' He whose self is refined by the teaching of scripture and teacher and by reasoning, in whom there is no notion of 'I have done it', no conviction of 'I am the agent' — he sees thus: 'these five, the seat and the rest, imagined in the Self through ignorance, are the agents of all actions, not I; I am the witness of their activity, "without breath, without mind, pure, higher than the high imperishable" (Muṇḍaka 2.1.2), alone, changeless' — and whose understanding, the inner organ that is the Self's adjunct, is not tainted, does not repent, thinking 'I did this, and by it I shall go to hell' — he whose understanding is not tainted thus is of good understanding; he sees. Though he slays these worlds — all these living beings, that is — he does not slay, does not perform the act of slaying, and is not bound, not connected with its effect, the fruit of adharma.

nanu hatvā'pi na hanti iti vipratiṣiddhaṃ ucyate yadyapi stutiḥ? naiṣa doṣaḥ laukika pāramārthikadṛṣṭyapekṣayā tadupapatteḥ. dehādyātmabuddhyā hantā ahaṃ iti laukikīṃ dṛṣṭiṃ āśritya “hatvā'pi” iti āha, yathādarśitāṃ pāramārthikī dṛṣṭiṃ āśritya “na hanti na nibadhyate” iti etat ubhayaṃ upapadyate eva. nanu adhiṣṭhānādibhiḥ sambhūya karoti eva ātmā, “kartāramātmānaṃ kevalaṃ tu” (18.16) iti “kevala” śabdaprayogāt. naiṣa doṣaḥ ātmanaḥ avikriyasvabhāvatve adhiṣṭhānādibhiḥ saṃhatatvānupapatteḥ. vikriyāvataḥ hi anyaiḥ saṃhananaṃ sambhavati, saṃhatya vā kartṛtvaṃ syāt. na tu avikriyasya ātmanaḥ kenacit saṃhananaṃ asti iti na sambhūya kartṛtvaṃ upapadyate. ataḥ kevalatvaṃ ātmanaḥ svābhāvikam iti kevalaśabdaḥ anuvādamātram. avakriyatvaṃ ca ātmanaḥ śrutismṛtinyāya prasiddham. “avikāryo'yamucyate”, (2.25) “guṇaireva karmāṇi kriyante”, (3.27) “śarīra stho'pi na karoti”, (13.31) ityādi asakṛt upapāditaṃ gītāsu eva tāvat. śrutiṣu ca dhyāyatīva lelāyatīva (bṛ.u.43.7) ityevamādyāsu. nyāyataśca niravayavaṃ, aparatantraṃ, avikriyaṃ ātmatattvaṃ iti rājamārgaḥ. vikriyāvattvābhyupagame'pi ātmanaḥ svakīyā eva vikriyā svasya bhavituṃ arhati, na adhiṣṭhānādīnāṃ karmāṇi ātmakartṛkāṇi syuḥ na hi parasya karma pareṇa akṛtaṃ āgantuṃ arhati. yattu avidyayā gamitaṃ, na tat tasya, yathā rajatatvaṃ na śuktikāyāḥ, yathā vā talamalinatvaṃ bālaiḥ gamitaṃ avidyayā na ākāśasya tathā adhiṣṭhānādi vikriyā'pi teṣāṃ eva, na ātmanaḥ. tasmāt yuktaṃ uktam “ahaṅkṛtatatvabuddhi lepābhāvāt vidvān na hanti na nibadhyate” iti.

Objection: even as praise, 'though he slays he does not slay' is a contradiction. This is no fault, since it is explained by the difference between the worldly and the ultimate standpoints. Relying on the worldly standpoint — 'I am the slayer', from identifying the body and the rest with the Self — he says 'though he slays'; relying on the ultimate standpoint as shown, 'he does not slay and is not bound'; so both are explained. Objection: the Self does act, together with the seat and the rest, because of the use of the word 'alone' in 'sees the Self alone as the agent' (18.16). This is no fault: since the Self is changeless by nature, combination with the seat and the rest is impossible for it. Only what changes can combine with others, or be an agent after combining; but the changeless Self combines with nothing, so agency through combination is impossible for it. So being alone is natural to the Self, and the word 'alone' merely restates it. And the changelessness of the Self is well known from śruti, smṛti and reason. In the Gītā itself it has been established again and again: 'it is called changeless' (2.25), 'actions are done by the guṇas alone' (3.27), 'though dwelling in the body it does not act' (13.31); and in the śrutis, 'it seems to meditate, it seems to move' (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.3.7) and the like. And by reason, that the reality of the Self is partless, independent and changeless is the royal road. Even if the Self were admitted to change, its own change would belong to itself alone; the actions of the seat and the rest could not have the Self as their agent, for one's action cannot come to another who did not do it. What is attributed through ignorance does not belong to it — as silver does not belong to mother-of-pearl, or the surface and dirt attributed by children through ignorance do not belong to space; so the changes of the seat and the rest belong to them alone, not to the Self. Therefore it is rightly said that the knower, free of the notion of 'I have done' and of the taint of understanding, neither slays nor is bound.

“nāyaṃ hanti na hanyate” iti pratijñāya “na jāyate” ityādi hetuvacanena avikriyatvaṃ ātmanaḥ uktvā, “vedāvināśinaṃ” iti viduṣaḥ karmādhikāranivṛttiṃ śāstrādau saṅkṣepataḥ uktvā madhyaprasāritāṃ, tatra tatra prasaṅgaṃ kṛtvā iha upasaṃharati śāstrārtha piṇḍikaraṇāya “vidvān na hanti na nibadhyate” iti. evaṃ ca sati dehabhṛttvābhimānāpapattau avidyākṛtāśeṣakarma sannyāsopapatteḥ sannyāsināṃ aniṣṭādi trividhaṃ karmaṇaḥ phalaṃ na bhavati iti upapannaṃ, tadviparyayāt ca itareṣāṃ bhavati iti etat ca aparihāryaṃ iti eṣaḥ gītāśāstrārthaḥ upasaṃhṛtaḥ. sa eṣaḥ sarvavedārthasāraḥ nipuṇamatibhiḥ paṇḍitaiḥ vicārya pratipattavyaḥ iti tatra tatra prakaraṇavibhāgena darśitaḥ asmābhiḥ śāstranyāyānusāreṇa.

Having promised 'it does not slay and is not slain' (2.19), stated the changelessness of the Self with the reason 'it is not born' (2.20), and briefly told at the beginning of the teaching the knower's freedom from qualification for action with 'he who knows it to be indestructible' (2.21), and having spread this out in the middle, bringing it in at many points, he now concludes, to gather up the meaning of the teaching, with 'the knower neither slays nor is bound'. That being so, since identification with being embodied is absent, renunciation of all action, which is made by ignorance, is possible; so it is established that for renouncers there is no threefold fruit of action, undesired and the rest, while for others, the opposite, there is — and this is unavoidable. Thus the meaning of the teaching of the Gītā is concluded. This essence of the meaning of all the Vedas is to be understood after reflection by learned men of keen intellect; and we have shown it, section by section, in accordance with scripture and reasoning.

atha idānīṃ karmaṇāṃ pravartaka mucyate –

Now what impels actions is told:

'Knowledge, the known and the knower…' Knowledge — that by which something is known — is spoken of in general, as knowledge of any object; likewise the known, what is to be known, all of it, in general; likewise the knower, the enjoyer, characterised by adjuncts, imagined by ignorance — these three in general are the threefold, three kinds of, impulse to action, impelling all actions. For when knowledge and the other two come together, all action is begun, with avoiding, taking up or ignoring as its purpose. Then what is begun by the five, the seat and the rest, gathered into three groups according to whether it rests on speech, mind or body, is included in the three, the instrument and the rest; this is now told: the instrument — that by which something is done: outer, hearing and the rest; inner, understanding and the rest; the action — what the agent most wishes to obtain, reached by his activity; the agent — he who sets the instruments to work, characterised by adjuncts: so the basis of action is threefold, of three kinds. A basis is that in which something is gathered; the basis of action — action inheres in these three; so this is the threefold basis of action.

Hear these too duly, properly, according to scripture — knowledge and the rest, and their varieties made by the distinction of the guṇas; fix your mind on what is about to be said, that is.

So actions divided by scripture are also said to be divided by the guṇas.

Is it that one should not give up action because it cannot be given up entirely? Or is it that there is fault in giving up the action born with one? What follows? If the action born with one is not to be given up because it cannot be given up entirely, then it is established that giving it up entirely would be a merit. True, it is so. If it is said that giving it up entirely is simply impossible — is a person by nature ever in motion, like the guṇas of the Sāṅkhyas? Or is the action itself the factor, like the momentary aggregates of the Buddhists? On either view the complete giving up of action is impossible. There is also a third view: when a thing acts, it is active; when it does not act, that same thing is actionless; on this view action can be given up entirely. The special feature of this third view is: a thing is not ever in motion, nor is action itself the factor; rather, in an enduring substance a non-existent action arises and an existing one perishes, and the substance remains pure, possessed of power. So say the followers of Kaṇāda, and that substance is the factor. What fault is there in this view?

ayameva tu doṣaḥ – yatastvabhāgavataṃ matamidaṃ, kathaṃ jñāyate? yataḥ āha bhagavān “nāsato vidyate bhāvaḥ” (2.16) ityādi. kāṇādānāṃ hi asataḥ bhāvaḥ, sataścābhāvaḥ. iti, idaṃ matamabhāgavataṃ, abhāgavatamapi nyāyavaccet ko doṣaḥ iti cet, ucyate – doṣavattvidaṃ sarvapramāṇavirodhāt. kathaṃ? yadi tāvaddvyaṇukādi dravyaṃ prāgutpatteḥ atyantameva asat, utpannaṃ ca sthitaṃ kañcitkālaṃ punaḥ atyantameva asattvamāpadyate, tathā ca satyasadeva sat jāyate, sadeva asattvaṃ āpadyate, abhāvaḥ bhāvo bhavati, bhāvaścābhāvo bhavatīti, tatrābhāvo jāyamānaḥ prāgutpatteśśaśaviṣāṇakalpaḥ samavāyyasamavāyinimittākhyaṃ kāraṇamapekṣya jāyate iti, na caivamabhāva utpadyate, kāraṇaṃ cāpekṣate iti śakyaṃ vaktuṃ, asatāṃ śaśaviṣāṇādīnāmadarśanāt, bhāvātmakāścet ghaṭādayaḥ utpādyamānāḥ, kiñcit abhivyaktimātre kāraṇaṃ apekṣya utpadyante iti śakyaṃ pratipattuṃ. kiṃ ca, asataśca sadbhāve sataścāsadbhāve na kvacit pramāṇaprameyavyavahāreṣu viśvāsaḥ kasyacitsyāt, “satsadevāsadeveti” niścayānupapatteḥ. kiñca – utpadyate iti dvyaṇukādeḥ dravyasya svakāraṇasattāsambandhaṃ āhuḥ. prāk utpatteśca asat, paścāt kāraṇavyāpāraṃ apekṣya svakāraṇaiḥ paramāṇubhiḥ sattayā ca samavāyalakṣaṇena sambandhena sambadhyate. sambaddhaṃ sat kāraṇasamavetaṃ sat bhavati. tatra vaktavyaṃ kathaṃ asataḥ svaṃ kāraṇaṃ bhavet, sambandhaḥ vā kenacit syāt? na hi vandhyāputrasya svaṃ kāraṇaṃ, sambandhaḥ vā kenacit pramāṇataḥ kalpayituṃ śakyate.

This is the fault: this view is not the Lord's. How is that known? Because the Lord says 'the unreal has no being' (2.16) and so on. For the followers of Kaṇāda the non-existent comes into being and the existent goes out of being; so this view is not the Lord's. If it is said that, though not the Lord's, it is reasonable, what fault is there? It is said: it is faulty, because it conflicts with every means of knowledge. How? If substances such as the dyad are utterly non-existent before their arising, and once arisen and having lasted for some time become utterly non-existent again, then the non-existent becomes existent and the existent non-existent; non-being becomes being and being becomes non-being. Then non-being, being produced — like the hare's horn before its arising — would be produced depending on the causes called inherent, non-inherent and instrumental. But it cannot be said that non-being is produced thus and depends on causes, since non-existent things like the hare's horn are never seen to be produced. If pots and the like being produced are of the nature of being, it can be understood that they arise depending on a cause only for their mere manifestation. Moreover, if the non-existent came into being and the existent went out of being, no one would have any confidence in dealings with means and objects of knowledge, since no certainty 'the existent is existent, the non-existent non-existent' would be possible. Moreover, they say 'arises' means the dyad and other substances come into relation with their own causes and with existence: before arising it is non-existent; afterwards, depending on the activity of its causes, it is related by the relation of inherence to its own causes, the atoms, and to existence; so related, it becomes inherent in its causes and existent. Here it must be explained how the non-existent could have its own cause, or any relation with anything; for neither a cause of its own nor a relation with anything can be imagined, on any means of knowledge, for the son of a barren woman.

nanu naivaṃ vaiśeṣikaiḥ abhāvasya sambandhaḥ kalpyate. dvyaṇukādīnāṃ hi dravyāṇāṃ svakāraṇasamavāyalakṣaṇaḥ sambandhaḥ satāmeva ucyate iti. na; sambandhāt. prāk sattvānabhyupagamāt. na hi vaiśeṣikaiḥ kulāladaṇḍa cakrādivyāpārāt prāk ghaṭādīnāmastitvaṃ iṣyate. na ca mṛda eva ghaṭādyākāraprāpti – micchanti. tataśca asata eva sambandhaḥ pāriśeṣyādiṣṭo bhavati.

Objection: the Vaiśeṣikas do not imagine any relation of non-being. The relation of inherence in their own causes is said of the dyad and other substances only as existent. No: because existence is not admitted before the relation. The Vaiśeṣikas do not hold that pots and the like exist before the activity of the potter, stick, wheel and the rest; nor do they hold that clay itself takes the form of the pot. So, by elimination, the relation must be held to be of the non-existent itself.

nanu asato'pi samavāyalakṣaṇaḥ sambandhaḥ na viruddhaḥ. na, vandhyāputrādīnāmadarśanāt. ghaṭādereva prāgabhāvasya svakāraṇa sambandho bhavatīti na vandhyāputrādeḥ, abhāvasya tulyatve'pi iti viśeṣaḥ abhāvasya vaktavyaḥ, ekasya abhāvaḥ, dvayoḥ abhāvaḥ sarvasyābhāvaḥ, prāgabhāvaḥ pradhvaṃsābhāvaḥ, itaretarābhāvaḥ, atyantābhāvaḥ iti ca lakṣaṇato na kenacidviśeṣo darśayituṃ śakyaḥ. asati ca viśeṣe ghaṭasya prāgabhāva eva kulālādibhirghaṭabhāvamāpadyate sambadhyate ca, bhāvena, kapālākhyena, svakāraṇena sambaddhaśca sarvavyavahārayogyaśca bhavati, na tu ghaṭasyaiva pradhvaṃsādyabhāvaḥ abhāvatve satyapi iti pradhvaṃsā bhāvānāṃ na kvacit vyavahārayogyatvam prāgabhāvasyaiva dvyaṇukādidravyākhyasya utpattyādi – vyavahārārhatvaṃ ityedasamañjasaṃ, abhāvatvāviśeṣāt atyantapradhvaṃsābhāvayoriva. nanu naiva asmābhiḥ prāgabhāvasya bhāvāpattiḥ ucyate. bhāvasyaiva tarhi bhāvāpattiḥ, yathā ghaṭasya ghaṭāpattiḥ paṭasya vā paṭāpattiḥ etadapi abhāvasya bhāvāpatti vadeva pramāṇa viruddhaṃ.

Objection: a relation of inherence is not contradictory even for the non-existent. No: because it is not seen for the son of a barren woman and the like. If the prior non-existence of the pot alone is related to its own cause, and not that of the barren woman's son, though both are equally non-being, a difference in non-being must be stated. But no distinction by mark can be shown between non-being of one, of two, of all, prior non-being, posterior non-being, mutual non-being and absolute non-being. And in the absence of any distinction, it would be the prior non-being of the pot alone that, through the potter and the rest, becomes the pot and is related to an existent, its own cause called the potsherds, and so related becomes fit for every dealing; while the posterior non-being of the same pot, though equally non-being, does not; so posterior non-beings are never fit for dealings, while only prior non-being, called the dyad and other substances, is fit for dealings such as arising — this is incongruous, since they are equally non-being, like absolute and posterior non-being. Objection: we do not say that prior non-being becomes being. Then being becomes being, as a pot becomes a pot or a cloth becomes a cloth; this too conflicts with the means of knowledge just like non-being becoming being.

sāṅkhyasyāpi yaḥ pariṇāmapakṣaḥ so'pi apūrvadharmotpattivināśāṅgī karaṇāt vaiśeṣika – pakṣānna viśiṣyate, abhivyaktitirobhāvāṅgīkaraṇe'pi abhivyaktitirobhāvayoḥ vidyamānatvāvidyamānatvanirūpaṇe pūrvavadeva pramāṇavirodhaḥ. etena kāraṇasyaiva saṃsthāna mutpattyādi ityetadapi pratyuktam. pāriśeṣyāt – sat ekameva vastvavidyayotpattivināśādidharmaiḥ anekadhā naṭavat vikalpyate iti. idaṃ bhāgavataṃ mataṃ uktaṃ, “nāsato vidyate bhāvaḥ” (2.16) ityasmin śloke, satpratyayasya avyabhicārāt vyabhicārācca itareṣāmiti.

The Sāṅkhyas' doctrine of transformation also does not differ from the Vaiśeṣika view, since it admits the arising and destruction of new attributes. Even if manifestation and disappearance are admitted, when one determines whether manifestation and disappearance existed or not, there is conflict with the means of knowledge as before. This also refutes the view that arising and the rest are only a configuration of the cause. By elimination: the existent, which is one only, is imagined through ignorance in many ways, like an actor, with the attributes of arising, destruction and the rest. This is the Lord's view, stated in the verse 'the unreal has no being' (2.16), because the awareness of the existent never fails, while the others fail.

kathaṃ tarhyātmano avikriyatve aśeṣataḥ karmaṇastyāgo nopapadyata iti? yadi vastubhūtāḥ guṇāḥ. yadi vā avidyā kalpitāḥ taddharmaḥ karma, tadātmanyavidyayādhyāropitamevetyavidvān) “na hi kaścit kṣaṇamapi aśeṣatastyaktuṃ śaknoti” (3.5) ityuktam. vidvāṃstu punaḥ vidyayā avidyāyāṃ nivṛttāyāṃ śaknotyevāśeṣataḥ karma parityaktuṃ avidyā'dhyāropitasya śeṣānupapatteḥ. na hi taimirikadṛṣṭyā adhyāropitasya dvicandrādestimirāpagame'pi śeṣaḥ avatiṣṭhate, evaṃ ca sati idaṃ vacanamupapannaṃ “sarva karmāṇi” manasā” (5.13) ityādi, “sve sve karmaṇyabhiratassaṃsiddhiṃ labhate naraḥ”, (18.45) “sakarmaṇā tamabhyarcya siddhiṃ vindati mānavaḥ” (18.46) iti ca.

How then, given the changelessness of the Self, is the complete giving up of action impossible? Whether the guṇas are real things or imagined by ignorance, action is their attribute and is superimposed on the Self by ignorance; so of the ignorant man it was said 'no one can give it up entirely even for a moment' (3.5). But the knower, when ignorance has been removed by knowledge, can give up action entirely, since nothing superimposed by ignorance can remain. For when the eye disease is gone, nothing remains of the double moon and the like superimposed by diseased vision. This being so, these words are explained: 'renouncing all actions with the mind' (5.13), 'each devoted to his own action, a man attains perfection' (18.45), and 'worshipping him with his own action, a man attains perfection' (18.46).

Learn from me in brief, son of Kuntī, how, having attained perfection, he attains Brahman — that highest consummation of knowledge.

bhāṣya

pūrvoktena svakarmānuṣṭhānena īśvarābhyarcanarūpeṇa janitāṃ prāguktalakṣaṇāṃ siddhiṃ prāptasyotpannātmavivekajñānasya kevalātmajñānaniṣṭhārūpā naiṣkarmyalakṣaṇā siddhiḥ yena krameṇa bhavati, tadvaktavyamityāha –

siddhimiti. siddhiṃ prāptaḥ svakarmaṇeśvaraṃ samabhyarcya tatprasādajāṃ kāyendriyāṇāṃ jñānaniṣṭhāyogyatālakṣaṇāṃ siddhiṃ prāptaḥ – siddhiṃ prāptaḥ iti tadanuvāda uttarārthaḥ. kiṃ taduttaraṃ yadarthaḥ anuvādaḥ iti ucyate – yathā yena prakāreṇa jñāniniṣṭhārūpeṇa brahma paramātmānaṃ āpnoti. tathā taṃ prakāraṃ jñānaniṣṭhāprāptikramaṃ me mama vacanāt nibodha tvaṃ niścayena avadhāraya ityetat, kiṃ vistareṇa? na ityāha – samāsenaiva saṅkṣepeṇaiva, he kaunteya, yathā brahma prāpnoti tathā nibodheti anena yā pratijñatā brahmaprāptiḥ tāṃ idantayā darśayitumāha – “niṣṭhā jñānasya yā parā” iti. niṣṭhā paryavasānaṃ parisamāptiḥ ityetat, kasya brahmajñānasya, yā parā, sā kīdṛśī? yādṛśaṃ ātma jñānaṃ kīdṛk tat? yādṛśaḥ ātmā? kīdṛśa saḥ? yādṛśo bhagavatā uktaḥ, (18.17) upaniṣadvā kyaiśca nyāyataśca.

'How one who has attained perfection attains Brahman…' Having attained perfection — having worshipped the Lord with his own action, having attained the perfection, born of his grace, that consists in the fitness of body and senses for steadfastness in knowledge — 'having attained perfection' restates this for the sake of what follows. What follows, for which it is restated? It is said: how, in what way — in the way of steadfastness in knowledge — he attains Brahman, the supreme Self; learn that way, the stages of attaining steadfastness in knowledge, from my words; understand it with certainty, that is. At length? No, he says: in brief, concisely, Kaunteya. With 'learn how he attains Brahman' he promised the attainment of Brahman; to show it as 'this', he says: 'which is the supreme steadfastness in knowledge'. Steadfastness is culmination, completion; of what? Of the knowledge of Brahman, which is supreme. What is it like? Like the knowledge of the Self. What is that like? Like the Self. What is that like? As the Lord has described it (18.17), and as the Upaniṣadic texts and reasoning describe it.

nanu viṣayākāraṃ jñānaṃ. na jñānaviṣayaḥ nāpyākāravān ātmeṣyate kvacit. nanuḥ “ādityavarṇaṃ” (śve.u.3.8) “bhārūpaḥ” (chāṃ.u.3.14.2) “svayañjyotiḥ” (bṛ.u. 4.3.9) ityākāra vattvamātmanaḥ śrūyate. na; tamorūpatva pratiṣedhārthatvāt teṣāṃ vākyānām – dravyaguṇādyākārapratiṣedhe ātmanaḥ tamorūpatve prāpte tatpratiṣedhārthāni “ādityavarṇaṃ” ityādīni vākyāni. “arūpaṃ” (kaṭha. 3.15) iti ca viśeṣataḥ rūpa pratiṣedhāt. aviṣayatvāccha – “na sandṛśe tiṣṭhati rūpamasya na cakṣuṣā paśyati kaścanainaṃ. Objection: knowledge takes the form of its object; but the Self is nowhere held to be an object of knowledge or to have form. Objection: but form is heard of the Self in 'sun-coloured' (Śvetāśvatara 3.8), 'whose form is light' (Chāndogya 3.14.2), 'self-luminous' (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.3.9). No: those texts are to deny that the Self is of the nature of darkness. When forms such as substance and quality are denied of the Self, it might be thought to be of the nature of darkness; texts like 'sun-coloured' are to deny that. And because form is specifically denied in 'formless' (Kaṭha 3.15); and because it is not an object — 'his form does not stand within the field of vision; no one sees him with the eye' (Śvetāśvatara 4.20), 'soundless, touchless' (Kaṭha 3.15) and so on. So knowledge in the form of the Self is impossible. How then is there knowledge of the Self? All knowledge takes the form of whatever is its object, and the Self has been said to be formless; if both knowledge and the Self are formless, how is there steadfastness in contemplating it? No difficulty: because the Self is utterly stainless, utterly clear and utterly subtle. And since the understanding is stainless like the Self, it can appear in the form of the consciousness of the Self. The mind reflects the understanding, the senses reflect the mind, and the body reflects the senses; so worldly people see the Self in the body alone.

dehacaitanyavādinaśca lokāyatikāḥ “caitanyaviśiṣṭaḥ kāyaḥ puruṣaḥ” ityāhuḥ. tathā cānye indriyacaitanyavādinaḥ, anye manaścaitanyavādinaḥ, anye buddhicaitanyavādinaḥ tato'pyāntaramavyaktamavyākṛtākhyaṃ avidyāvasthamātmatvena pratipannāḥ kecit. sarvatra buddhyādidehānte ātmacaitanyā bhāsatā ātmabhrāntikāraṇamityataśca ātmaviṣayaṃ jñānaṃ na vidhātavyaṃ, kiṃ tarhi? nāmarūpādya nātmādhyāropaṇa nivṛttirevakāryā. nātmacaitanyavijñānaṃ kāryaṃ avidyā'dhyāropitasarvapadārthākāraiḥ eva viśiṣṭatayā dṛśyamānatvāt iti. ata eva hi vijñānavādino bauddhāḥ vijñānavyatirekeṇa vastvenāstīti pratipannāḥ, pramāṇāntara nirapekṣatāṃ ca svasaṃviditatvābhyupagamena. tasmādavidyā'dhyāropita nirākaraṇamātraṃ brahmaṇi kartavyaṃ, na tu brahmavijñāne yatnaḥ, atyantaprasiddhatvāt. avidyākalpitanāmarūpaviśeṣākārapahṛtabuddhīnāṃ atyantaprasiddhaṃ suvijñeyaṃ āsannataramātmabhūtamapyaprasiddhaṃ durvijñeyamatidūramanyadiva ca pratibhāti avivekināṃ. bāhyākāranivṛtta buddhīnāṃ tu labdhagurvātmaprasādānāṃ nātaḥ paraṃ sukhaṃ suprasiddhaṃ suvijñeyaṃ svāsannataramasti, tathācoktaṃ – “pratyakṣāvagamaṃ dharmya” (9.2) mityādi.

And the materialists, who hold that consciousness belongs to the body, say 'the person is the body qualified by consciousness'. Likewise others hold that consciousness belongs to the senses, others to the mind, others to the understanding; and some take as the Self what is still more inward, the unmanifest, called the undifferentiated, the state of ignorance. In every case, from the understanding to the body, the reflection of the consciousness of the Self is the cause of mistaking them for the Self. So knowledge of the Self is not to be enjoined; rather, only the removal of the superimposition of what is not the Self, name, form and the rest, is to be done. The knowledge of the consciousness of the Self is not something to be produced, since it is always seen qualified by the forms of all the things superimposed by ignorance. That is why the Buddhists who hold that consciousness alone exists concluded that there is no thing apart from consciousness, and that it needs no other means of knowledge, since they admit it is self-revealed. So with regard to Brahman only the removal of what ignorance has superimposed is to be done, and no effort is needed for knowing Brahman, since it is utterly evident. To the undiscriminating, whose understanding is carried away by the particular forms of name and form imagined by ignorance, the Self, though utterly evident, easily known, nearer than anything and their very Self, appears unknown, hard to know, very far away, as if something else. But for those whose understanding has turned away from outer forms, who have gained the grace of teacher and self, there is nothing more joyful, more evident, more easily known or nearer than this. And so it has been said: 'known directly, righteous' (9.2) and so on.

But some who think themselves learned say: 'because the Self is formless, the understanding does not reach it; so steadfastness in right knowledge is hard to achieve'. True — for those without the tradition of the teachers, who have not heard the Vedānta, whose understanding is utterly attached to outer objects, and who have not laboured over the right means of knowledge. But for those of the opposite kind it is the conviction of reality in the worldly duality of grasped and grasper that is very hard to produce, since nothing other than the consciousness of the Self is perceived; and it is just so, not otherwise, as we have said; and the Lord has said, 'where beings are awake, that is night for the sage who sees' (2.69). So only the turning away of the understanding from the distinctions of outer forms is the means of resting in the nature of the Self. For the Self is never unknown to anyone, nor something to be reached, avoided or taken up. If that Self were unknown, all activities for oneself would become pointless; and it cannot be imagined that they are for the sake of the body and other non-conscious things; nor is pleasure for pleasure's sake or pain for pain's sake. And all dealings end in knowing the Self (Brahmasūtra 3.4.26–27). So, just as no other means of knowledge is needed to know one's own body, still less is any other needed to know the Self, which is more inward than that. So it is established that steadfastness in knowledge of the Self is evident to the discriminating.

yeṣāmapi nirākāraṃ jñānaṃ apratyakṣaṃ teṣāmapi jñānavaśenaiva jñeyāvagatiriti jñānamatyanta prasiddhaṃ sukhādidevetyabhyupaṅgatavyaṃ. jijñāsānupapatteśca aprasiddhaṃ cet, jñānaṃ jñeyavat jijñāsyeta. yathā jñeyaṃ ghaṭādilakṣaṇaṃ jñānena jñātā vyāptumicchati tathā jñānamapi jñānāntareṇa jñātā vyāptumicchet. na caitadasti, ataḥ atyantaprasiddhaṃ jñānaṃ, jñātā'pi ata eva prasiddhaḥ iti. tasmād jñāne yatno na kartavyaḥ, kiṃ tu anātmani ātmabuddhi nivṛttāneva. tasmāt jñānaniṣṭhā susampādyā.

Even those for whom formless knowledge is not directly perceived must admit that the known is understood only through knowledge, and so that knowledge is utterly evident, like pleasure and the rest. And because a desire to know it is impossible: if knowledge were unknown, it would be sought to be known like the known. As the knower wishes to reach the known, such as a pot, by knowledge, so he would wish to reach knowledge by another knowledge. But that is not so. So knowledge is utterly evident, and for that very reason the knower too is evident. Therefore no effort is to be made regarding knowledge, but only regarding the removal of the notion of the Self in what is not the Self. So steadfastness in knowledge is easy to achieve.

Objection: it is contradictory to say 'by that which is the supreme culmination of knowledge he knows me'. How so? Whenever knowledge of an object arises in a knower, he knows that object then and there; he does not need steadfastness in knowledge, consisting in repetition of knowledge. So it would be saying that he knows not by knowledge but by repetition of knowledge, by steadfastness in it. This is no fault: the word 'steadfastness' expresses the culmination in the certainty of experiencing the Self, of knowledge that has the causes of its own arising and maturing and is free of opposition. Knowledge of the oneness of the knower of the field and the supreme Self — produced by the teaching of scripture and teacher, depending on the causes of the arising and maturing of knowledge and on the auxiliary causes, purity of understanding and the qualities such as humility (13.7), and accompanied by the renunciation of all actions, which are rooted in the notion of distinct factors such as agency — its abiding in the form of the certain experience of one's own Self is called the supreme steadfastness in knowledge. This steadfastness in knowledge has been called the supreme, fourth devotion, in relation to the threefold devotion of the distressed and the rest (7.17). By that supreme devotion one knows the Lord in truth, immediately upon which the notion of difference between the Lord and the knower of the field ceases entirely. So the statement 'by devotion consisting in steadfastness in knowledge he knows me' is not contradictory. And then all the scripture that enjoins withdrawal — Vedānta, Itihāsa, Purāṇa and smṛti — well established by reasoning, becomes meaningful: 'having known… rising above, they live the life of a mendicant' (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 3.5.1); 'therefore they call renunciation the highest of these austerities' (Mahānārāyaṇa 24.1); 'renunciation alone excelled' (Mahānārāyaṇa 21.2); 'renunciation is the laying down of actions' (18.2); 'giving up the Vedas, this world and the next' (Āpastamba Dharmasūtra 2.23.13); 'give up dharma and adharma' (Śāntiparvan 329.40, 331.44); and the texts shown here (5.13). These texts cannot rightly be pointless, nor are they mere arthavāda, since they stand in their own context, and since liberation consists in being steadfast in the changeless nature of the inmost Self. One who wishes to go to the eastern ocean cannot share a path with one who wishes to go the opposite way, to the western ocean. Steadfastness in knowledge is the absorption in producing a continuous stream of awareness of the inmost Self, and it is opposed to coexisting with action, like going to the western ocean; those who know the means of knowledge have determined that their opposition is as wide as that between a mountain and a mustard seed. Therefore it is established that steadfastness in knowledge is to be practised only through the renunciation of all action.

What is the result of this inquiry? Just this: determining which of them is the means to the supreme highest good; so it must be examined at some length. The knowledge of the Self alone is the cause of the highest good, because by removing the notion of difference it culminates in the fruit of aloneness. The notion of distinctions of action, factors and fruit is always at work in the Self through ignorance: 'my action; I am the agent; I will do this action for that fruit' — this ignorance has been at work from beginningless time. What removes this ignorance is knowledge about the Self in the form 'this I am — alone, non-agent, actionless, without fruit; there is no one other than me'; when it arises it removes the notion of difference which is the cause of engaging in action. The word 'tu' excludes two positions: the highest good is attained neither by actions alone nor by knowledge and action combined. And since the highest good is not something to be produced, it cannot have action as its means; for what is eternal is not made either by action or by knowledge.

Then knowledge alone is also useless? No: since it removes ignorance, it is seen to culminate in the fruit of aloneness. Knowledge that removes the darkness of ignorance is seen to culminate in the fruit of aloneness, like the light of a lamp that removes the darkness of ignorance of a snake and the like regarding a rope: the fruit of the light ends in the rope alone, with the imagined snake removed; so with knowledge. And just as, in acts with seen results such as cutting or kindling fire by friction, the agent and other factors engaged cannot turn to another act or another fruit apart from the result, the splitting in two or the appearance of fire, so in the act of steadfastness in knowledge, which has a seen result (readings: 'in the actionless act of steadfastness in knowledge', 'in actionless steadfastness in knowledge'), the factors such as the knower, being engaged, cannot turn to another action apart from the fruit, the aloneness of the Self. So steadfastness in knowledge combined with action is impossible. If it be said it is like eating and the agnihotra [done by the same person], no: when knowledge whose fruit is aloneness has been gained, desire for the fruit of action is impossible. When knowledge whose fruit is aloneness has been gained, there can be no desire for another fruit or for action that is its means — as one who has water flooding everywhere has no desire for the fruit of wells and tanks. One engaged in action whose fruit is gaining a kingdom does not busy himself with action whose fruit is gaining a mere field, or desire it. Therefore action is not a means to the highest good; nor are knowledge and action combined; nor does knowledge, whose fruit is aloneness, need the help of action, since being the remover of ignorance it is opposed to action; darkness does not remove darkness. So knowledge alone is the means to the highest good.

Some say: obligatory actions, being painful, are themselves the fruit of sinful actions done before, and have no fruit beyond their own nature, since none is declared in scripture and they are enjoined with living and the like as occasion. No: actions that have not started cannot give fruit, and the particular painful fruit would be inexplicable. The claim that the pain of the toil of performing obligatory actions is experienced as the fruit of sinful actions of a previous birth is wrong. It does not stand to reason that the fruit of an action which had not sprouted at the time of death to give fruit should be experienced in a birth begun by another action; otherwise, in a birth begun by the agnihotra and the like for enjoying heaven, the experience of the fruit of hell would not be impossible. And it cannot be the particular painful fruit of sins: when there are many possible sins with fruits of different means of pain, to imagine that their fruit is only the pain of the toil of obligatory rites, while afflictions such as the pairs of opposites and disease are without cause, is impossible — that only the pain of the toil of obligatory rites, and not the pain of carrying stones on the head and the like, is the fruit of past sin. And this is beside the point: the point at issue was that past sin whose fruit has not come forth cannot be exhausted; you speak of the pain of obligatory rites as the fruit of action whose fruit has come forth, not of that whose fruit has not. If you think all past sin has already come forth in fruit, then restricting its fruit to the pain of obligatory rites is unjustified; and the injunction of obligatory action would become pointless, since sin whose fruit has come forth is exhausted by experience anyway. Moreover, if pain is the fruit of the enjoined obligatory rite, it is seen to come from the toil of performing it, like exercise; to imagine it as the fruit of something else is unwarranted. And since obligatory rites are enjoined with living and the like as occasion, they cannot be the fruit of past sin, any more than an expiation: the expiation enjoined on the occasion of a sin is not the fruit of that sin. If the pain of the expiation were the fruit of the sin that occasions it, then the pain of performing obligatory rites, occasioned by living and the like, would be the fruit of living and the like, since obligatory rites and expiations are equally occasional. Moreover, since the toil of performing the agnihotra and the like is the same whether obligatory or desire-prompted, there is no difference by which only the pain of obligatory rites, and not that of desire-prompted ones, would be the fruit of past sin; so the latter too would be the fruit of past sin. And so the presumption — that because no fruit of obligatory rites is declared and their injunction is otherwise inexplicable, the pain of performing them is the fruit of past sin — is unwarranted; rather, from the injunction being otherwise inexplicable, a fruit other than the pain of toil is to be inferred for obligatory rites. And there is a contradiction: if it is held that by performing obligatory action the fruit of another action is experienced, that experience itself is the fruit of the obligatory action, and to say obligatory action has no fruit is contradictory. Moreover, when the desire-prompted agnihotra is performed, the obligatory agnihotra is performed along with it in the same procedure; so the fruit of the desire-prompted agnihotra would be used up by the pain of its toil. If the fruit of the desire-prompted agnihotra is something else, heaven and the like, then the pain of its toil would also have to be different; but it is not, since that contradicts experience — the pain of performing the obligatory alone is not seen to differ from that of the desire-prompted. Moreover, action neither enjoined nor forbidden gives its fruit at the time; but action enjoined or forbidden by scripture does not give its fruit at the time. Otherwise there would be no exertion for heaven and the like on the strength of teaching about unseen fruit — with the agnihotra and the rest the same in nature, the obligatory being used up by the mere pain of toil and the desire-prompted having the great fruit of heaven merely by the desire for fruit, without any excess of auxiliaries or procedure. Therefore it is never possible that obligatory actions have no unseen fruit.

And therefore knowledge alone, not the performance of obligatory rites, is the cause of the complete destruction of action, good or bad, that is based on ignorance; for all action has ignorance and desire as its seed. So it has been established that action concerns the ignorant, and steadfastness in knowledge, preceded by the renunciation of all action, concerns the knower: 'neither of them knows' (2.19), 'he who knows it to be indestructible, eternal' (2.21), 'that of the Sāṅkhyas in the yoga of knowledge, that of the yogins in the yoga of action' (3.3), 'of the ignorant attached to action' (3.26), 'the knower of the truth, mighty-armed… thinking "the guṇas move among the guṇas", is not attached' (3.28), 'renouncing all actions with the mind he dwells' (5.13), 'the disciplined knower of the truth thinks "I do nothing at all"' (5.8) — by implication the ignorant man thinks 'I do'; 'for one wishing to ascend, action is the means; for one who has ascended, abiding in yoga, calm alone is the means' (6.3); 'noble' — all three ignorant ones — 'but the man of knowledge is my very Self' (7.18); 'the ignorant men of action, desiring desires, obtain going and coming' (9.21); 'those who, thinking of nothing else, ever disciplined, worship' the Self as described, like space, stainless (9.22); 'I give the yoga of understanding by which they come to me' (10.10) — by implication the ignorant men of action do not come; those who do the Lord's work, even if most disciplined, are ignorant men of action, whose means descend by stages, ending with giving up fruit (12.6–11). But those who meditate on the indefinable imperishable have the means told from 'without hatred towards all beings' to the end of that chapter (12.13–20), and the means of knowledge told in the chapter on the field and after (13.7–11, 14.22–26, 15.3–5). For those who renounce all actions, which have the five, the seat and the rest, as their causes (18.14), who have knowledge of the oneness and non-agency of the Self, who abide in the supreme steadfastness in knowledge, who know the reality of the Lord — for the paramahaṃsa wandering monks alone, whose refuge is the realised oneness of the Self with the Lord's nature — the threefold fruit of action, undesired and the rest (18.12), does not come; it does come to the others, the ignorant men of action who are not renouncers. This is the division of what is to be done as taught in the teaching of the Gītā.

If it is said that it is not established that all action is done through ignorance, no: it is like killing a brāhmaṇa and the like. Although obligatory action is known from scripture, it belongs only to one who has ignorance. Just as action such as killing a brāhmaṇa, though known from prohibitive scripture, causes harm and belongs to one with the faults of ignorance, desire and the like — otherwise there could be no engaging in it — so too obligatory, occasional and desire-prompted actions. If it is said that for one who does not know the Self as distinct from the body, engaging in obligatory and other actions is impossible, no: we see people engaging with the thought 'I do' in action that is movement, whose agent is not the Self. If it is said that the notion of 'I' in the aggregate of body and the rest is figurative, not false, no: then its effects too would be figurative. It may be said that the notion of 'I' in the aggregate of body and the rest, which belongs to oneself, is figurative, as with one's own son — 'you are myself, named son' (Taittirīya Saṃhitā 2.11) — and as in the world 'this cow is my very life'; so it is not a false notion. A false notion is that about a post and a man whose distinguishing features are not grasped. But a figurative notion does not serve the purpose of the literal effect; it serves to praise its subject, the word of comparison being dropped. As in 'Devadatta is a lion' or 'the boy is fire' — like a lion, like fire, through common features such as fierceness or tawniness — it only serves to praise its subject, Devadatta or the boy; nothing of a lion's or fire's work is accomplished by the figurative word and notion. But one experiences the harmful effect of a false notion. One knows the object of a figurative notion: 'this Devadatta is not a lion', 'this boy is not fire'. Likewise action done by the figurative self, the aggregate of body and the rest, would not be done by the literal Self, the object of the notion of 'I'. For action done by a figurative lion or fire is not done by a real lion or fire; nor does fierceness or tawniness do any work of the real lion or fire, being used up in praise. And those praised know 'I am not a lion, I am not fire' — not 'the lion's action is mine, or fire's'. So the notion 'the action of the aggregate is not mine, the literal Self's' would be more fitting, not 'I am the agent, the action is mine'. As for those who say 'the Self does action through its own memory, desire and effort, the causes of action', no: these are preceded by false notions. Memory, desire, effort and the rest are preceded by impressions produced by experiencing the fruits of actions, desired and undesired, caused by false notions. As in this birth merit and demerit, and the experience of their fruits, are produced by identification with the aggregate of body and the rest, by attachment, aversion and the like, so in the previous birth and the one before; thus saṃsāra, made by ignorance, without beginning, past and future, is to be inferred. And so it is established that through steadfastness in knowledge together with the renunciation of all action there is the absolute cessation of saṃsāra. And since identification with the body is of the nature of ignorance, when it ceases there can be no body and so no saṃsāra. Identification of the Self with the aggregate of body and the rest is of the nature of ignorance; for no one in the world who knows 'I am other than cattle and the like, and cattle and the like are other than me' thinks of them as 'I'. It is one who does not know who, from lack of discrimination, like taking a post for a man, forms the notion of 'I' in the aggregate of body and the rest — not one who knows by discrimination. The notion of 'I' in a son, as in 'you are myself, named son' (Taittirīya Saṃhitā 2.11), is figurative, based on the relation of begetter and begotten; and by a figurative self no real purpose, such as eating, can be served, just as a figurative lion or fire cannot do the work of a real one.

If it is said that, on the authority of injunctions concerning unseen matters, what the Self must do is done by the figurative selves, body and senses, no: they are made the Self by ignorance. Body, senses and the rest are not figurative selves; rather, though not the Self, they are made into the Self by false notion alone, since they are taken as the Self when it is present and not when it is absent. For in the undiscriminating, in children, at the time of ignorance, the notion of 'I' in the aggregate of body and the rest is seen — 'I am tall', 'I am fair'; but in the discriminating, who know 'I am other than the aggregate of body and the rest', the notion of 'I' in the aggregate does not arise at that time. So, since it is absent when the false notion is absent, it is produced by that, and is not figurative. A figurative notion or use of words is possible between things whose distinctions and common features are grasped separately, such as a lion and Devadatta or fire and a boy; not between things whose distinctions and common features are not grasped. As for 'on the authority of śruti', no: its authority concerns the unseen. Śruti is authoritative in matters not perceived by perception and the other means, such as the relation of means and end in the agnihotra and the like; not in matters of perception and the like, since a means of knowledge serves to show what is not seen. So the notion of 'I' in the aggregate of body and the rest, seen to be caused by false knowledge, cannot be imagined to be figurative. Not even a hundred śrutis declaring 'fire is cold' or 'fire is dark' would become authoritative. If a śruti did say 'fire is cold' or 'dark', some other intended meaning would have to be imagined, since its authority could not otherwise stand; but nothing contradicting another means of knowledge or its own words is to be imagined.

If it is said that since the agent of action has false notions, then in the absence of an agent the śruti would lose its authority, no: it has meaning in the knowledge of Brahman. If it is said that, like the śruti enjoining action, the śruti enjoining the knowledge of Brahman too would lose its authority, no: no sublating cognition is possible. Just as, when the Self is known through the śruti about the knowledge of Brahman, the notion of 'I' in the aggregate of body and the rest is sublated, the knowledge of the Self as the Self can never be sublated by anyone in any way, since the knowledge is not separate from its fruit — as 'fire is hot and luminous'. Nor does the śruti enjoining action thereby lose its authority, since by checking each earlier activity and producing each later new activity it serves to produce activity turned towards the inmost Self. Even if the means is false, it is true by the truth of its goal, as with arthavādas that are supplementary to injunctions; and in the world, telling children or madmen that their topknot will grow, in order to make them drink milk. And in its own sphere its authority is established directly, like the authority of perception and the rest, which rests on identification with the body, before knowledge of the Self.

yattu manyase svayamavyāpriyamāṇo'pi ātmā sannidhimātreṇa karoti, tadeva mukhyaṃ kartṛtvamātmanaḥ yathā rājā yudhyamāneṣu yodheṣu yudhyata iti prasiddhaṃ svayamayudhya – māno'pi sannidhānādeva jitaḥ parājitaśceti, tathā senāpatiḥ vācaiva karoti, kriyāphalasambandhaśca rājñaḥ senāpateśca dṛṣṭaḥ. yathā ca ṛtvikkarma yajamānasya, tathā dehādīnāṃ karma ātmakṛtaṃ syāt, phalasya ātmagāmitvāt, yathā vā bhrāmakasya lohabhrāmayitṛtvāt, avyāpṛtasyaiva mukhyameva kartṛtvaṃ, tathā cātmāna iti. tadasat akurvataḥ kārakatva prasaṅgāt. kārakamaneka prakāramiti cet – na, rājaprabhṛtīnāṃ mukhyasyāpi kartṛtvasya darśanāt. rājā tāvat svavyāpāreṇāpi yudhyate yodhānāṃ ca yodhayitṛtve dhanādāne ca mukhya kartṛtvaṃ tathā jayaparājaya phalopabhoge, yajamānasyāpi pradhānatyāge dakṣiṇādāne ca mukhyameva kartṛtvaṃ. tasmāt avyāpṛtasya kartṛtvopacāro yaḥ, saḥ gauṇaḥ iti avagamyate. yadi mukhyaṃ anyata kartṛtvaṃ svavyāpāralakṣaṇaṃ nopalabhyeta rājayajamānaprabhṛtīnāṃ, tadā sannidhi mātreṇāpi kartṛtvaṃ mukhyaṃ parikalpyeta, yathā bhrāmakasya lohabhramaṇena, na tathā rājayajamānādīnāṃ svavyāpāro nopalabhyate, tasmātsannidhimātreṇa kartṛtvaṃ gauṇameva tathā ca sati tatphalasambandhopi gauṇa eva syāt, na gauṇena mukhyaṃ kāryaṃ nirvartyate, tasmātsannidhimātreṇa kartṛtvaṃ gauṇameva. tathā ca sati tatphalasambandho'pi gauṇa eva syāt, na gauṇena mukhyaṃ kāryaṃ nirvartṛte, tasmādasadeva etat gīyate “dehādīnāṃ vyāpāreṇa avyāpṛta evātmā kartā bhoktā ca syāt” iti. bhrāntinimittaṃ tu sarvamupapadyate, yathā svapne, māyāyāṃ ca evaṃ, na ca dehādyātmapratyayabhrānti santānavicchedeṣu suṣuptisamādhyādiṣu kartṛtvabhoktṛtvādyanarthaḥ upalabhyate. tasmāt bhrāntipratyaya nimittaevāyaṃ saṃsārabhramaḥ, na tu paramārthataḥ iti samyagdarśanāt atyantoparama iti siddham.

You may think: though not itself active, the Self acts by its mere presence, and this is its literal agency — as a king is well known to fight when his soldiers fight, and though not fighting himself, is said to be victorious or defeated by his mere presence; likewise a general acts by speech alone; and connection with the action's fruit is seen for king and general; and as the priests' action belongs to the sacrificer, so the action of the body and the rest would be done by the Self, since the fruit goes to the Self; or as a magnet is literally the agent while itself inactive, by moving iron, so is the Self. That is wrong, for then what does nothing would be a factor of action. If it is said that factors are of many kinds, no: kings and the like are seen to have literal agency too. The king fights by his own activity as well, and in making the soldiers fight and in giving wealth he is literally the agent, and likewise in enjoying the fruit of victory or defeat; the sacrificer too is literally the agent in the chief offering and in giving fees. So the attribution of agency to one not active is understood to be figurative. If no literal agency of their own, consisting in their own activity, were found elsewhere for kings, sacrificers and the like, literal agency by mere presence might be imagined, as with the magnet moving iron; but the own activity of kings, sacrificers and the like is found; therefore agency by mere presence is figurative, and so the connection with its fruit too would be figurative; a literal effect is not produced by what is figurative. Therefore this song is false: 'through the activity of the body and the rest, the Self, though inactive, is agent and enjoyer'. But everything is explained as caused by error, as in dream and magic. And in deep sleep, samādhi and the like, when the stream of erroneous notions of the body and the rest as the Self is interrupted, no harm such as agency and enjoyership is found. Therefore this delusion of saṃsāra is caused by erroneous notions alone and is not real in truth; so it is established that from right vision comes its absolute cessation.

śiṣyasya śāstrārthagrahaṇāgrahaṇavivekabubhutsayā pṛcchati. tadgrahaṇe jñāte punaḥ grāhayiṣyāmi upāyāntareṇa api iti praṣṭuḥ abhiprāyaḥ yatnāntaraṃ ca āsthāya śiṣyasya kṛtārthatā kartavyā iti ācāryadharmaḥ pradarśitaḥ bhavati.

He asks, wishing to know whether the disciple has grasped the meaning of the teaching or not. The intention of the questioner is: if I learn that he has not grasped it, I will make him grasp it again, even by another means. This shows the teacher's duty: to make the disciple fulfilled, undertaking further effort.

kaccit iti. kaccit kiṃ etat mayā uktaṃ śrutaṃ śravaṇena avadhāritaṃ pārtha! tvayā ekāgreṇa cetasā cittena? kiṃ vā apramādataḥ? kaccit ajñānasammohaḥ ajñānanimittaḥ sammohaḥ aviviktabhāvaḥ (vicittabhāvaḥ) avivekaḥ svābhāvikaḥ kiṃ pranaṣṭaḥ? yadarthaḥ ayaṃ śāstraśravaṇāyāsaḥ tava, mama ca upadeṣṭṛtvāyāsaḥ pravṛttaḥ – te tava he dhanañjaya.

'Has this been heard by you, Pārtha, with one-pointed mind?' Has what I have told been heard, grasped by hearing, by you, Pārtha, with one-pointed mind, with attention, or not? And has the delusion of ignorance — the delusion caused by ignorance, lack of discrimination, natural non-discrimination — been destroyed in you, O Dhanañjaya? For that is the purpose for which your effort of hearing the teaching, and my effort of teaching, were undertaken.

|| 73 ||

word by word · translation

naṣṭaḥ destroyed mohaḥ is delusion smṛtiḥ memory labdhā has been gained tvat-prasādāt by your grace mayā by me acyuta O Acyuta sthitaḥ firm asmi I stand gata-sandehaḥ my doubt gone kariṣye I will do vacanam word tava your

Arjuna said: My delusion is destroyed, and by your grace I have regained my memory, Acyuta. I stand firm, my doubts gone. I will do as you say.

bhāṣya

naṣṭaḥ iti. naṣṭaḥ mohaḥ ajñānajaḥ samastasaṃsārānarthahetuḥ, sāgaraḥ iva dustaraḥ. smṛtiḥ ca ātmatattvaviṣayā labdhā – yasyāḥ lābhāt sarvahṛdayagranthīnāṃ vipramokṣaḥ tvatprasādāt tava prasādāt mayā tvatprasādaṃ āśritena acyuta. aneka mohanāśapraśna prativacanena sarvaśāstrārthajñānaphalaṃ etāvat eva iti niścitaṃ darśitaṃ bhavati, yataḥ jñānāt mohanāśaḥ ātmasmṛtilābhaḥ ca iti. tathā ca śrutau “anātmavit śocāmi” iti upanyasya ātmajñānena sarvagranthīnāṃ vipramokṣaḥ uktaḥ (chāṃ.u.7.1.3.26.2) “bhidyate hṛdayagranthi” (muṃ.u.2.2.8) “tatra ko mohaḥ kaḥ śokaḥ ekatvamanupaśyataḥ” (ī.u.7) iti ca mantravarṇaḥ. atha idānīṃ tvacchāsane sthitaḥ asmi gatasandehaḥ muktasaṃśayaḥ kariṣye vacanaṃ tava. ahaṃ tvatprasādāt kṛtārthaḥ, na me kartavyaṃ asti ityabhiprāyaḥ.

'Delusion is destroyed…' Destroyed is delusion born of ignorance, the cause of all the misfortune of saṃsāra, hard to cross like the ocean. And memory concerning the reality of the Self has been gained — by gaining which all the knots of the heart are loosened — by your grace, through your grace, by me who have resorted to your grace, O Acyuta. By the answer to the question about the destruction of delusion it is shown as settled that this alone is the fruit of knowing the meaning of the whole teaching: from knowledge come the destruction of delusion and the gaining of the memory of the Self. So too in the śruti, having set forth 'not knowing the Self, I grieve', the loosening of all knots through knowledge of the Self is told (Chāndogya 7.1.3, 7.26.2); and the mantras 'the knot of the heart is cut' (Muṇḍaka 2.2.8) and 'what delusion, what grief, for one who sees oneness?' (Īśa 7). Now I stand firm in your command, my doubt gone, freed from doubt; I will do your word. By your grace I have attained my end; nothing remains for me to do — that is the purport.

Sañjaya said: Thus I heard this wondrous dialogue between Vāsudeva and the great-souled Pārtha, which makes my hair stand on end.

The meaning of the teaching is complete. Now, to show the connection with the story, Sañjaya [said]:

ityahaṃ iti. iti evaṃ ahaṃ vāsudevasya pārthasya ca mahātmanaḥ saṃvādaṃ imaṃ yathoktaṃ aśrauṣaṃ śrutavān asmi adbhutaṃ atyantavismayakaraṃ romaharṣaṇaṃ romāñcakaram. taṃ ca imam.

'Thus I have heard…' Thus I have heard this dialogue, as described, of Vāsudeva and the great-souled Pārtha, wondrous, exceedingly astonishing, making the hair stand on end. And this —

By the grace of Vyāsa I heard this supreme secret, this yoga, from Kṛṣṇa, the lord of yoga, himself declaring it directly.

bhāṣya

vyāsa iti. vyāsaprasādāt tataḥ divyacakṣurlābhāt śrutavān imaṃ saṃvādaṃ guhyatamaṃ paraṃ yogaṃ – yogārthatvāt grantho'pi yogaḥ – saṃvādaṃ imaṃ yogam eva vā yogeśvarāt kṛṣṇāt sākṣāt kathayataḥ svayaṃ, na paramparayā.

'By the grace of Vyāsa…' By the grace of Vyāsa — through gaining the divine eye from him — I have heard this supreme, most secret yoga — the text too is yoga, since it is for yoga; this dialogue, or yoga itself — from Kṛṣṇa, the Lord of yoga, himself telling it directly, not through a succession.

'O king, recalling again and again…' O king Dhṛtarāṣṭra, recalling again and again, every moment, this wondrous, sacred dialogue of Keśava and Arjuna — which removes sin even by being heard — having heard it, I rejoice again and again, every moment.

yatra where yoga-īśvaraḥ the Lord of yoga kṛṣṇaḥ Kṛṣṇa yatra where pārthaḥ Pārtha dhanuḥ-dharaḥ the bearer of the bow tatra there śrīḥ fortune vijayaḥ victory bhūtiḥ prosperity dhruvā steady nītiḥ policy matiḥ conviction mama my

Wherever there is Kṛṣṇa, the lord of yoga, and wherever there is Pārtha, the archer, there, I believe, are fortune, victory, prosperity and sound policy.

bhāṣya

yatra iti. yatra yasmin pakṣe yogeśvaraḥ sarvayogānāṃ īśvaraḥ tatprabhavatvāt sarvayogabījasya – kṛṣṇaḥ, yatra pārthaḥ yasmin pakṣe dhanurtharaḥ gāṇḍivadhanvā tatra śrīḥ tasmin pāṇḍavānāṃ pakṣe śrīḥ vijayaḥ, tatraiva bhūtiḥ śriyaḥ viśeṣaḥ vistāraḥ bhūtiḥ dhruvā avyabhicāriṇī nītiḥ nayaḥ, ityevaṃ matiḥ mama iti.

'Where Kṛṣṇa, the Lord of yoga, is…' Where, on whichever side, is Kṛṣṇa, the Lord of yoga — Lord of all yogas, since the seed of all yoga springs from him — where, on whichever side, is Pārtha, the bearer of the bow, wielding the Gāṇḍīva — there is fortune: on that side of the Pāṇḍavas are fortune and victory; there too is prosperity — prosperity is the special expansion of fortune — and steady, unfailing, policy, statecraft: such is my conviction.

iti śrīmahābhārate śatasāhasryāṃ saṃhitāyāṃ vaiyāsikyāṃ bhīṣmaparvaṇi yogaśāstre śrī kṛṣṇārjuna saṃvāde mokṣa sannyāsayogo nāma aṣṭādaśo'dhyāyaḥ.

Thus, in the Bhagavad Gītā — the Upaniṣad, the knowledge of Brahman, the scripture of yoga, the dialogue of Śrī Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna — within the Bhīṣma Parva of the Mahābhārata, the collection of a hundred thousand verses by Vyāsa, ends the eighteenth chapter, Mokṣasannyāsa Yoga.

iti śrīmatparamahaṃsaparivrājakācāryagovindabhagavatpūjyapādaśiṣya śrīmacchaṅkarabhagavataḥ kṛtau śrīmadbhagavadgītābhāṣye mokṣasannyāsayogo nāma aṣṭādaśo'dhyāyaḥ.

Thus ends the eighteenth chapter, Mokṣasannyāsa Yoga, of the commentary on the Bhagavad Gītā composed by Śrī Śaṅkara Bhagavat, wandering ascetic of the highest order and disciple of Śrī Govinda Bhagavatpāda.
